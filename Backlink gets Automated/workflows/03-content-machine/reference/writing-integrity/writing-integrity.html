Writing Integrity — the honest + distinctive writing contract

Ten rules for making claims that are truthful, well-sourced, and distinctive when drafting an article. Not the SEO/AEO/GEO structure checklist — this one is about honesty, positioning, sourcing, and standing out. Read by /write before drafting, and checked again before shipping.

01The one idea

A reader should finish the article trusting it. That happens only when every claim is honest about what the product is, backed by a source we can stand behind, and says at least one thing a generic vendor never would.

🎯

Honest

Claim the product only where it genuinely wins. Say plainly where it doesn't fit. No guaranteed outcomes, no hype words with nothing behind them.

🔎

Well-sourced

Every strong claim carries a real, cited number, or it gets cut. A citation is evidence for one fact, never an endorsement. Never a competitor.

✋

Distinctive

Carry a real point of view or a concrete workflow. Hypothetical examples are fine; fabricated real customers, quotes, and first-party metrics are a hard ban.

The one rule that governs everything

If you can't stand behind it, don't write it. Every claim traces to something real: a cited source, a genuine product capability, or a clearly-labelled hypothetical. Anything you'd have to invent — a customer, a quote, a metric, a superlative with no number — is cut, not softened.

What this is NOT

This is not the SEO/AEO/GEO checklist (that governs structure and format, and lives separately in reference/seo-aeo-geo-guidelines/). Integrity is about what you claim and how you back it — not headings, schema, or answer-first layout.

02The words you need first

A few terms carry the whole contract. Each is defined once here, then used exactly.

First-party metric — a number about our own company or product: a win rate, customer count, NPS, or a time-to-hire delta. Usable only if it is actually supplied for this run. Otherwise it counts as fabricated.
Hypothetical (illustrative) scenario — a clearly-invented example tied to a genuine capability: "a 500-person SaaS hiring 20 engineers a quarter could…". Allowed and encouraged. This is the opposite of a fabricated real customer.
External citation — a link or reference to an outside source, placed as evidence for one specific stat. Not a recommendation, not a shout-out.
Superlative / hype word — an unprovable big claim with no number behind it: "ultimate", "game-changing", "revolutionary", "seamless", "world-class". Banned unless a real cited figure backs it.
Competitor — a direct rival, listed in 02-asset-engine/competitor-study/output/competitors.md. Never a source, never named as one. Critique their approach generically only.
nofollow — a link attribute telling search engines not to pass ranking credit. It's how a citation stays evidence rather than a traffic gift to another brand.
Company byline — an article published under the company's name, not a person's. It uses collective markers ("we", "our team") and never a fabricated personal story ("I once worked with a team that…").

04Positioning honesty Rules 1 · 2 · 3

The product wins in a real, specific slice. Name that slice, admit the rest, and never promise an outcome you can't guarantee. Claiming one slice well beats claiming the whole stack vaguely.

Rule 1 — claim only what the product actually is

  • When the topic is broad, write the broad context honestly, then locate the product at its real slice.
  • The product is positioned only where it genuinely wins — nowhere else.

This is the one company-specific slot in the whole contract. Everything else is universal; these two lines get filled per company from its features.md / product pages:

PRODUCT_IS
The real slice the product owns. Filled per company. Position claims here and only here.
PRODUCT_IS_NOT
The adjacent territory it does NOT own — never imply it does. Filled per company.

Rule 2 — the honesty tell: say when we're NOT the right fit

  • State plainly where the product isn't the answer (a tiny team, a niche need).
  • It builds trust, and it's exactly what the best comparison content already does. Don't pretend to fit everyone.

Rule 3 — don't overpromise; acknowledge limitations

  • No guaranteed outcomes. No "this will fix hiring."
  • Acknowledge trade-offs and limits out loud. Transparent beats salesy, every time.
Overclaim ✕

"Our platform is the complete end-to-end hiring solution that guarantees you'll hire faster — the ultimate stack for every team."

→
Honest slice ✓

"Structured skills assessments are where this shines. For a five-person team hiring twice a year, a full assessment platform is probably overkill — a good ATS may be enough."

Illustrative example. Note the honest slice does three things at once: names the real strength (Rule 1), admits a non-fit (Rule 2), and drops the guarantee and the "ultimate" (Rules 3 + 4).

05No hype, no fabrication Rules 4 · 5

Two failures kill trust fastest: a big claim with nothing behind it, and an invented "fact" about a real customer. Both are banned outright.

Rule 4 — no empty hype or unprovable superlatives

  • Never claim big and prove nothing. Every strong claim carries a real, cited number, or it's cut.
  • Banned with no number behind them: "the ultimate", "game-changing", "revolutionary", "seamless", "world-class".
  • No buzzword soup: "synergistic", "holistically", "paradigm".

Rule 5 — differentiation: say something only we'd say

  • The piece must carry a real point of view, a non-obvious trade-off, or a concrete workflow — not generic vendor filler.
  • Clearly-hypothetical scenarios are allowed ("a 500-person SaaS hiring 20 engineers a quarter could…") when tied to a genuine product capability.
Hard ban — never, under any framing
  • No named or implied real customer.
  • No fake quote.
  • No fabricated first-party metric — win rate, time-to-hire delta, NPS, customer count.
  • Use real first-party data only if it is actually supplied for this run.
✕

What NOT to do

Each of these is a hard-ban violation
  • "One of our customers cut time-to-hire by 60%" — unless you have that customer's written consent and verified data.
  • "Our platform boasts a 94% adoption rate" — unless the run's supplied data actually says so.
  • "I once worked with a team that…" in a company byline — bylines use collective markers only, never a fabricated personal story.
✓

What to do instead

Same point, honestly made
  • "A 200-person company running quarterly hiring sprints could cut screening rounds using structured assessments" — a labelled hypothetical tied to a real capability.
  • Cite a real, external, verifiable stat for the adoption/impact claim instead of inventing a first-party one.
  • Speak as the company: "In our experience, teams that…" — collective, not a made-up individual.

06The numbers must reconcile Rules 6 · 10

Stats are the load-bearing part of an authoritative piece — so they get two guards: they must all be true together, and each must earn its place.

Rule 6 — self-consistency (PASS / FAIL before shipping)

  • List every number used and confirm none contradict each other. An adoption %, an ROI figure, and a time-to-hire must all be possible at the same time.
  • This is a hard PASS/FAIL gate. On a conflict, re-verify the stat or drop it — never ship an unresolved contradiction.
Claim in the draftNumberReconciles?
"90% of teams were fully onboarded in week one"90% / 1 wkFAIL
"…average onboarding takes six weeks"6 wkscontradicts above
Verdict: the two cannot both be true — fix before shipping. Re-verify which figure is real, or drop one. (Illustrative.)

Rule 10 — no stat quota

  • Don't force a fixed number of statistics to sound authoritative, and don't stuff.
  • Use a stat only where it genuinely supports the point. A few load-bearing verified stats beat many impressive-sounding ones.
  • Natural over volume.

07Sourcing — cite, don't promote Rules 7 · 8 · 9

You may draft from any source. But when a citation lands on the page, it's evidence for one fact — chosen for credibility, never a competitor, and never a plug for someone else's brand.

RuleWhat it requires
7 · Never cite a competitor
  • Never cite or link a direct competitor as a source. The list lives in 02-asset-engine/competitor-study/output/competitors.md.
  • Also auto-enforced upstream by the research-structure filter — but check anyway.
  • If the only source for a stat is a competitor, drop the stat or use a neutral primary source.
  • Critique competitor approaches generically, never by name.
8 · Cite with credibility
  • When placing an external citation, prefer the most credible and most recent source available.
  • The model judges credibility — there is no fixed whitelist.
9 · Cite, don't promote
  • A citation is evidence for one stat, not an endorsement. Never write "X has a great guide" or drive traffic to another brand's content.
  • State the fact, cite the source (neutral, nofollow), move on.
  • Volume limits: ≤2 citations per external domain; aim for 3+ distinct domains.
Promotion ✕

"Acme HR published a fantastic guide on onboarding that everyone should read — see their full breakdown here."

→
Evidence ✓

"Onboarding that runs past six weeks correlates with higher first-year attrition.[cited, neutral, nofollow]" — the fact, one source, no shout-out.

Illustrative. The right-hand version states the fact, attaches one neutral source, and passes no ranking credit or praise to the other brand.
