How is cost per hire formally defined?

  • Cost per hire (CPH) is a standardized recruiting metric that captures the average amount an organization spends to fill one open position, combining both the money paid to outside vendors and the internal labor and overhead consumed by the hiring process[1].2shrm.org
  • The formal definition, set by the SHRM/ANSI 06001.2012 standard, states CPH as "the mean average of total costs divided by the number of hires"[1].2shrm.org
  • LinkedIn's recruiting metrics guide describes it in similar terms: total spend associated with filling an open position (recruiter fees, advertisements, etc.) divided by the number of hires[1].2shrm.org
  • In practice, this is a straightforward average — how much money it takes, on average, to hire one person[1].2shrm.org

How the split is defined: internal vs. external (hard vs. soft) costs

  • The standard explicitly delineates internal versus external cost components[1].2shrm.org
  • External costs are payments made to outside vendors or individuals during recruiting — job advertising and job board fees, third-party recruiting/agency fees, background checks, referral rewards, and recruiting technology or ATS subscription costs[3].7costperform.com
  • Internal costs include recruiting staff salaries and benefits (fully loaded compensation for in-house recruiters, talent acquisition team members, and coordinators, including prorated time from HR staff or hiring managers spent interviewing or recruiting), and recruiting department overhead such as a share of office rent, utilities, and equipment allocated to recruiting activities[1].2shrm.org
  • Hiring managers' and interviewers' time spent recruiting is treated as an optional inclusion representing paid labor, and organizations are advised to decide whether to count it and then stay consistent[3].7costperform.com
  • External costs are third-party, invoiceable expenses — dollars that leave the organization — and are described as easier to calculate because vendors bill them directly[10][11].59hiretruffle.comshrm.org
  • Internal costs are the expenses generated inside the organization to make a hire happen, and they are largely time-based rather than invoice-based, which is precisely why they are the most commonly undercounted[6][11].916shrm.orgintegralrecruiting.com
  • HR practitioners further split these components into two practical buckets: **hard costs** are tangible, easily trackable expenses such as job board fees and agency invoices, while **soft costs** are intangible expenses such as the time an internal manager spends on the process[24].18juicebox.ai

The rules for counting hires and costs correctly

  • The denominator counts employees who accepted offers **and started** during the measurement period — not offers extended[23][11].910shrm.orgcorrectcontext.com
  • match the cost period to the hire period (don't compare annual costs against monthly or quarterly hires)our own page
  • count whole hires rather than fractional full-time-equivalents[6].16integralrecruiting.com
  • Counting offers instead of starts artificially deflates the metric, and this matters because a declined offer represents 100% of the cost with 0% of the returnour own page

Recruiting Cost Ratio: an alternative metric

  • A related variant is the Recruiting Cost Ratio (RCR), which varies from the CPHI or CPHC formulas only in its denominator: instead of dividing by number of hires, the RCR divides total recruiting costs by the total first-year annual compensation of the new hires[9].8a.storyblok.com
  • This produces a ratio (cost as a percentage of first-year pay) rather than a flat dollar figure per hire.our own page
