## What are white-collar jobs?
White-collar jobs are roles that center on knowledge, administration, or management, usually done in an office, remotely, or in a hybrid setup. The work is mostly mental rather than physical: analyzing data, advising clients, writing code, or running teams. The term goes back to the 1930s, when office staff wore white dress shirts. A white-collar worker is typically paid a salary.
### Examples of white-collar jobs
- Accountants, financial analysts, and auditors
- Software developers, data analysts, and IT staff
- Lawyers, consultants, and marketers
- HR managers, recruiters, and operations leads
- Doctors, teachers, and other licensed professionals
## What are blue-collar jobs?
Blue-collar jobs are roles built around manual or technical work, done on a site, in a factory, in the field, or in a workshop. The work is hands-on and skill-heavy: building, fixing, installing, operating equipment, or producing goods. The name comes from the durable blue uniforms that held up to physical labor. A blue-collar worker is more often paid by the hour, with overtime.
### Examples of blue-collar jobs
- Electricians, plumbers, and HVAC technicians
- Welders, machinists, and CNC operators
- Construction workers, carpenters, and roofers
- Truck drivers, warehouse staff, and forklift operators
- Mechanics, line workers, and maintenance crews
## White collar vs blue collar jobs: what is the difference?
The core difference is the type of work: white-collar roles lean on knowledge and judgment, while blue-collar roles lean on physical and technical skill. That one split ripples into how people train for the job, how they get paid, how overtime law treats them, and even how exposed the role is to automation. Here is how the two stack up.

One distinction trips up new managers: overtime. In the U.S., many salaried white-collar roles are exempt from overtime, while most hourly blue-collar roles are non-exempt and must be paid extra past 40 hours a week. The rules sit in the federal overtime law, and getting them wrong is a common payroll mistake.
## How much do white-collar vs blue-collar workers earn?
On average, white-collar work still pays more, but the gap is narrower than most people think, and it flips for skilled trades. Management occupations had a median annual wage of $122,090 in May 2024, far above the $49,500 median for all U.S. jobs, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Construction and extraction work had a $58,360 median over the same period, per BLS wage data.
Averages hide the interesting part. A licensed electrician, elevator installer, or crane operator can out-earn plenty of entry-level office staff, because pay tracks scarcity and skill, not the collar. As skilled-trade shortages grow, that pull only gets stronger. For hiring teams, the takeaway is simple: do not assume a blue-collar req is the cheap one, and do not assume a degree justifies a bigger salary band. Postings that still price trade roles like 2015 entry-level jobs lose skilled candidates to competitors that priced the role correctly.
## Are blue-collar jobs more resistant to AI and automation than white-collar work?
Early evidence says yes, directionally. Automation and generative AI tend to replace routine, computer-based tasks faster than physical, on-site work that needs judgment and dexterity. Blue-collar trades are not automation-proof, but demand for skilled trade labor keeps climbing even as AI reshapes many white-collar workflows.
The clearest signal is hiring, not headlines. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics projects the economy will add roughly 81,000 electrician openings a year through 2034, a 9 percent growth rate, well above the average for all occupations, according to BLS employment projections. If routine cognitive work is the first target for AI tools, on-site trade work that depends on physical presence and hands-on judgment is proving harder to automate away, at least so far.
That does not make either collar safe by default. AI is already reshaping white-collar workflows like drafting, coding, and first-pass analysis, and blue-collar sites are adopting more automation of their own. The safer bet for hiring teams is the one that runs through this whole comparison: test for the specific skills a role needs today, and revisit that list as the work itself changes.
## Is white-collar or blue-collar work better?
Neither is better as a category, and treating one as the smart choice is how good candidates get overlooked. White-collar paths can offer flexibility, remote options, and clear promotion ladders. Blue-collar paths can offer faster entry, lower student debt, strong pay in high-demand trades, and work that is hard to offshore or automate. The honest answer is that fit depends on the person and the role, not the label.
The labels also carry baggage. Calling one type of work “higher” than another is a bias that shrinks your talent pool and bruises your employer brand. The teams we see hire best treat both as skilled work that simply needs different proof.
## Other job collars: grey, pink, and new-collar
The two-box model is showing its age. Plenty of modern roles do not fit neatly in either bucket, which is why newer labels keep appearing:
- Grey-collar: hands-on work that also needs technical knowledge, like medical technicians, aircraft mechanics, and HVAC specialists. See our guide to grey-collar jobs.
- Pink-collar: service and care roles such as nursing, teaching, and support work. Our pink-collar jobs breakdown has examples.
- New-collar: tech roles, like cloud support or cybersecurity, that reward certifications and demonstrated skill over a four-year degree.
Each new label makes the same point: the collar is a rough proxy. What actually predicts performance is the specific mix of skills a role needs, and those cut across every color.
## How to hire white-collar and blue-collar roles well
Start with the role, not the resume. For an office hire you might weigh cognitive ability, communication, and software fluency; for a trade hire you might weigh technical skill, safety judgment, and reliability. Both can be measured before an interview, and measuring beats guessing from a degree or a job title.

# Screening In vs Screening Out: Which to Use and When
Screening in vs. screening out: Learn the differences and discover which approach is best for your hiring strategy to find the right candidates.

Most hiring teams think their candidate screening catches the best people. The data says it quietly does the opposite. In a study of 8,720 workers and 2,275 executives, Harvard Business School and Accenture found that 88% of employers admit qualified, high-skilled candidates get rejected because they do not match a job description word for word.
That is the hidden cost of how you filter applicants. Every knockout rule you set to save time also throws away people who could have done the job well, and you never see the ones you lost. The short version: screening in looks for reasons to advance a candidate, screening out looks for reasons to reject one, and most teams should screen in by default and screen out only on genuine non-negotiables.

## TL;DR
- Screening in evaluates candidates for what they can do and actively looks for reasons to move them forward.
- Screening out eliminates candidates against fixed criteria and looks for reasons to reject them.

## What is screening in?
Screening in is the process of evaluating candidates to find the best match for a role by focusing on their skills, potential, and evidence of what they can do. It is an inclusive approach: instead of asking what a candidate lacks on paper, it asks what they can contribute, even when their background is not a perfect fit.
This matters because resumes are weak evidence. As Abhishek Shah, founder of Testlify, puts it: “It is easy to bend the truth on resumes, and many jobseekers do exactly that.” Screening in leans on signals that are harder to fake, like a work sample or a structured skills test, so you judge ability rather than how well someone writes about themselves.
It pays off where talent is scarce. McKinsey reports that hiring for skills is five times more predictive of job performance than hiring for education, and more than twice as predictive as hiring for experience. Widening the lens past credentials is not charity, it is a better bet on who will actually perform.
## What is screening out?
Screening out is the process of removing candidates from consideration based on specific criteria they fail to meet. It narrows a large applicant pool quickly by filtering on fixed requirements, such as a certification, a years-of-experience threshold, or a location. The goal is speed: cut the list down so recruiters spend time only on people who clear the bar.
Used well, it is useful triage. When a role has a genuine non-negotiable, a pharmacy license, a CDL, a security clearance, screening out on that one rule is the right call and saves everyone time. The trouble starts when teams screen out on proxies (a degree, a six-month employment gap, an exact job title) that have little to do with whether someone can do the work.
## How do screening in and screening out differ?
Both are screening methods, but they run on opposite instincts. Screening in starts from “what can this person do for us?” and treats the application as a starting point. Screening out starts from “what disqualifies this person?” and treats the application as a checklist. One widens the pool, the other narrows it. The table below maps where they split.

## Why does screening out backfire?
Screening out backfires because it optimizes for rejection, and rejection rules are blunt. A filter that drops anyone without a four-year degree also drops the self-taught developer who ships better code. At scale, those false rejections add up: Harvard estimates 27 million Americans are “hidden workers,” capable people screened out before a human ever reads their application.
Joseph Fuller, a professor at Harvard Business School, frames the mechanism plainly: “In filtering out all but those candidates who match exacting and expanding requirements, companies reject many experienced, reliable, and creative individuals.” The filter feels efficient while it is busy removing your best non-obvious hires.
There is a fairness cost too. When the job is to eliminate fast, reviewers lean on shortcuts, and shortcuts pick up bias around names, gaps, schools, and demographics. That narrows your pool exactly when the McKinsey skills data above says ability predicts performance better than pedigree, and 92% of talent professionals already tell LinkedIn that soft skills matter as much as or more than hard ones.
## When should you screen out?
Screen out when a requirement is genuinely non-negotiable and easy to verify. These are rules where failing them makes the rest of the evaluation moot. Keep the list short and honest, because every item on it shrinks your pool.
- Legal or licensing requirements: a role that legally needs a CPA, a nursing license, or a commercial driver’s license.
- Safety-critical rules: certifications or clearances where a miss carries real risk.
- Hard logistics: a time zone, work authorization, or on-site requirement the role truly cannot flex on.
- Very high volume: when a single posting draws hundreds of applicants, one or two knockout questions make the pile reviewable before you screen in.
Notice what is not on that list: a specific degree, an exact past job title, or a tidy employment history. Those are proxies, and proxies are where good candidates get lost. If a requirement is a nice-to-have rather than a deal-breaker, move it out of your screen-out and into your screen-in scoring instead.
## How do you build a screening-in process?
Run screening in as a structured sequence, not a gut read of resumes. The point is to gather evidence of ability early, so you spend human time on the people most likely to perform. Here is a process that scales without drowning your team.
- Define the real competencies. List the 4 to 6 skills the role actually needs, separate from credentials. Anchor each one to evidence you can measure, not a line on a resume.
- Screen out once, on deal-breakers only. Apply your short non-negotiable list first to make a high-volume pile reviewable. Everything past this point is screen-in.
- Send a skills assessment from your test library early. A focused 20 to 30 minute test on the core competency tells you more than another resume read. Send it within 24 hours so strong candidates do not cool off.
- Add a second signal. Pair the test with a structured video interview or a short work sample. Two independent signals pointing the same way beat one polished interview.
- Score against the competencies, then rank. Rate every candidate on the same rubric and review the top 10 to 15 by score. Ranking by demonstrated power skills surfaces people a resume filter would have buried.
Pro Tip: turn one of your old knockout filters into a scored signal for 30 days and compare. Teams that score a skill instead of requiring a credential routinely find their strongest finalist sitting just outside the old cutoff. The pattern is consistent enough that Harvard found firms hiring from overlooked pools were 36% less likely to report talent shortages.

## Frequently asked questions (FAQs)
What does screened in by HR mean?
Screened in means a candidate has passed the first evaluation stage and is being moved forward. HR has judged that they meet enough of the role criteria, often through a skills assessment or screening interview, to deserve further consideration such as a structured interview.
What are the two types of screening methods?
The two types are screening in and screening out. Screening in advances candidates by looking for evidence of what they can do, while screening out removes candidates by checking them against fixed criteria. Most strong hiring processes sequence both: a short screen-out on non-negotiables, then a screen-in on skills.
Is it better to screen in or screen out candidates?
For most roles, screening in is better because it widens your pool and judges ability rather than pedigree, and skills evidence predicts performance far better than credentials. Screening out is better only for genuine non-negotiables, like a required license, or for cutting a very high-volume pile down to a reviewable size first.
What does screening out candidates mean?
Screening out candidates means eliminating applicants from consideration based on specific criteria they fail to meet, such as a missing certification or an experience threshold. It is useful for true deal-breakers, but it backfires when the criteria are proxies that reject capable people who do not fit a template.
What is the purpose of screening?
The purpose of screening is to focus hiring time on the candidates most likely to perform. Done well, it combines a quick filter on real non-negotiables with an evidence-based evaluation of role-relevant skills, so the shortlist reflects what people can actually do rather than how their resume reads.

## What are aptitude assessment questions?
An aptitude assessment question is a standardized problem that measures a candidate’s ability to reason, not the facts they have memorized. It targets a specific mental skill (working with numbers, reading for meaning, spotting a pattern, picturing an object in space) and it has one defensible answer, so two reviewers grading the same response land in the same place. That last part matters more than it sounds: consistency is what makes the score comparable from candidate to candidate.
This is different from a knowledge test. A knowledge test asks whether someone has learned a tool or a topic; an aptitude question asks whether they can pick up the next one. ## What do aptitude tests predict about a hire?

## Which types of aptitude questions should you use?
Pick the families that map to how the job is actually done, then ignore the rest. An accounts role leans numerical; a support or content role leans verbal; a warehouse or field-engineering role leans spatial. Here is what each family measures, the kind of candidate it sorts, and where it fits.

### Numerical reasoning
Numerical questions test whether someone can make a sound call from numbers under time pressure, not whether they memorized formulas. The trap to watch is the candidate who rushes the arithmetic and misses the structure of the problem. A clean numerical item has data, a short time limit, and one correct read.
### Verbal reasoning
Verbal questions give a short passage and ask the candidate to draw only what the text supports. They reveal who reads carefully and who fills gaps with assumptions. For roles where a misread email becomes a churned customer, this family earns its slot.
### Logical and abstract reasoning
Logical and abstract items use number series, shapes, or coded sequences with no domain knowledge required, so they sort raw problem-solving ability from schooling. They are strong for technical, product, and data roles, and easy to over-weight, so cap this family at one section, not the whole test.
### Spatial reasoning
Spatial items ask the candidate to rotate, fold, or assemble an object in their head, with no verbal or numerical shortcut available. They predict performance in field engineering, design, and logistics roles where a misread layout costs real time or material, and they are the family most guides skip, which makes them easy to under-test for hands-on hiring.
### Situational judgment
Situational judgment items describe a realistic, role-specific scenario and ask the candidate to pick the best response among several plausible ones. They measure decision quality under the kind of ambiguity a resume never shows, which is why they earn a slot for management, frontline, and client-facing roles even though they are the hardest family to write well.
## What are 20 aptitude assessment questions to ask candidates?
Each question below pairs the prompt with what a strong answer tells you, so a hiring manager can score it without second-guessing. Vary the exact numbers per candidate to keep a fair test, and keep the reasoning, not the final digit, as what you grade.
### Numerical reasoning questions
- A product costs $80. You apply a 30% discount, then take 20% off the reduced price. What does the candidate pay? Answer: $44.80. The reveal: strong candidates apply the discounts in sequence (80 to 56 to 44.80), a 44% total cut. The trap is adding 30 and 20 to get a 50% discount.
- Five machines make five widgets in five minutes. How long do 100 machines need to make 100 widgets? Answer: five minutes. Each machine makes one widget in five minutes, so scaling both sides keeps the time constant. This sorts rate reasoning from reflexive multiplication.
- If the probability of an event is 0.2, what are the odds against it? Answer: 4 to 1, because 0.8 divided by 0.2 is 4. It checks whether a candidate can move between probability and odds.
- Revenue grew from $40,000 to $50,000. What is the percentage increase? Answer: 25%, since the $10,000 rise is one quarter of $40,000. Watch for candidates who divide by the new number instead of the original.
### Logical and abstract reasoning questions
- What number comes next: 2, 6, 12, 20, 30, ? Answer: 42. The gaps grow by 4, 6, 8, 10, then 12. Strong candidates find the rule behind the gaps, not just the gaps.
- If MONDAY is coded by shifting each letter forward one place to NPOEBZ, how is FRIDAY coded? Answer: GSJEBZ. This checks whether a candidate can apply a simple rule consistently across new input.
- The day after tomorrow is Wednesday. What day was three days before yesterday? Answer: Thursday. Today is Monday, yesterday was Sunday, and three days before Sunday is Thursday. It tests careful step-by-step deduction.
- All of a firm’s auditors are certified. Priya is certified. Can you conclude Priya is an auditor? Answer: no. Certification does not work backward to the job title. This catches candidates who confuse a rule with its reverse.
### Verbal and spatial reasoning questions
- Read a three-sentence policy passage, then ask which of four statements the passage actually supports. The reveal: who answers from the text versus who answers from what they assume the policy should say.
- Give a passage with a clear claim and a qualifier (“usually”, “except”), then ask a question that hinges on the qualifier. Careful readers catch it; fast skimmers miss it.
- A cube has a surface area of 96 square centimeters. How long is one edge? Answer: 4 centimeters, because each of the six faces is 16 square centimeters and the square root of 16 is 4.
- Show a flat net of a folded box and ask which 3D shape it forms. This sorts candidates who can picture an object in motion, which matters for trades, logistics, and design work.
### Situational judgment questions
- A top performer on the numerical test was curt with your receptionist on the way in. How much should that weigh, and what would you check next? Strong answers separate one data point from a pattern.
- A candidate finishes the timed test with half the time to spare but makes two avoidable errors. Speed or accuracy: which do you probe in the interview, and why? This reveals how a manager trades off the two.
- Two finalists score within one point of each other. What second signal do you add to break the tie? Good answers reach for a work sample, not a coin toss.
- An applicant requests extra time for a documented disability. What is the fair adjustment that keeps the test valid for everyone? This checks judgment on accessibility and consistency at once.

## How do you keep aptitude testing fair?
Run an adverse-impact check, and run it before a score ever gates a candidate. The U.S. federal standard is the four-fifths rule from the EEOC Uniform Guidelines: if the selection rate for any race, sex, or ethnic group falls below 80% of the rate for the highest-scoring group, enforcement agencies generally treat that as evidence of adverse impact. So if 50% of one group clears your aptitude band but only 30% of another does, the second rate is 60% of the first, well under the 80% line, and the test needs a second look.
Three habits keep aptitude testing defensible. Test only the competencies the job requires, so the content stays job-related. Track selection rates by group and apply the four-fifths check each cycle. And offer documented accommodations without weakening the standard for everyone else. Fairness here is not a compliance nicety; a test that screens out capable people is also a test that hands your competitors the candidates you passed on.

This assessment is typically conducted online with parts evaluating work orientation, personal attributes, and interpersonal skills.
Through scenario-based role-specific tests, recruiters assess and benchmark candidates globally.
### Interview rounds
Typically, one or two brief rounds for entry-level positions. First, there is a screening, followed by a meeting with the hiring manager.
### Offer & onboarding
Following background and internal reviews, orientation commences. However, it may be delayed if hiring volumes are high or orientations are infrequent.
For recruiters, this stage serves as a critical quality and integrity filter, typically adding a few days to the overall hiring timeline. Candidates can expedite this stage and avoid surprises by running a background check on themselves beforehand to catch and fix any data errors.
Total hiring time: Ranges from a few days to three weeks, with a potential duration of up to 30 days during busy seasons.
## How does Walmart hire for technical roles?
There are four different ways in which Walmart hires for technical roles.
- Direct applications: Walmart regularly posts open positions on its official careers site. Candidates can apply directly, sign up for job alerts, and track new opportunities in real-time by entering their email address on job listings of interest.
- Hackathons: Walmart Labs runs hackathons like Wonder Coder and CodeHers to tap into diverse tech talent, especially women coders. Winners often receive prizes, and some are offered direct hiring opportunities after the event.
- Employee referrals: Walmart encourages employees to refer others. Networking with Walmart associates on LinkedIn and requesting recommendations or referrals can increase your chances of landing an interview.
- On-campus drives: Walmart conducts campus placement drives at universities, especially for engineering and tech talent. These drives often come with competitive salary packages for high-performing students.
Technical hiring at Walmart is more detailed, with assessment rounds focusing on both technical and cultural fit. For tech and corporate roles, the recruitment step involves:
### Application
Candidates apply online through Walmart’s careers portal. Job descriptions are precise and skill-specific, attracting the right talent from the start.
### Recruiter screening
Dedicated recruiters screen resumes for relevant experience, technical expertise (languages, tools, platforms), and key career achievements, filtering fast and accurately at the top of the funnel.
### Phone screening (30 mins)
Typically scheduled within one week after resume screening, the first step involves a phone screening with recruiters or talent acquisition specialists. Hiring specialists often ask general questions related to job fit, past work experience, background, motivation, and experience.
### Online technical assessment (60 to 90 mins)
Shortlisted candidates undergo skill-based tests that cover coding, algorithms, data structures, and situational judgment, allowing recruiters to validate skills before interviews objectively.
For software developer roles, there would be a mix of coding questions (3) and aptitude-related multiple-choice questions (10).
The aptitude test will evaluate candidates’ knowledge of mathematics and logical reasoning. Coding questions will be based on the candidates’ preferred programming language.
Apart from these, situational judgment, behavioral, and cultural questions might also be asked.
The overall online assessment is quite comprehensive, lasting about 60-90 minutes on platforms like Testlify, which also has all the above-mentioned tests.
### Technical interviews (2-4 rounds for 60 mins)
Senior engineers or hiring managers conduct a secondary technical interview, which typically lasts 2-4 rounds. However, this stage could vary based on position and seniority.
Technical interviews include live coding challenges, system or product design scenarios, and in-depth reviews of past projects, providing recruiters with rich, structured data to inform their decisions. Each round typically lasts 45 to 60 minutes.
### Behavioral & managerial interview (30-45 mins)
The final step emphasises teamwork, communication, adaptability, and cultural fit, which are essential for Walmart’s collaborative and fast-paced environment.
Once candidates successfully demonstrate cultural alignment, problem-solving ability, and other key competencies, their profiles are reviewed by the hiring committee before an offer is extended.
Timeline: Conducted after technical rounds, generally in the same interview phase or shortly after.
### Review & offer
A panel or hiring manager reviews combined feedback, and offers are extended to successful candidates. The average time-to-hire for technical roles ranges from 3 to 6 weeks.
This process enables recruiters to scale hiring for niche, high-skill roles while maintaining quality, fairness, and speed, a valuable lesson for any high-volume recruiting team.
## Why is the Walmart hiring process lengthy?
Walmart’s hiring process is often seen as lengthy due to the high volume of applications, which necessitates multiple manual approval stages.
Additionally, for technical roles, recruiters also need to schedule separate orientation sessions. Corporate roles also include additional interview rounds, which adds to interview complexity.
Most technical roles at Walmart don’t require drug testing, but for roles involved with machinery and heavy lifting, a pre-employment drug test is mandatory.
## How Walmart became a tech giant?
Walmart became a tech giant by steadily investing in technology, data, and supply chain innovation. It was one of the first retailers to use UPC barcodes for product tracking and establish a private satellite network in the 1980s to connect its stores, warehouses, suppliers, and headquarters.
Later, it created Walmart Labs, now Walmart Global Tech, by acquiring companies like Kosmix to bring research, engineering, and digital expertise in-house. Over time, Walmart built advanced systems for inventory management, predictive analytics, and real-time pricing.
Today, it operates more than 10,500 stores in 19 countries, serving approximately 255 million customers each week, and generating over USD 600 billion in annual revenue.
These moves have helped Walmart shift from a traditional retailer into a tech-powered omnichannel business that competes with digital-first companies.
Walmart is also an excellent example of a company that depends heavily on skills-based hiring. One study found that by partnering with skills assessment platforms, Walmart saw a 40% increase in candidate test completion rates. Such a skills-first approach reduced time to hire and improved cost per hire.
## How does Walmart use AI?
Walmart uses AI across nearly every aspect of its business to enhance speed, accuracy, and personalisation. ## What are the hiring challenges faced by Walmart recruiters?
Walmart recruiters face significant challenges managing the enormous volume of applications, especially for frontline and hourly positions where turnover is high.
The reliance on automated resume screening to efficiently filter candidates can sometimes create a detached experience, leaving applicants feeling overlooked.
Additionally, Walmart’s process-driven and standardized culture can be perceived as rigid and less appealing to candidates seeking creative or entrepreneurial roles.
The decentralized hiring practices across various locations add complexity, making it difficult to maintain consistency and efficiency in recruitment.
Another major challenge lies in attracting and retaining specialized technical talent amid intense competition from other tech giants.
Walmart has launched initiatives like coding challenges and innovation contests to engage skilled technologists, but competing for these candidates remains difficult due to the demand for specialized skills and the company’s retail-centric reputation.
Moreover, the multi-stage interview processes, especially for technical roles, can sometimes extend timelines, causing candidate drop-off and frustration.
Balancing high-volume hiring needs with quality and candidate experience remains a constant challenge for Walmart’s talent acquisition teams.
