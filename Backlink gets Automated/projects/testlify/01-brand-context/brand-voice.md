# Testlify Brand Voice & Messaging

This document defines the Testlify brand voice, tone, and messaging framework. Reference this when writing all content to ensure consistency.

## Brand Voice Pillars

### 1. Skills Beat Resumes
- **What it means**: Every piece of content pushes the same conviction: judge candidates on demonstrated skills, never on resumes or gut feel. The site names the enemy outright ("resume manipulation," "relying on their gut feeling") and offers evidence as the cure.
- **How it sounds**: A confident recruiter-to-recruiter argument. It states the problem with resumes plainly, then shows the skills-first alternative in the same breath, like a colleague who has already made this switch and wants you to catch up.
- **Example**: "By going beyond traditional resumes, it allows companies to assess candidates based on real-world skills, competencies, and job readiness." (testlify.com/makipeople-alternatives)
- **Avoid**: praising resumes or credentials as proxies for ability, "gut feel" framed as a valid method, vague talk of "potential" with no assessment behind it. If a claim about a candidate can't be tested, don't write it.

### 2. Show the Number
- **What it means**: Proof arrives as a specific figure with a source, never as an adjective. Outcomes (55% faster time-to-hire, 72% lower hiring costs, 94% candidate satisfaction), scale (3,000+ tests, 4,500+ roles, 45+ programming languages, 100+ ATS integrations), and research (Gallup, McKinsey, PwC, cited by name) all get exact numbers.
- **How it sounds**: Stat-first and matter-of-fact. Blog posts open with a statistic and its source; product pages stack counted features. The number does the persuading; the sentence around it stays calm.
- **Example**: "Reduce time-to-hire by 55%." (testlify.com homepage)
- **Avoid**: "significantly," "dramatically," "many companies," "studies show" with no named study, rounded vagueness where the site has an exact figure. Check: every claim of improvement carries a number or a named source.

### 3. Fair by Design, Careful by Law
- **What it means**: Fairness is a core promise, stated in legally careful language: assessments "reduce bias" and create "a level playing field," and the process is "defensible under EEOC Uniform Guidelines." The brand never claims to eliminate bias.
- **How it sounds**: Measured and precise, closer to a compliance briefing than a slogan when the stakes are legal ("Lawful DEI programs produce diverse hires as an outcome of better processes; they do not set demographic hiring quotas"), warm when the stakes are human ("creating a level playing field and ensuring a fair assessment process").
- **Example**: "Enterprise HR teams use Testlify to evaluate all candidates against the same job-related criteria, reducing adverse impact and building a defensible selection process." (testlify.com/hr-glossary/diversity-candidate)
- **Avoid**: "bias-free," "eliminates bias," "removes bias," any absolute claim about the product's fairness, quota-flavored language. Test: could a lawyer sign off on the sentence?

### 4. Simple. Fair. Risk-free.
- **What it means**: Buying and using Testlify carries no traps. Pricing is transparent to the dollar, the guarantee is unconditional, and the copy removes risk instead of piling on hype. The pillar name is the site's own line.
- **How it sounds**: Short, clipped declaratives that strip risk away one fragment at a time. Direct FAQ answers that open with "Yes," or "No." Exact prices and limits stated plainly ($0.5 per photo ID verification, 1,000 monthly invites, $1 per extra invite).
- **Example**: "If Testlify doesn't meet your expectations, you can request a full refund within the first 30 days. No explanations needed. Simple. Fair. Risk-free." (testlify.com/why-testlify)
- **Avoid**: hidden-cost hedging, "contact us for pricing" evasiveness, fine-print qualifiers stacked onto a promise, hype adjectives doing the work a number should do. Check: is every commercial claim specific enough to hold the company to?

### 5. The Hiring Coach
- **What it means**: Outside product pages, Testlify writes as a working coach for recruiters and HR teams: it teaches processes step by step, answers the questions readers actually type, and treats the reader as a capable peer under real pressure.
- **How it sounds**: Question-led and encouraging. Sections open with the reader's own question ("What is a talent review?", "But how do you find them?"), answer it directly, and reassure without condescending: "The talent review process doesn't have to be a chore, though it does require planning."
- **Example**: "In this blog, we break down Walmart interview process so your company can do it too." (testlify.com/walmart-hiring-process)
- **Avoid**: lecturing from above, academic distance, content that explains a concept but never tells the reader what to do next, third-person detachment where second person fits. Check: does the piece hand the reader a runnable next step?

## Tone Guidelines

### General Tone: The Evidence-First Hiring Coach
Imagine you're an experienced talent-assessment consultant sitting beside a recruiter who is drowning in applications. You're confident because you have the numbers (you cite them constantly, with sources), you're direct because their time is short, and you're warm because hiring is human work: "Testlify makes hiring more human." You teach more than you sell, you name the enemies openly (resumes, gut feel, cheating), and when you talk fairness you choose your words the way a lawyer would.

### Tone Variations by Content Type

**How-To Guides**: Instructional, step-by-step, reassuring, concrete
- "The talent review process doesn't have to be a chore, though it does require planning."
- "In this blog, we break down Walmart interview process so your company can do it too."
- "Below, we've compiled a list of interview questions to help you identify the best candidates."
- "Find the best candidates early. Make sure only those with strong creative skills move forward."

**Strategy/Advice Content**: Question-led, coaching, stat-backed, peer-to-peer
- "Are your employees handling too many tasks at once? Many businesses believe multitasking boosts productivity, but research suggests otherwise."
- "Transferable skills form the backbone of the resilient workforce."
- "While gut feelings shouldn't be the sole decision-making factor, they often reflect the subconscious recognition of personality traits that align with the team's needs."
- "You should look for candidates with the potential to grow within the organization instead of only hiring candidates who meet specific job requirements."

**Industry News/Trends**: Explanatory, even-handed, myth-busting, curious
- "But why is this trend emerging now? What should recruiters consider when hiring pink-collar workers?"
- "However, this is just a perception and not true."
- "It might sound outdated, but the mindset still lingers today."
- "As hiring becomes more competitive and high-stakes, these gaps become harder to ignore."

**Product/Feature Content**: Confident, benefit-first, number-dense, punchy
- "Hire Better. Faster. Globally."
- "Don't just hire; hire the best with Testlify!"
- "Pay less as your usage grows."
- "Our advanced algorithms do the heavy lifting, automatically grading and ranking participants."

## Writing Style Guidelines

### Sentence Structure
- **Vary length**: Mix clipped fragments ("Simple. Fair. Risk-free." / "Hire Better. Faster. Globally.") with medium declaratives and the occasional feature-dense compound sentence. Never run three same-length sentences in a row.
- **Active voice preferred**: "Our advanced algorithms do the heavy lifting, automatically grading and ranking participants" — not "Participants are automatically graded and ranked by the platform."
- **Average length**: > ⚑ HUMAN DECISION: no word-count range is grounded in the evidence. The observed pattern is "short benefit-led sentences mixed with feature-dense compound ones" and "medium-length declaratives"; set a numeric range only after measuring real pages.
- **Clarity first**: If a sentence needs a second read, split it. The FAQ register is the model: open with "Yes," or "No.", then explain.

### Paragraph Structure
- **Length**: 1–3 sentences, the pattern observed across the blog ("Short one-to-three-sentence paragraphs").
- **One idea per paragraph**: Each paragraph makes one claim, backs it with one number or example, and stops.
- **Transitions**: Bridge with the reader's next question ("But how do you find them?") rather than connective filler.
- **White space**: Break dense material into bolded-lead-in bullets, numbered steps, or a "TLDR: Key takeaway" list at the top, as the white-collar/blue-collar post does.

### Word Choice
- **Conversational and second-person**: Talk to "you," the recruiter ("to help you identify the best skills assessment tool for your recruitment needs").
- **Specific with real numbers**: "Reduce time-to-hire by 55%," "42 days to fill a role," "$15,000 per bad hire" — never "much faster" or "expensive mistakes."
- **Verb-first in bullets**: The site's feature bullets open with an action verb: Gain, Create, Assess, Safeguard, Automatically generate.
- **Cut the fluff**: Drop "in today's rapidly evolving landscape," "unlock the potential," "cutting-edge," "world-class," and any adjective a number could replace. (Some legacy pages carry these; new content should not.)

### Terminology
**Say This** → **Not That**
- Candidates → Applicants (site-wide convention: people taking assessments are always "candidates")
- Conversational AI interview → Chatbot ("chatbot" undersells the chat/voice/video simulation and dates the product)
- Reduce bias / a level playing field → Bias-free / eliminates bias (legally careful language only; never an absolute claim)
- Skills assessment / talent assessment → Quiz / exam ("assessment" carries the validated, SME-built, I/O-psychologist-reviewed rigor)
- Test library → Question bank (the product is 3,000+ complete validated tests, not loose questions)
- Time-to-hire, hiring costs, candidate satisfaction → "Faster, cheaper, better" (name the metric the 55% / 72% / 94% claims attach to)

## Content Formatting

### Headlines
- **Be specific**: name the capability and its count: "Live coding tests in 45+ languages," "Skills assessments in 15+ languages."
- **Include the benefit**: sentence-case benefit statements: "Pay less as your usage grows," "Built to keep your organization secure."
- **Use numbers**: "Top 15 Vervoe Alternatives and Competitors in 2026," "How to perform a talent review in 2026 [in 5 simple steps]," "7 interview questions to check if the candidate is tech-savvy."
- **Length cap**: > ⚑ HUMAN DECISION: no character cap is grounded in the evidence; observed headlines run from three words to ~12. Set a cap after auditing real titles.

### Subheadings
- **Question-form H2s** that mirror what readers search: "What is a talent review?", "Why is the Walmart interview process lengthy?", "Is a personality hire sensible?"
- **Sentence case** throughout ("What are the advantages of job specialization?"), never Title Case on subheads.
- **Numbered step H3s** for processes, with durations in parentheses where known: "Phone screening (30 mins)."
- **Plain noun labels** for catalog content: "Numerical aptitude tests," "Welder roles and responsibilities."

### Lists
- **Numbered lists** for sequences and rankings (Step 1/2/3, Top 15); **bulleted lists** for features and benefits.
- **Bolded lead-ins** on bullets: "**Test library:** Over 3,500 pre-built tests…", "**Healthcare:**", "**Written:**".
- **Parallel structure**: every bullet in a set opens the same way, usually verb-first (Gain, Create, Safeguard).
- **Open long pieces with a "TLDR: Key takeaway" bullet list** so the skimmer gets the answer first.

### Calls-to-Action
- **Clear action**: use the site's real button text: "Try for Free," "Book a Demo," "Sign up for free," "View plans," "Generate," "start your free trial," "Add to testlify," "View sample questions."
- **Value-focused**: state what the click delivers: "Book a free 30-minute live demo with Testlify" (free, 30 minutes, live).
- **Timing**: on blog posts, CTAs are inline text links at natural decision points and at the close ("Explore Testlify's extensive test library to get started."), not interruptive banners.
- **Placement**: product pages pair a primary button ("Sign up for free") with a secondary proof link ("View sample questions"); repeat "Learn more" per feature section.

## Voice Examples

### Excellent Testlify Voice ✅

**Example 1 — the product/pricing register** (verbatim, testlify.com/why-testlify):

"Credit-based pricing. Pay only when a qualified candidate starts an assessment. One credit per candidate."

"If Testlify doesn't meet your expectations, you can request a full refund within the first 30 days. No explanations needed. Simple. Fair. Risk-free."

**Why this works**:
- Buyer risk is acknowledged head-on and dismantled piece by piece, not talked around.
- Clipped fragments ("Simple. Fair. Risk-free.") carry conviction without a single hype adjective.
- Every claim is concrete and checkable: one credit, one candidate, 30 days, full refund.
- Active, second-person framing puts the reader in control ("you can request").
- The expectation is set exactly: what you pay, when, and what happens if it doesn't work out.

**Example 2 — the editorial/blog register, hook construction** (verbatim, testlify.com/walmart-hiring-process):

"But, here's the catch: Walmart isn't just the world's largest retailer; it's also #1 on the Fortune Global 500 and hires over 500,000 people a year."

"In this blog, we break down Walmart interview process so your company can do it too."

**Why this works**:
- The hook earns attention with stacked, verifiable facts (Fortune #1, 500,000 hires), not manufactured suspense.
- The conversational pivot ("But, here's the catch") reads like a colleague talking, then the numbers do the persuading.
- The promise to the reader is immediate and practical: "so your company can do it too."
- "We break down" positions Testlify as the coach doing work for the reader.
- Scale proof doubles as product proof: the world's biggest hirer runs on skills testing.

## Audience Understanding

### Who We Write For

**Primary Audience**: Recruiters and talent acquisition teams (SMB to enterprise)
- Screening high volumes of applications, often "for frontline and hourly positions where turnover is high"
- Under time pressure: "recruiters take 42 days to fill a role" on average
- Skeptical of resumes: struggling with "resume manipulation and interview cheating"
- Measured on time-to-hire, cost-per-hire, and quality-of-hire
- Working global, multilingual pipelines and connected ATS stacks (Lever, BambooHR, Greenhouse, JazzHR)

**Secondary Audiences**:
- **HR professionals and talent-management leaders**: run talent reviews, succession planning, and retention programs; worried that "63% of companies are concerned about their ability to develop future leaders."
- **CXOs and business leaders**: care about quality-of-hire, the cost of a bad hire (up to 30% of the role's salary), and hiring at scale; the interview-question resources address "HR professionals and CXOs" directly.
- **Enterprise compliance and legal-minded HR**: OFCCP-covered contractors and teams managing adverse-impact risk who need "documented scoring records that support adverse impact defense."
- **Career counselors and educators**: reach the HR glossary, tech glossary, and skills content ("HR professionals, career counselors, and workforce planners") to teach hiring vocabulary and skills-based thinking.

### What They Care About

**Top Priorities**:
- Cutting time-to-hire (the 55% claim is the flagship number)
- Reducing hiring costs (72%) and avoiding the $15,000 bad hire
- Quality of hire, verified through demonstrated skills
- Candidate experience (94% satisfaction) and employer brand
- Fair, defensible selection that survives legal scrutiny

**Pain Points**:
- Resumes and interviews that can be gamed: "resume manipulation and interview cheating often make it difficult to identify truly qualified candidates"
- Volume: "the enormous volume of applications" and automated screening that "can sometimes create a detached experience"
- Bias creeping in: screening out "often results in unconscious biases"; "Bias creeps into talent review if caution is not exercised"
- The skills gap: "87% of companies either have a skills gap or expect one in the next two years"
- Slow, multi-stage processes that "extend timelines, causing candidate drop-off and frustration"

### How to Serve Them
- **Answer the searched question first**: structure content around the exact questions readers type ("What is a talent review?"), and answer in the first paragraph.
- **Hand over runnable process**: numbered steps, interview-question lists with "look for:" guidance, and board-ready templates, so the reader can act today.
- **Prove before you claim**: attach a named source or exact number to every assertion, the way the blog cites Gallup, McKinsey, and PwC by name.
- **Route to the relevant test, not the homepage**: link the specific assessment ("Use our teamwork test," "Check out our problem solving test") at the moment of need.
- **Respect their expertise**: write peer-to-peer, acknowledge trade-offs honestly (as the personality-hire post argues both sides), and never lecture.

## Quality Checklist

- [ ] **Voice**: Does it sound like the evidence-first hiring coach: confident, direct, human?
- [ ] **Tone**: Does the register match the page type (punchy for product, question-led for advice, measured for compliance)?
- [ ] **Value**: Does the reader leave with something runnable: a step list, a template, a named test?
- [ ] **Clarity**: Paragraphs of 1–3 sentences, one idea each, skimmable structure with bolded lead-ins?
- [ ] **Accuracy**: Is every number real and current (55% / 72% / 94%, 3,000+ tests, 45+ languages) and every study named?
- [ ] **Examples**: Are claims illustrated with concrete cases (Walmart's 500,000 hires) rather than abstractions?
- [ ] **Action**: Is there one clear, valued CTA using real button text ("Try for Free," "Book a Demo")?
- [ ] **Messaging**: Does it reinforce one of the five core messages, especially skills over resumes?
- [ ] **Terminology**: Candidates not applicants, conversational AI interview not chatbot, "reduce bias" never "bias-free"?
- [ ] **Empowerment**: Does it treat the reader as a capable peer and leave them more able to hire well, with or without the product?
