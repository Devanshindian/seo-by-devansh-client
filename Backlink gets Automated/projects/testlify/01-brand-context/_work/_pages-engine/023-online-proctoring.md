# What is online proctoring? A simple guide
Online proctoring helps teams run secure remote assessments with the right mix of identity checks, monitoring, and fair review.

Online proctoring is the process of supervising an assessment remotely. It helps verify identity, watch for rule violations, and keep the result fair when the test is taken remotely. Depending on the setup, it can include ID checks, browser controls, webcam monitoring, and session review.
As more assessments move online (65%), protecting exam integrity has become harder. Journal of Academic Ethics indicates that 44.7% of surveyed students self-reported cheating in online exams.
Organizations now have to think about risks like impersonation, AI-assisted answering, hidden devices, remote access tools, and other workarounds that can affect fairness if the testing environment is not controlled properly.
This guide will help you make sense of that. We will break down how online proctoring works, the main models used today, the risks it is designed to reduce, the features that matter most, and the trade-offs recruiting teams should think about before choosing the right setup.

## How does online proctoring work?
Online proctoring usually follows a simple flow. First, the system checks whether the candidate can take the exam properly. Then it verifies who they are, secures the test session, watches for suspicious activity during the exam, and finally saves the right evidence for review. Each step is meant to reduce uncertainty, so the final result is easier to trust.
A simple view of how online proctoring moves from setup to review.
### Pre-exam tech checks and candidate instructions
The process starts before the first question appears. Most online proctoring platforms run a quick system check to confirm the candidate’s device, browser, webcam, microphone, and internet connection are working properly. This reduces avoidable issues once the exam begins.
This stage also sets the rules clearly. Candidates are usually told what is allowed, what is not, what permissions they must grant, and how the session will be monitored.
### Identity verification and room scan
Once the candidate is ready, identity verification begins. This often includes a government ID check, a live photo or selfie match, and in some setups, biometric verification. The purpose is to make sure the person starting the test is the same person who was invited to take it.
Identity verification is important in remote hiring and other high-stakes assessments where impersonation is a real risk.
After identity checks, many platforms ask the candidate to show their surroundings. This can include a desk scan, a live environment check, or even a second camera angle in stricter setups. The aim is to catch obvious risks early, such as hidden notes, another device nearby, someone else in the room, or off-camera help waiting just outside the view.
### Secure session controls during the exam
Once the exam starts, this is where lockdown browsers, full-screen rules, copy-paste restrictions, tab-switch tracking, and app blocking come in. These controls help stop common workarounds such as opening search engines, switching to messaging apps, screen sharing, or using remote access tools during the test.
Many modern systems also monitor behavior during the session. That can include webcam and screen recording, face and eye movement tracking, object detection for phones, and logs for actions like tab changes or unusual typing behavior.
In more advanced setups, the software can also look for signs of virtual machines, browser extensions, or other technical tricks used to cheat.
### Flags, recordings, and post-exam review
Online proctoring does not always mean a human watches every second live. In many cases, the system records the session, flags suspicious moments, and creates an audit trail that reviewers can check later. That may include video clips, screen recordings, snapshots, timestamps, suspicion scores, and question-level activity logs showing how long someone spent on each part of the exam.
This matters because raw monitoring alone is not enough. Review is what turns data into a decision.
For example, if a candidate disconnects, reconnects, and submits a difficult answer almost instantly, the timeline gives reviewers useful context. Strong platforms make it easier to understand what happened, separate harmless behavior from real misconduct, and make a fair final call.
## What are the main types of online proctoring?
In practice, most platforms use one of three models, or a mix of them. The real difference is, who is watching, when suspicious activity is handled, and how much scale the process can support.
### Live remote proctoring
Live remote proctoring means a human proctor watches the session as the candidate takes the exam. The proctor may monitor through webcam, screen share, chat, or voice, and can step in immediately if something looks wrong.
That real-time intervention is what makes this model different. If a candidate keeps looking off-screen, leaves the frame, tries to use another device, or fails an environment check, the proctor can pause the test, ask for a fresh room scan, issue a warning, or end the session based on the rules in place.
This model is usually chosen for high-stakes exams where immediate action matters more than scale. It works well when the cost of a bad decision is high, such as certification exams, licensing tests, university entrance assessments, or tightly controlled internal evaluations.
### Automated or AI-based proctoring
AI-powered proctoring or automated proctoring uses software to monitor the session instead of relying on a human to watch live from start to finish. The system can verify identity, enforce browser restrictions, track tab switches, record the screen and webcam feed, detect phones or extra faces, and flag unusual behavior for review.
This model is popular because it is easier to run at scale. A hiring team can assess many candidates across locations without needing a live proctor for every session. That makes it practical for volume hiring, campus drives, large applicant funnels, and distributed testing programs.
Its biggest strength is coverage and consistency. The system can watch for the same rule violations across every session, create logs automatically, and surface suspicious moments faster than a manual reviewer could from raw footage alone.
But automated proctoring still needs human judgment. A flag is not proof of cheating by itself. Looking away from the screen or reconnecting after a network issue may be harmless.
### Record and review proctoring
Record and review proctoring sits between live monitoring and fully automated decisioning. The session is recorded, the system may still apply some checks or controls, but the actual review happens after the exam rather than during it.
This model is useful when organizations want evidence and auditability without the cost and complexity of live proctors. Reviewers can go back to flagged timestamps, watch the relevant clips, check activity logs, and decide whether the session looks clean.
For many teams, this is the most practical middle ground. It gives more flexibility than live proctoring and more context than basic automated alerts alone. It is especially useful when the exam does not require instant intervention, but still needs a fair review trail.
### Which model fits which use case?
The right model depends on three things: how high the stakes are, how many people you need to assess, and whether you need to intervene during the session or review it later.

## What kinds of risks is online proctoring designed to prevent?
Online proctoring is not just about catching obvious cheating. The real job is to reduce the ways a remote test can be manipulated when no one is physically in the room. That includes identity fraud, AI-assisted answering, hidden devices, off-camera help, browser workarounds, and technical tricks that make a session look normal when it is not.
A simple risk map showing where remote assessments are most likely to be manipulated.
### Impersonation and proxy test-taking
the wrong person takes the test. In remote hiring, certification, and other high-stakes assessments, a stand-in can completely distort the result. That is why many proctoring flows start with ID verification, face matching, and continuous webcam checks, not just a one-time login.
If the face on screen changes, another person enters the frame, or the session shows signs of swapping candidates, the platform can flag it for review. This is a core use case for online proctoring
Impersonation is not just a policy problem on paper. There are open online posts advertising “bypass” help and real-time support for proctored exams, which shows there is still an active market for impersonation and assisted testing. That is exactly why identity verification matters so much at the start of a session.
### AI tools, search help, and unauthorized assistance
This is the risk that has changed the most. It is no longer just about someone opening Google in another tab. Candidates may try to use ChatGPT, browser-based AI helpers, hidden overlays, screen-reading tools, or external help from another person during the session.
Some assessment vendors now openly discuss the rise of AI-assisted cheating and the need to detect attempts to hide or bypass monitoring, while recent research also suggests a meaningful share of candidates are willing to use AI tools in assessments.
That is why lockdown and proctoring tools focus so much on restricting what else can run during the session. Lockdown Browser prevents access to other URLs, apps, printing, and copying. In plain terms, these controls are meant to make it harder to jump from the test to outside help.
### Hidden notes, extra devices, and off-camera support
A lot of real-world cheating is low-tech and practical. Hidden notes on the desk, a second phone just outside the webcam frame, a smartwatch, an extra monitor, or another person sitting nearby can be enough to compromise the session. This is where room scans, secondary camera setups, object detection, and repeated visual checks matter.
Major platforms specifically call out phone usage, multiple people in frame, and off-camera assistance as risks worth flagging, because these are common, scalable, and hard to catch without structured monitoring.
### Tab switching, screen sharing, and remote access tools
Not every violation is visible on camera. Some of the most important risks happen on the device itself. Candidates may switch tabs, leave full-screen mode, use copy-paste, open messaging apps, share their screen, or connect to remote access tools so someone else can guide or control the session.
Proctoring systems try to reduce this through lockdown controls, focus tracking, browser event logs, and alerts tied to suspicious system behavior.
### Technical workarounds and testing environment blind spots
Some of the hardest online proctoring risks are not obvious at all. They come from gaps in what the system can actually see.
A candidate may keep the test screen looking clean while getting help somewhere just outside the monitored view, such as a phone placed below the webcam line, a poor room angle that hides part of the desk, weak lighting, or an incomplete environment scan. That is exactly why stricter proctoring setups now use room scans and, in some cases, a secondary mobile camera to widen visibility beyond one webcam view.
There is also a more technical side to this. Some users try to create distance between the monitored browser and what they are really doing by using software or settings that interfere with screen sharing, limit visibility, or disrupt the session at useful moments.
In current remote-testing setups, vendors still actively troubleshoot extension-related screen-sharing failures and secure exam providers still restrict certain network conditions because these setup-level gaps can weaken monitoring even when the candidate appears normal on screen.
The real risk here is not just suspicious behavior. It is a test environment that looks compliant while leaving enough blind spots for outside help, hidden workflows, or partial monitoring to slip through.

## What are the biggest concerns and limitations of online proctoring?
Online proctoring can improve trust in remote assessments, but it is not frictionless. Research on remote-proctored exams keeps pointing to the same trade-offs: false positives, privacy concerns, technical barriers, added stress, and the fact that proctoring alone is not a perfect fix.
Online proctoring can improve test security, but candidate concerns around privacy, access, and false flags still matter.
- False positives can damage trust: In one 2023 study, 68.3% of students who ranked all concerns said they were very concerned about being wrongfully flagged by proctoring software. That is a strong reminder that automated alerts need human review, not blind enforcement.
- Privacy is still the biggest objection for many test takers: A survey of 102 students found that 52% agreed online proctoring was too privacy invasive, while only 39% said they preferred an online proctored exam. When a platform records the screen, webcam, microphone, ID, and room view, consent and data handling matter a lot.
- Not every candidate has the same setup quality: In one study, 38% of students were somewhat or not at all confident they had the equipment needed for online proctoring, and 52% were somewhat or not at all confident they had the technical know-how to set it up properly. Another study found 18.5% struggled to find a quiet space and 8.4% faced Wi-Fi issues during the exam.
- The experience can add stress and test anxiety: The same University of Minnesota study found that only 40% of students felt comfortable or very comfortable in the testing environment. Another peer-reviewed study reported emotional distress related to remote proctoring in 21.4% of respondents.
- Proctoring does not eliminate cheating by itself: In the privacy survey, 61% of participants said they believed they could still cheat if they wanted to. That matters because even a strong online proctoring setup works best as one layer in a broader assessment design, not as the only safeguard.

## How do you choose the right level of online proctoring?
The right setup depends on one simple question: how much damage does a bad result cause? If the assessment is only one early signal in the hiring process, you usually do not need the strictest controls. But if the result will strongly influence who moves forward, who gets certified, or who is trusted with a high-risk role, the proctoring level needs to go up as well.
### Low-stakes vs high-stakes assessments
Not every assessment needs the same amount of control. A short screening test for early filtering is very different from a final-stage technical assessment, a certification exam, or a compliance-based evaluation.
For low-stakes assessments, the goal is usually to discourage casual misuse without creating too much friction. For high-stakes assessments, the goal shifts from convenience to defensibility. You need stronger proof that the right person took the test, followed the rules, and completed the session fairly.
### When live proctoring makes sense
Live proctoring makes the most sense when real-time intervention matters. That usually happens when the assessment is high value, tightly controlled, or difficult to repeat.
If a candidate leaves the frame, fails an environment check, or appears to get outside help, a live proctor can step in immediately.
### When automated monitoring is enough
Automated monitoring works well when scale matters more than live intervention. It is often enough for remote hiring teams running large volumes of assessments across locations and time zones.
In these cases, the platform can handle identity checks, browser restrictions, screen recording, suspicious behavior flags, and review trails without needing a human to watch every session in real time. That keeps the process more efficient while still giving teams useful evidence if something needs to be checked later.

### How to balance security with fairness
This is where many teams get it wrong. Stronger security is not always better if it adds more friction than the assessment actually needs.
A good setup should protect the integrity of the result without making honest candidates feel trapped, confused, or unfairly judged. That means matching the controls to the stakes, giving clear instructions upfront, and using human review before making serious decisions based on flags alone.

## Frequently asked questions (FAQs)
What does online proctoring check before the exam starts?
Common checks include ID verification, camera and microphone readiness, workspace review, allowed materials, and room conditions such as lighting, privacy, and whether extra monitors or programs are open.
Do you need a webcam, microphone, and private room for an online proctored exam?
In many setups, yes. Test-takers need to use a webcam and microphone, sit in a quiet and well-lit room, be alone, clear the workspace, and unplug additional monitors before starting.
Can online proctoring detect phones, extra monitors, or another person in the room?
It is designed to check for those risks. Official test-day guidance requires a clear desk, no extra monitors, and a private room, while Respondus describes room scans, facial detection, and flags tied to leaving the camera view or accessing devices.
Does online proctoring block tab switching, copy-paste, or other apps?
Secure exam browsers are specifically built to limit that kind of behavior. LockDown Browser prevents printing, copying, going to other URLs, and accessing other applications during the test.
Is online proctoring only for high-stakes exams?
No, but it matters most when exam integrity has real consequences. Current workflows show it is used in both live and automated formats, which makes it flexible enough for different test types and scale levels.
