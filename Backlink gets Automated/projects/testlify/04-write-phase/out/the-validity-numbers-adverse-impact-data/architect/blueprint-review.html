The facts are settled. This page is the plan for the article itself: what it argues, which sections carry it, and how long each one gets.

The spine — the one argument this article makes

Every section below exists to move this sentence forward. If a section does not serve it, it should not be here.

Every assessment type carries two numbers, a predictive-validity coefficient and a group-difference figure, and this article puts the pair side by side for cognitive ability, personality and situational judgment tests so a CHRO or compliance-accountable HR leader can see which tests actually forecast performance and what each one costs in pass-rate disparity. It then shows how that disparity is measured under the four-fifths rule, what the employer has to prove once it is flagged, where federal enforcement now stands after Executive Order 14281, and which design choices shrink the gap without giving up validity. At the end the reader can defend a specific test battery with named evidence rather than a vendor's fairness claim.

What it chose to leave out

The architect is shown the topics every ranking page covers. It is allowed to drop any that do not serve this article. Watch for the opposite of a problem here: if it never drops anything, it has stopped judging.

Google's own answer for this query is the generic disparate-impact doctrine (neutral rules, discriminatory effect, no intent needed, plaintiff's burden, employer's defense, less discriminatory alternative, Griggs/Title VII, recent federal shifts), and the ranking-page list came back empty. I gave that ground two real sections early enough to hold the reader: section 6 covers the measurement threshold, the burden shift, Griggs and the business-necessity defense, and section 7 covers the recent enforcement shift. I dropped only the housing/Fair Housing Act branch of Google's answer, because it sits outside employment law and outside this article's subject. The format's optional provenance block is folded into section 2, which names which publication and year each coefficient comes from and why the 1998 and 2022 figures cannot be compared directly.

The world — what this article is and is not about

Written in the research phase, before any searching started, and never changed since. It is one of the stated reasons the architect benches material below, so read it before judging whether a bench was right.

IS about
The statistical evidence behind pre-employment psychometric/cognitive-ability and personality assessments: validity coefficients (how well a test score predicts job performance), criterion-related validity studies, and adverse-impact data (the disparate pass/hire rates a test produces across protected groups, e.g. the four-fifths rule, EEOC guidelines, disparate-impact litigation). It covers how employers and I-O psychologists read these numbers to judge whether a hiring test is both predictive and legally defensible.
is NOT about
This is NOT about clinical or medical psychometrics (validity of a diagnostic instrument like a depression or IQ clinical scale, test-retest reliability in a clinical/research psychology sense). It is NOT about general statistical 'validity' in research methodology, survey design, or scientific studies unrelated to hiring. It is NOT about 'adverse impact' or 'adverse events' as used in medicine/pharmacovigilance (drug side effects). It is NOT about credit-scoring or lending 'adverse action' notices, which use similar disparate-impact language under different law (ECOA/FCRA) rather than employment law (Title VII/EEOC). It is NOT about broader DEI or workplace-culture diversity metrics unconnected to a specific test's pass-rate data. It is NOT about personality/psychometric tests used for non-hiring purposes such as team-building, coaching, or consumer personality quizzes (e.g. Myers-Briggs marketed to general audiences). It is NOT about academic testing validity (SAT/GRE admissions test validity), even though the same statistical vocabulary (validity coefficients, adverse impact) is used in that literature.
