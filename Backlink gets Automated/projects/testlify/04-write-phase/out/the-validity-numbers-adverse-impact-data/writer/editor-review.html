The score gaps in the previous sections become a legal problem through one piece of arithmetic: the four-fifths rule, which the rule. The EEOC treats it as a "rule of thumb" rather than a bright-line standard [c99]. Divide the pass rate of each group by the pass rate of the group that passed at the highest rate. If any ratio falls below 80%, federal enforcement agencies generally regard it as evidence of adverse impact, meaning one group is rejected at a much higher rate than another [c100]. The arithmetic is easy to run on a real hiring cycle. A "job fit" tool that selects 30 of 60 male candidates and 15 of 45 female candidates produces rates of 50% and 33%, a ratio well under the threshold [c102]. Nothing about that That flag says the test is biased or that the employer broke the law. It is a screening mechanism that invites scrutiny, not a finding of guilt [c143]. Small samples are where the rule misfires. Run it on 20 candidates and a single pass or fail can swing the ratio past the threshold in either direction, so statistical significance testing is used alongside it [c144]. Research comparing those tests found the Z-test on the difference between two proportions performs reasonably well except at very small samples, while the Fisher Exact Test and Yates's continuity-corrected chi-square test are not recommended for their low statistical power [c146]. A ratio above 80% is also not a safe harbor. Section 4D of the Uniform Guidelines states that "smaller differences in selection rate may nevertheless constitute adverse impact, where they are significant in both statistical and practical terms" [c147]. An employer at 78% with a non-significant test sits in a different legal position than one at 50% with a significant gap [c148]. ### What the employer has to prove once the ratio flags The flag shifts the burden. Once adverse impact is shown, the employer has to demonstrate that the selection procedure is job-related and consistent with business necessity [c155]. That standard, standard was codified in the Civil Rights Act of 1991, 1991. It requires evidence that the abilities the test measures are actually required to do the job, which in practice means a validation evidence study [c156]. Griggs v. Duke Power Co. established that standard in 1971, and it came out of a challenge to cognitive-style testing [c115]. The record since then favors specificity. In U.S. v.
