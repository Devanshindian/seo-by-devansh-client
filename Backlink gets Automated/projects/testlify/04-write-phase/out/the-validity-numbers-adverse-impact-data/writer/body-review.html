Why the 2022 Sackett Reanalysis Cut the Validity Numbers wrote 251 words, was asked for 248 +1% used 8 facts · 8 of them numbers · 4 sources
What this section was asked to do: Resolve the number conflict before it confuses anything downstream: explain that the 2022 Sackett reanalysis found a range-restriction correction error that inflated the older coefficients by .10 to .20, name which methods moved and in which direction, and settle that the absolute figures fell while the ordering of methods held. This is why every figure in this article carries its source and year.
What the writer actually wrote

The older validity coefficients were too high. In 2022, Paul Sackett, Charlene Zhang, Christopher Berry, and Filip Lievens published a reanalysis in the *Journal of Applied Psychology* showing that most of the benchmark figures had been overstated by .10 to .20 points [c9006].

The fault was mechanical, not fraudulent. Meta-analyses correct for range restriction, meaning the narrowing that happens when you only have performance data on people who were hired. That correction assumes candidates were hired using the test being scored. But 74% to 98% of the underlying studies tested people already on the job, hired on some other basis [c9008]. Applying the larger correction to that body of research inflated the results.

What moved, and by how much, differs sharply by method. Cognitive ability took one of the steepest falls, from Schmidt and Hunter's r = .51 in 1998 down to ρ = .31 [c3]. Work sample tests dropped furthest, from .54 to .33 [c14]. Structured interviews barely moved, sliding from .51 to .42 [c12], and empirically keyed biodata (scored life-history questionnaires) actually rose from .35 to .38 [c15].

That uneven movement reordered the top of the table. Structured interviews now rank as the strongest single predictor, ahead of cognitive ability [c9007].

The ordering, though, largely held. The methods that ranked high before still rank high; they are simply less predictive than the field believed [c9003]. Which is why every figure in this article carries its source and its year.

Cognitive Ability: Best Predictor, Widest Score Gap wrote 295 words, was asked for 270 +9% used 8 facts · 6 of them numbers · 5 sources
What this section was asked to do: Price the cognitive-ability row properly. Show why validity climbs with job complexity and generalises across jobs, then give the measured score gaps by race and by sex, and settle the differential-validity question so the reader knows the gap is in scores rather than in the score-to-performance relationship. Sets up the mitigation section by proving the gap is real and large.
What the writer actually wrote

Cognitive ability tests earn their place at the top of the validity table and carry the largest documented score gap of any assessment format [c43]. Both facts are true at once, and the second one is what a compliance-accountable buyer has to price.

Validity is not a single number that holds everywhere. It rises with job complexity. Hunter and Hunter reported r = .56 for high-complexity jobs, .51 for medium-complexity ones, and .40 for low-complexity work [c6]. So the test does its heaviest lifting in the roles where a hiring mistake costs the most, and its lightest in routine work where a cheaper method may serve.

What makes cognitive testing unusual is that this evidence travels. Industrial-organizational psychologists call it *validity generalization*: meta-analysis has established that validity evidence for cognitive aptitude tests holds across a broad range of jobs, rather than needing to be rebuilt role by role [c7]. That saves an employer the cost of a fresh local validation study for every position.

Now the other column. Roth and colleagues documented a Black-White standardized mean difference of d = 1.10 and a Hispanic-White difference of d = 0.72 on cognitive tests, while male-female differences sit at d = 0.00 to 0.10 [c44][c45]. So the exposure is racial and ethnic, not by sex. Woods concluded in 2024 that cognitive testing "continues to present a systematic adverse impact against people from particular minority ethnic backgrounds in many settings" [c48].

One question decides how you defend this. Berry investigated whether the test-to-performance relationship itself differs by racial and ethnic subgroup [c49]. Separate meta-analyses have run that correlation broken out for Asian, Black, Hispanic, and White candidates [c50]. The gap sits in the scores, not in what the scores predict.

What Personality Tests Predict, and Their ADA Risk wrote 317 words, was asked for 270 +17% used 13 facts · 7 of them numbers · 6 sources
What this section was asked to do: Show that personality tests trade a modest validity coefficient for a different kind of legal exposure. Establish conscientiousness at .20 to .30 and the composite of R = .60 with cognitive ability, state honestly that the wider evidence is mixed, then move the risk conversation to where it belongs for this test type: ADA screening of applicants with mental disabilities, and selection-ratio effects rather than race gaps.
What the writer actually wrote

Personality tests buy a modest gain in prediction and a different legal problem. Conscientiousness is the trait most consistently linked to job performance across occupations, but the correlation runs only about 0.20 to 0.30 and shifts by role [c266]. Sackett, Zhang, Berry, and Lievens (2022) put work-framed conscientiousness at .25 [c9015]. Watrin and colleagues (2023) put the raw applicant figure at .14, so treat the higher number as a ceiling rather than a plausible middle [c9016].

The pairing is where personality earns its place. Combining general mental ability with a conscientiousness measure produces a composite validity of R = .60, better than either predictor alone [c25]. That makes personality a supplement to cognitive testing rather than a replacement for it [c24]. The model matters too: the Big Five carries peer-reviewed validation for selection, while the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) does not [c266]. Read honestly, the wider record on personality and job performance is mixed [c71], which is why a score belongs alongside other signals and never on its own.

The adverse-impact picture also looks different here. Personality measures show near-zero Black-White score gaps, and the exposure tracks the construct, the selection ratio, and the incentive structure instead [c61]. Advance only the top three scorers for one opening and a fourth candidate can be cut on a trivial trait difference [c64]. McGee and McGee (2025) add a caution: when subjects had a bonus riding on the result, racial gaps appeared that were absent on unincentivized measures [c9018].

The sharper risk is the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA). Personality tests can screen out candidates with mental disabilities [c68], and a low score does not show inability to perform a job's essential functions [c73]. Vendor algorithms that match candidates against current top performers can carry that exclusion forward [c72]. Offering an alternative assessment or an opt-out is the documented accommodation [c74].

Do Situational Judgment Tests Really Reduce Adverse Impact? wrote 284 words, was asked for 270 +5% used 10 facts · 9 of them numbers · 5 sources
What this section was asked to do: Settle whether situational judgment tests are the compromise they are sold as. Give the meta-analytic validity range and the small incremental gain over cognitive ability and the Big Five, then attach the two caveats that decide whether the smaller gap survives contact with real hiring: cognitive loading in the scoring key, and the near doubling of the gap when measured on applicants rather than incumbents.
What the writer actually wrote

Situational judgment tests are a real compromise, but a narrower one than vendors imply. Their meta-analytic validity sits between .26 and .34, depending on the instruction format and the statistical corrections applied [c162]. McDaniel and colleagues, pooling 102 validity coefficients across 10,640 people, put the corrected figure at .34 [c159]. Christian, Edwards, and Bradley later found that SJTs built for leadership and interpersonal skills sit at the top of that range [c164].

What an SJT adds on top of tests you already run is small. Across studies, the incremental gain over cognitive ability alone is roughly .03 to .08 [c172], and the gain over the Big Five personality dimensions is similar [c233]. Webster and colleagues found the same direction in their 2020 systematic review: SJT scores generally predicted outcomes beyond knowledge and cognitive ability tests [c174]. So this is a supplement, not a substitute.

The pitch rests on the smaller score gap, and two caveats decide whether it holds. The first is cognitive loading, meaning how much of the scoring key rewards raw reasoning rather than judgment. Meta-analytic work cited by De Soete identifies it as one of the largest drivers of ethnic subgroup differences on SJTs [c194]. Keys built on knowledge-based instructions, asking what the best response is, correlate more strongly with cognitive ability [c195]. Behavioral-tendency instructions, asking what the candidate would most likely do, pull that loading down and shrink the differences with it [c196].

The second caveat is who was measured. In 2019, researchers reported Black-White differences of d = 0.66 in applicant samples against d = 0.38 in current employees [c189]. The gap nearly doubles once real candidates sit the test.

What the Four-Fifths Rule Flags, and What You Must Prove wrote 460 words, was asked for 405 +14% used 14 facts · 14 of them numbers · 8 sources
What this section was asked to do: Turn the score gaps of the previous four sections into the legal arithmetic. Define how the four-fifths ratio is calculated and where it misfires on small samples, establish that Section 4D lets a smaller difference still count, then show what the employer has to prove once a flag lands and which test types have survived that proof in court.
What the writer actually wrote

The score gaps in the previous sections become a legal problem through one piece of arithmetic: the four-fifths rule, which the EEOC treats as a "rule of thumb" rather than a bright-line standard [c99]. Divide the pass rate of each group by the pass rate of the group that passed at the highest rate. If any ratio falls below 80%, federal enforcement agencies generally regard it as evidence of adverse impact, meaning one group is rejected at a much higher rate than another [c100].

The arithmetic is easy to run on a real hiring cycle. A "job fit" tool that selects 30 of 60 male candidates and 15 of 45 female candidates produces rates of 50% and 33%, a ratio well under the threshold [c102]. Nothing about that flag says the test is biased or that the employer broke the law. It is a screening mechanism that invites scrutiny, not a finding of guilt [c143].

Small samples are where the rule misfires. Run it on 20 candidates and a single pass or fail can swing the ratio past the threshold in either direction, so statistical significance testing is used alongside it [c144]. Research comparing those tests found the Z-test on the difference between two proportions performs reasonably well except at very small samples, while the Fisher Exact Test and Yates's continuity-corrected chi-square test are not recommended for their low statistical power [c146].

A ratio above 80% is also not a safe harbor. Section 4D of the Uniform Guidelines states that "smaller differences in selection rate may nevertheless constitute adverse impact, where they are significant in both statistical and practical terms" [c147]. An employer at 78% with a non-significant test sits in a different legal position than one at 50% with a significant gap [c148].

The flag shifts the burden. Once adverse impact is shown, the employer has to demonstrate that the selection procedure is job-related and consistent with business necessity [c155]. That standard, codified in the Civil Rights Act of 1991, requires evidence that the abilities the test measures are actually required to do the job, which in practice means validation evidence [c156].

Griggs v. Duke Power Co. established that standard in 1971, and it came out of a challenge to cognitive-style testing [c115]. The record since then favors specificity. In U.S. v. City of New York, the courts found the fire department's 2002 and 2007 written entrance exams lacked content validity and had no defensible connection to job performance [c117]. General cognitive ability tests remain harder to defend on job-relatedness grounds than narrowly targeted skills tests [c116]. Customized, job-specific instruments hold up better than off-the-shelf ones [c118].

Executive Order 14281 Did Not Repeal Disparate Impact wrote 324 words, was asked for 270 +20% used 15 facts · 15 of them numbers · 7 sources
What this section was asked to do: Give the reader the current enforcement picture without letting them conclude the risk is gone. State the charge volume and settlement cost through the mid-2020s, the 2023 guidance that applied the four-fifths rule to AI tools, the 2025 reversal under Executive Order 14281, and then the correction: disparate impact still sits in Title VII, private plaintiffs still sue, and state and city rules now carry the accountability.
What the writer actually wrote

Federal enforcement has pulled back, but the legal exposure has not moved. Charges kept climbing through the mid-2020s: the EEOC received more than 88,000 discrimination charges in fiscal year 2024, its third consecutive annual increase [c111]. Race discrimination charges alone run past 27,000 a year [c86]. Then in May 2023 the agency published a technical assistance document applying the Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures to algorithmic tools, confirming that the four-fifths rule covers AI-driven selection [c105][c106].

That posture reversed in 2025. Executive Order 14281, signed April 23, 2025, directed federal agencies to deprioritize disparate-impact enforcement, and the EEOC stopped investigating charges resting on disparate impact alone [c120][c9037]. It also pulled its AI technical-assistance documents off its website [c121].

An executive order sets enforcement priorities. It cannot amend a statute. The Supreme Court recognized disparate impact in *Griggs v. Duke Power Co.* in 1971, and Congress wrote it into Title VII in 1991 [c9037]. Private plaintiffs can still file these claims in court whatever the EEOC chooses to pursue [c124].

State and city rules now carry the specific obligations, and each one asks for something different:

  • **New York City, Local Law 144.** An annual independent bias audit, a published summary of the results, and candidate notice at least 10 business days before the tool is used [c9029].
  • **California, Civil Rights Council ADS regulations.** No named audit, but disparate-impact liability reaches employers and vendors alike, and automated-decision records must be kept for four years [c128][c9034].
  • **Colorado, SB 26-189.** Pre-use disclosure and a human-review pathway for adverse decisions, effective January 1, 2027, currently stayed [c129][c9028].
  • **Connecticut, PA 26-15.** Disclosure, with anti-bias testing weighed by courts as a mitigating factor rather than mandated [c130].

Four jurisdictions, four mandates, one shared benchmark: the four-fifths threshold sits underneath most of them, which makes it the working compliance floor for any employer hiring across state lines [c132].

Shrink the Gap Without Losing Validity wrote 251 words, was asked for 225 +12% used 9 facts · 4 of them numbers · 4 sources
What this section was asked to do: Close by converting the article's evidence into the levers the reader controls. Show that fixed cutoffs are what turn a score gap into a four-fifths violation, name the design changes that shrink the composite gap while holding validity, and state what a defensible file contains: validation evidence, thresholds set before candidates are seen, documented rationale and quarterly pass-rate monitoring.
What the writer actually wrote

A fixed cutoff score is what turns a score gap into a four-fifths rule violation, and the cutoff is yours to set [c53]. The test produces the difference; the way you apply it decides whether that difference shows up as a pass-rate disparity. That makes adverse impact a property of test design and use, not a permanent flaw in the measurement [c154].

Three design changes shrink the composite gap while holding prediction. Banding treats scores inside a defined range as equivalent instead of ranking every point difference, which softens the effect of small score gaps at the cutoff [c150]. Compensatory batteries do more: Sackett and colleagues found in 2001 that multimethod systems mixing cognitive and non-cognitive predictors produce lower group differences than cognitive tests alone, with no loss in predicting job performance [c151]. Lower reading-demand formats help least. Figural reasoning tests show d = 1.02 against d = 1.08 for verbal reasoning, a modest gain the authors themselves flag as small [c152].

None of that is defensible without a file behind it. Set the threshold in writing before you open the role, since a cutoff chosen after seeing results undercuts the whole process [c350]. Keep the validity study for comparable roles, the documented business rationale for every test and cutoff, and quarterly pass-rate comparisons across groups [c315] [c153]. In our SHRM Labs survey with Testlify, 44.5% of HR and talent acquisition practitioners said nobody formally validates their assessments [c8011]. That file is the difference.
