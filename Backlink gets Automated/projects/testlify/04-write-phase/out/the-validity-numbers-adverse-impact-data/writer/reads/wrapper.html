Adverse Impact vs Validity: The Numbers by Test Type

# Adverse Impact vs Validity: The Numbers by Test Type

The assessment method that predicts job performance best is also the one that rejects Black candidates at the highest rate. That pattern is what a disparate impact claim is built on: one group passing a screen at a much lower rate than another. Dropping the test and interviewing instead looks like the safe move, and it is not. Unstructured interviews forecast performance less than half as well, and researchers find score gaps just as wide 1. Every assessment type carries two numbers: how well it forecasts performance, and how wide its score gap runs. Put those pairs side by side and you can see which tests you can actually defend.

Quick answer

Validity and adverse impact move together. The methods that forecast performance most accurately tend to produce the widest pass-rate gaps between racial groups, and cognitive ability tests sit at that extreme. Situational judgment tests narrow the gap but predict less, and they add little on top of a cognitive test you already run. Personality measures show almost no racial gap, though they carry disability-discrimination risk instead. The gap becomes a legal problem when a group's pass rate falls below 80% of the highest group's. From there you must show the test is job-related, so the answer is a mixed battery, a cutoff set in advance, and a validation file.

The Better a Test Predicts, the More It Screens Out

Cognitive ability tests predict performance at r =.51, against r =.20 for unstructured interviews 1. That same cognitive test carries a Black-White standardized mean difference of d = 1.10, the gap in average scores measured in standard deviations 2. That is roughly three times the d = 0.38 reported for situational judgment tests 3.

Why the 2022 Sackett Reanalysis Cut the Validity Numbers

Several of the coefficients in that table have since been revised down. In 2022, Paul Sackett, Charlene Zhang, Christopher Berry, and Filip Lievens published a reanalysis in the Journal of Applied Psychology. It found that most of the benchmark figures had been overstated by.10 to.20 points 5.

The fault was mechanical, not fraudulent. Meta-analyses correct for range restriction, meaning the narrowing that happens when you only have performance data on people who were hired. That correction assumes candidates were hired using the test being scored.

But 74% to 98% of the underlying studies tested people already on the job, hired on some other basis 6. Applying the larger correction to that body of research inflated the results.

What moved, and by how much, differs sharply by method. Cognitive ability took one of the steepest falls, from Schmidt and Hunter's r =.51 in 1998 down to ρ =.31 2. Work sample tests dropped furthest, from.54 to.33 7. Structured interviews barely moved, sliding from.51 to.42 7, and empirically keyed biodata (scored life-history questionnaires) actually rose from.35 to.38 7.

That uneven movement reordered the top of the table: structured interviews now rank as the strongest single predictor, ahead of cognitive ability 6. Below that, the ordering largely held. The methods that ranked high before still rank high; they are simply less predictive than the field believed 7. Which is why every figure in this article carries its source and its year.

Cognitive Ability: Best Predictor, Widest Score Gap

Cognitive ability tests sit near the top of the validity table under either set of numbers, and carry the largest documented score gap of any assessment format. The second fact is what a compliance-accountable buyer has to price.

Validity rises with job complexity. Hunter and Hunter reported r =.56 for high-complexity jobs,.51 for medium-complexity ones, and.40 for low-complexity work 2. So the test does its heaviest lifting in the roles where a hiring mistake costs the most, and its lightest in routine work where a cheaper method may serve.

What makes cognitive testing unusual is that this evidence travels. Industrial-organizational psychologists call it validity generalization. Meta-analysis has established that validity evidence for cognitive aptitude tests holds across a broad range of jobs, rather than needing to be rebuilt role by role 8. That saves an employer the cost of a fresh local validation study for every position.

Now the other column. Roth and colleagues documented a Black-White standardized mean difference of d = 1.10 and a Hispanic-White difference of d = 0.72 on cognitive tests 2. Male-female differences sit at d = 0.00 to 0.10 2. So the exposure is racial and ethnic, not by sex. Woods concluded in 2024 that cognitive testing "continues to present a systematic adverse impact against people from particular minority ethnic backgrounds in many settings" 9.

One question decides how you defend this. Berry investigated whether the test-to-performance relationship itself differs by racial and ethnic subgroup 10. The gap sits in the scores, not in what the scores predict.

What Personality Tests Predict, and Their ADA Risk

Personality tests predict less than cognitive ability and carry a different legal problem. Conscientiousness is the trait most consistently linked to job performance across occupations, but the correlation runs only about 0.20 to 0.30 and shifts by role 11. Sackett and colleagues (2022) put work-framed conscientiousness at.25 12. Watrin and colleagues (2023) put the raw applicant figure at.14, so treat the higher number as a ceiling rather than a plausible middle 12.

The pairing is where personality earns its place. Combining general mental ability with a conscientiousness measure produces a composite validity of R =.60, better than either predictor alone 2. That makes personality a supplement to cognitive testing rather than a replacement for it 2.

The model matters too: the Big Five carries peer-reviewed validation for selection, while the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) does not 11. Read honestly, the wider record on personality and job performance is mixed.

The adverse-impact picture also looks different here. Personality measures show near-zero Black-White score gaps, and the exposure tracks the trait measured, the selection ratio, and the incentive structure instead. Advance only the top three scorers for one opening and a fourth candidate can be cut on a trivial trait difference 13. McGee and McGee (2025) add a caution: when subjects had a bonus riding on the result, racial gaps appeared that were absent on unincentivized measures 14.

The sharper risk is the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA). Personality tests can screen out candidates with mental disabilities, and a low score does not show inability to perform a job's essential functions. Vendor algorithms that match candidates against current top performers can carry that exclusion forward. Offering an alternative assessment or an opt-out is the documented accommodation 15.

Do Situational Judgment Tests Really Reduce Adverse Impact?

Situational judgment tests are sold as the way out of the cognitive score gap. They are a real compromise, but a narrower one than vendors imply. Their meta-analytic validity sits between.26 and.34, depending on the instruction format and the statistical corrections applied 16. McDaniel and colleagues, pooling 102 validity coefficients across 10,640 people, put the corrected figure at.34 17. Christian, Edwards, and Bradley later found that SJTs built for leadership and interpersonal skills sit at the top of that range 16.

What an SJT adds on top of tests you already run is small. Across studies, the incremental gain over cognitive ability alone is roughly.03 to.08 16, and the gain over the Big Five personality dimensions is similar 16. So this is a supplement, not a substitute.

The pitch rests on the smaller score gap, and two caveats decide whether it holds. The first is cognitive loading, meaning how much of the scoring key rewards raw reasoning rather than judgment. Meta-analytic work cited by De Soete identifies it as one of the largest drivers of ethnic subgroup differences on SJTs 18.

Keys built on knowledge-based instructions, asking what the best response is, correlate more strongly with cognitive ability 16. Behavioral-tendency instructions, asking what the candidate would most likely do, pull that loading down and shrink the differences with it 16.

The second caveat is who was measured. In 2019, researchers reported Black-White differences of d = 0.66 in applicant samples against d = 0.38 in current employees 19. The gap nearly doubles once real candidates sit the test.

What the Four-Fifths Rule Flags, and What You Must Prove

The score gaps in the previous sections become a legal problem through one piece of arithmetic: the four-fifths rule. The EEOC treats it as a "rule of thumb" rather than a bright-line standard 20. Divide the pass rate of each group by the pass rate of the group that passed at the highest rate. If any ratio falls below 80%, federal enforcement agencies generally regard it as evidence of adverse impact, meaning one group is rejected at a much higher rate than another 21.

A "job fit" tool that selects 30 of 60 male candidates and 15 of 45 female candidates produces rates of 50% and 33%, a ratio well under the threshold 22. That flag is a screening mechanism that invites scrutiny, not a finding of guilt 23.

Small samples are where the rule misfires. Run it on 20 candidates and a single pass or fail can swing the ratio past the threshold in either direction, so statistical significance testing is used alongside it 24.

A ratio above 80% is also not a safe harbor. Section 4D of the Uniform Guidelines states that "smaller differences in selection rate may nevertheless constitute adverse impact, where they are significant in both statistical and practical terms" 1. An employer at 78% with a non-significant test sits in a different legal position than one at 50% with a significant gap 1.

What the employer has to prove once the ratio flags

The flag shifts the burden. Once adverse impact is shown, the employer has to demonstrate that the selection procedure is job-related and consistent with business necessity 24. That standard was codified in the Civil Rights Act of 1991. It requires evidence that the abilities the test measures are actually required to do the job, which in practice means a validation study 1.

Griggs v. Duke Power Co. established that standard in 1971, and it came out of a challenge to cognitive-style testing 25. The record since then favors specificity. In U.S. v. City of New York, the courts found the fire department's 2002 and 2007 written entrance exams lacked content validity and had no defensible connection to job performance 25. General cognitive ability tests remain harder to defend on job-relatedness grounds than narrowly targeted skills tests 25.

Executive Order 14281 Did Not Repeal Disparate Impact

Federal enforcement has pulled back, but the legal exposure has not moved. Charges kept climbing through the mid-2020s: the EEOC received more than 88,000 discrimination charges in fiscal year 2024, its third consecutive annual increase 20. Race discrimination charges alone run past 27,000 a year. Then in May 2023 the agency applied the Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures to algorithmic tools in a technical assistance document, confirming that the four-fifths rule covers AI-driven selection 2627.

That posture reversed in 2025. Executive Order 14281, signed April 23, 2025, directed federal agencies to deprioritize disparate-impact enforcement, and the EEOC stopped investigating charges resting on disparate impact alone 2028. It also pulled its AI technical-assistance documents off its website 24.

An executive order sets enforcement priorities. It cannot amend a statute. The Supreme Court recognized disparate impact in Griggs v. Duke Power Co. in 1971, and Congress wrote it into Title VII in 1991 28. Private plaintiffs can still file these claims in court whatever the EEOC chooses to pursue 20.

State and city rules now carry the obligations, and each asks for something different:

  • New York City, Local Law 144. An annual independent bias audit, a published summary of the results, and candidate notice at least 10 business days before the tool is used 29.
  • California, Civil Rights Council ADS regulations. No named audit, but disparate-impact liability reaches employers and vendors alike, and automated-decision records must be kept for four years 2430.
  • Colorado, SB 26-189. Pre-use disclosure and a human-review pathway for adverse decisions, effective January 1, 2027, currently stayed 2429.
  • Connecticut, PA 26-15. Disclosure, with anti-bias testing weighed by courts as a mitigating factor rather than mandated 24.

One benchmark sits underneath most of them: the four-fifths threshold is the working compliance floor for any employer hiring across state lines 20.

Shrink the Gap Without Losing Validity

A fixed cutoff score is what turns a score gap into a four-fifths rule violation, and the cutoff is yours to set 2. That makes adverse impact a property of test design and use, not a permanent flaw in the measurement 2.

Three design changes shrink the composite gap while holding prediction. Banding treats scores inside a defined range as equivalent instead of ranking every point difference, which softens the effect of small score gaps at the cutoff 2.

Compensatory batteries do more. Sackett and colleagues found in 2001 that multimethod systems mixing cognitive and non-cognitive predictors produce lower group differences than cognitive tests alone, with no loss in predicting job performance 2. Lower reading-demand formats help least. Figural reasoning tests show d = 1.02 against d = 1.08 for verbal reasoning, a modest gain the authors themselves flag as small 2.

None of that is defensible without a file behind it. Set the threshold in writing before you open the role, since a cutoff chosen after seeing results undercuts the whole process 31. Keep the validity study for comparable roles, the documented business rationale for every test and cutoff, and quarterly pass-rate comparisons across groups 321.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between disparate impact and disparate treatment?

Disparate treatment is intentional: you treat one group differently on purpose. Disparate impact needs no intent. A neutral rule, applied to everyone, screens out one group at a much higher rate and cannot be justified as job-related.

What is an example of disparate impact?

A neutral screen that rejects one group far more often. In Griggs v. Duke Power Co., a general intelligence test and a diploma requirement screened out Black applicants, with no evidence the abilities were needed for the job 25.

Can we rely on a vendor's validation study instead of running our own?

Sometimes. Validity evidence for cognitive tests generalizes across jobs, so a fresh local study is not always required 8. You still need a documented job analysis showing the vendor's roles match yours, plus your own pass-rate data.

Does an NYC Local Law 144 bias audit make a test legally defensible?

No. The audit is a transparency requirement: an independent check of pass rates, published, with candidate notice 29. It does not prove the test is job-related. That is what Title VII asks for once impact is shown.

How often should we check pass rates by group?

Quarterly at minimum, comparing pass rates across groups 1. Rerun the check whenever you change a cutoff, add a test, or open a high-volume role, since small samples swing the ratio easily.
