Adverse Impact vs Validity: The Numbers by Test Type

# Adverse Impact vs Validity: The Numbers by Test Type

You are about to sign off on a hiring assessment. Someone on the panel asks the question nobody wants to go first on: could this get us sued? It is a fair question, and the honest answer is uncomfortable. The methods that predict job performance best are also the ones that reject Black candidates at the highest rate. That pattern is exactly what a disparate impact claim is built on.

The instinct at that point is to drop the test and go back to interviewing. That instinct is wrong, and the numbers below show why. Every assessment carries two figures: how well it forecasts performance, and how wide a score gap it leaves between groups. Put the pairs side by side and you can see which tests you are able to defend.

Quick answer

The methods that forecast performance best tend to leave the widest pass-rate gaps between racial groups, and cognitive ability tests sit at that extreme. Situational judgment tests narrow the gap but predict less well, and they add almost nothing on top of a cognitive test you already run. Personality measures carry a different exposure altogether: disability discrimination rather than race.

The gap turns into a legal problem when one group's pass rate falls below 80% of the top group's. From there you have to prove the test is job-related. So the answer is a mixed battery, a cutoff set in advance, and a validation file you can hand to someone.

The Better a Test Predicts, the More It Screens Out

Every assessment is two numbers, and most vendors only quote you one of them. The first is validity: how closely test scores track later job performance, reported as a correlation where a higher figure means a closer match. The second is the group difference, written as d, the distance between two groups' average scores measured in standard deviations. A cognitive ability test does well on the first and badly on the second: its Black-White difference runs at d = 1.10 1, wide enough that a single cutoff can pass most of one group and a minority of another.

Why the 2022 Sackett Reanalysis Cut the Validity Numbers

Before you plan around that table, you should know that most of the validity figures in it are too high. In 2022, Paul Sackett and colleagues re-ran the meta-analyses behind those standard numbers. The benchmark coefficients had been overstated by roughly.10 to.20 points 6.

The fault was mechanical rather than dishonest. Researchers can only measure a test on people who were actually hired, which strips the low scorers out of the sample and shrinks the apparent relationship, so studies apply a correction to estimate what the link would look like across everyone who applied. The correction is legitimate. It just assumes the people in the study were hired using the test being scored, and in most of the underlying research they were not: they were already on the job, hired some other way, and the correction was doing more work than it should have been.

Cognitive ability took one of the steepest falls, from r =.51 down to a corrected.31 1. It still sits near the top of the table. It simply predicts less than a generation of HR training said it did, and that should change what you promise your hiring managers.

What Personality Tests Predict, and Their ADA Risk

Personality testing gets pitched as the low-impact alternative to cognitive testing. It is better understood as a passenger than a driver.

On its own, a conscientiousness measure predicts job performance weakly. Paired with a cognitive test, it earns its seat. The combination reaches a composite validity of R =.60, better than either measure alone 1. That is a strong case for running both. It is not a case for running personality instead, and a vendor who quotes the composite figure while selling you only the personality half is quoting the wrong number.

The risk personality tests carry is also not the one you are watching for. The exposure is not really racial. It is the Americans with Disabilities Act. A personality inventory can screen out a candidate whose scores reflect a mental health condition rather than any inability to do the work. A low score on emotional stability is not evidence that someone cannot perform a role's essential functions.

The practical fix is small and mostly administrative. Tell candidates the assessment is coming and give them a documented route to ask for an alternative. Make sure somebody on your side can approve that request without escalating it. If you cannot offer an alternative, you should not be running the test as a hard gate.

Do Situational Judgment Tests Really Reduce Adverse Impact?

Situational judgment tests are sold as the way out of the trade-off: most of the predictive power, a fraction of the gap. They are a genuine compromise, and a narrower one than the pitch suggests.

Start with what they predict. Meta-analytic validity sits between.26 and.34, depending on how the questions are framed and which corrections you accept 3. That is respectable on its own, and beside the point if you already run a cognitive test, because the gain an SJT adds on top of one is about.03 to.08 3. You are buying a second assessment, adding twenty minutes to your funnel, and getting back a rounding error.

Then the gap, which is the actual reason people buy them. The reassuring figure vendors quote usually comes from studies run on current employees. Put the same test in front of real applicants and the Black-White difference nearly doubles, from d = 0.38 to d = 0.66 4. Your candidates are applicants, not employees, so the applicant figure is the one to plan against.

None of this makes an SJT a bad instrument. It makes it a supplement, picked for the judgment it measures, rather than a way to make an adverse impact problem disappear.

What the Four-Fifths Rule Flags, and What You Must Prove

A score gap turns into a legal matter through one piece of arithmetic. Take the pass rate for each group. Divide it by the pass rate of whichever group passed at the highest rate, and look at the ratio. If any group comes in below 80% of that top group, federal enforcement agencies generally treat the result as evidence of adverse impact 7.

Here is what that looks like on a live screen. A "job fit" tool passes 30 of 60 male candidates and 15 of 45 female candidates. That is 50% against 33%, a ratio comfortably under the threshold 8. Notice what the calculation does not say. It does not say the tool is biased, and it does not say the employer did anything wrong. It says a regulator now has a reason to ask questions.

So run the check yourself, per role, once a quarter, before anyone else runs it for you. The arithmetic takes ten minutes in a spreadsheet. The gap between finding the flag yourself and having it found for you is the gap between a quiet fix and a filing.

What the employer has to prove once the ratio flags

Once adverse impact is shown, the burden moves across the table to you. You have to demonstrate that the selection procedure is job-related for the position and consistent with business necessity 9. That phrase is not rhetoric. It is the standard Congress wrote into the Civil Rights Act of 1991. Satisfying it means producing a validation study, not an opinion.

Where the rule came from tells you what it is aimed at. In 1971 the Supreme Court decided Griggs v. Duke Power Co. A general intelligence test and a high school diploma requirement screened out Black applicants. The employer could not show either was needed to do the work 10. Both requirements were perfectly neutral on their face. The effect was what mattered, and it still is.

What survives that standard is specificity. A test built around the tasks of the role you are hiring for is far easier to defend. A general reasoning test bolted onto every job in the company is not. So when you buy, ask which jobs the vendor's validation evidence covers. Then check that your own job analysis puts your role inside that set. If nobody can answer, what you have is not a defense. It is a purchase order.

Executive Order 14281 Did Not Repeal Disparate Impact

In April 2025 the federal posture changed. Executive Order 14281 told agencies to deprioritize disparate-impact enforcement, and the EEOC stopped pursuing charges resting on disparate impact alone. Read quickly, that looks like the problem going away.

It is not, and the reason is worth holding onto. An executive order sets enforcement priorities inside the executive branch. It cannot amend a statute. Disparate impact is a statute, written into Title VII by Congress in 1991 11. What changed is who is likely to come after you, not whether anyone can.

Private plaintiffs still file these claims in court. A court applies the law as Congress wrote it, not as an agency currently prioritizes it. State regulators are also moving into the space federal agencies left. Several now impose their own disclosure and audit duties on automated hiring tools, so an employer hiring across state lines is held to the strictest of them.

Which makes this the worst possible moment to stop keeping records. Enforcement priorities reverse with administrations, and litigation looks backwards at hiring you are doing right now.

Shrink the Gap Without Losing Validity

Adverse impact is not baked into a test. It is produced by how you use one, and the largest lever you hold is the cutoff score, which is yours to set.

The most effective change is to stop leaning on a single predictor. Sackett and colleagues found that batteries mixing cognitive and non-cognitive measures produce smaller group differences than cognitive tests alone. The mixed battery predicts job performance just as well 1. That is the rare trade with no downside attached. It is the argument for a two or three part assessment rather than one big test carrying every decision.

The second change costs nothing at all. Write the cutoff down before you open the role 12. A threshold picked after you have seen who cleared it is indefensible, however reasonable the number looks. You chose it knowing the outcome.

The third is a file, and it is where most employers are quietly exposed. In our own survey with SHRM Labs, 44.5% of HR and talent acquisition practitioners said nobody formally validates their assessments. Keep the validity evidence for comparable roles, a written rationale for every test and every cutoff, and quarterly pass-rate comparisons across groups. That folder is the difference between a defensible process and an expensive one.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between disparate impact and disparate treatment?

Disparate treatment is deliberate: you treat one group differently on purpose. Disparate impact needs no intent at all. A neutral rule, applied to everyone, rejects one group at a much higher rate and cannot be shown to be job-related.

What is an example of disparate impact?

A neutral screen that rejects one group far more often. In Griggs v. Duke Power Co., an intelligence test and a diploma requirement screened out Black applicants, and neither was needed to do the work 10.

Can we rely on a vendor's validation study instead of running our own?

Often, yes. Validity evidence for cognitive tests generalizes across a broad range of jobs, so a fresh local study is not always required 13. You still need a job analysis showing the vendor's roles match yours.

Does an NYC Local Law 144 bias audit make a test legally defensible?

No. The audit is a transparency requirement: an independent check of pass rates, published, with notice to candidates. It does not show the test is job-related, which is what Title VII asks of you.

How often should we check pass rates by group?

Quarterly at a minimum, and again whenever you change a cutoff, add a test, or open a high-volume role. Small samples swing the ratio easily, so recheck once real volume arrives.
