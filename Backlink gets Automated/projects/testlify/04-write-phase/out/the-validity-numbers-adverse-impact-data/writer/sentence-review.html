What the Four-Fifths Rule Flags, and What You Must Prove401 → 403 words · avg 12.2 → 11.9

Divide it by the pass rate of whichever group passed at the highest rate, and look at the ratio. If any group comes in below 80% of that top group, federal enforcement agencies generally treat the result as evidence of adverse impact [c100].

Here is what that looks like on a live screen. A "job fit" tool passes 30 of 60 male candidates and 15 of 45 female candidates. It does not say the tool is biased, and it does not say the employer did anything wrong. It says a regulator now has a reason to ask questions.

So run the check yourself, per role, once a quarter, before anyone else runs it for you. The arithmetic takes ten minutes in a spreadsheet. The gap between finding the flag yourself and having it found for you is the gap between a quiet fix and a filing.

Once adverse impact is shown, the burden moves across the table to you. You have to demonstrate that the selection procedure is job-related for the position and consistent with business necessity [c155]. That phrase is not rhetoric. Where the rule came from tells you what it is aimed at. In 1971 the Supreme Court decided Griggs v. Duke Power Co.
