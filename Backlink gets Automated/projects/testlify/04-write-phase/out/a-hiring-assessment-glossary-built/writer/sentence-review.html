Five categories of test carry validated predictive power in hiring: cognitive ability, skills or job knowledge, personality, situational judgment, and work samples [c2723]. Each catches a different way a hire goes wrong. Run two or three per role rather than all five.

| Test type | The failure it catches |
|---|---|
| Cognitive ability | Cannot pick up an unfamiliar problem |
| Skills or job knowledge | Cannot yet do the task the role is paid for |
| Work sample or simulation | Interviews well and produces poorly |
| Situational judgment | Makes the wrong call in an ordinary moment |
| Personality and behavioral | Working style will not survive the team |

Cognitive ability shows the highest standalone predictive validity across role types, so if you can only add one, add that one [c2723].

Pick the job first and the instrument second. Write down the two or three skills without which the hire fails in the first ninety days, then choose the test that measures those [c2684]. A developer gets a code review. A support hire gets a scenario about a refund that never arrived.

### The forty-minute ceiling, and what drop-off costs you

Where the test sits in the funnel decides how many people ever sit it. Put it at step two, right after the application and before anyone reads a resume [c2684]. Scores then build your shortlist instead of thinning it three weeks later.

Validity: What a Vendor Is Claiming When They Say Validated303 → 302 words · avg 14.0 → 11.7

Before
Validity is not a property the test owns. It belongs to the interpretation of the scores, and to the actions you take because of them [c2219]. The thing that will need defending is your decision to screen someone out at a given score.

The kind that settles the argument is criterion validity, which asks the blunt question. Do the people who scored higher actually do the job better? A vendor establishes it by lining scores up against real performance data: supervisor ratings, sales per hour, retention past a year.

The word validated on a pricing page does not mean any of that. A vendor can truthfully say a test has been validated in accordance with the Uniform Guidelines. One legal analysis calls that statement irrelevant at best and potentially misleading [c2224].

So ask for the technical manual, and ask for the psychologist on staff rather than the account executive. Almost nobody does. In our own survey with SHRM Labs of 128 practitioners, 50.8% had no internal check on assessment validity at all [c8012].

### Validated for whose job? The call centre problem

Unless a test has been validated for your specific job, it is not valid as to that job [c2223]. Evidence gathered on other companies, in other industries, does nothing for you.

Identical job titles are where this bites. Picture two call centre roles advertised in almost the same words. One is inbound: the agent takes the call and pulls up account data while the customer waits. The other is outbound sales, where the same person keeps dialling after nine rejections. Persistence carries the second job and barely registers in the first.

A test validated on the inbound team will not defend a rejection on the outbound one. You have replaced judgment nobody wrote down with a documented rule you cannot explain.

Reliability, and Why a Score Is a Range Not a Number122 → 120 words · avg 13.6 → 12.0

Before
Reliability asks a simpler question than validity. If the same person sat this test again next month, would they land on roughly the same score? When the answer is no, nothing else about the instrument matters. You are not measuring a person, you are measuring a mood.

Vendors report it as a coefficient that tops out at 1.00, meaning perfect consistency. There is a conventional minimum below which an estimate is too weak to build a decision on. What you want on the page is a number well clear of it [c2311].

Norm Groups: Why the Same Score Lands at Two Percentiles140 → 142 words · avg 15.6 → 12.9

Before
A raw score is the count of questions a candidate got right. A percentile rank is a position: it tells you what share of a comparison group scored at or below them.

This is where it gets expensive. On a hard test, a candidate at the 70th percentile may have answered fewer than 60% of the questions correctly [c202]. A recruiter who reads that 70 as a percentage sees a weak pass, and rejects someone who outperformed seven applicants in ten.

The comparison group moves the result as much as the answers do. A raw score of 28 out of 40 lands at the 80th percentile against general population norms, and at the 55th against graduate norms [c218]. Same person, same answers, two verdicts. Adverse Impact and the Four-Fifths Rule185 → 186 words · avg 14.2 → 12.4

Before
Adverse impact is what happens when one selection standard, applied identically to everybody, produces substantially different pass rates across demographic groups. It is a property of your outcome, not of anyone's intent, and you measure it on your own hiring data.

The four-fifths rule puts a number on it. Take the selection rate for any race, sex, or ethnic group. If it falls below four-fifths of the rate for the highest group, federal enforcement agencies will generally regard that as evidence of adverse impact [c423].

Here is the arithmetic on a scored test for entry-level engineers. The company hires 30 of 50 female candidates, a 60% rate, and 80 of 100 male candidates, an 80% rate. Divide 60 by 80 and you get 75%, under the threshold, so the rule is violated [c1569]. Nobody in that hiring loop did a single deliberate thing. The cut score did it.

Which makes the cut score your lever on this ratio. Ask any provider for pass rate data broken down by group at the score you are considering. A provider who cannot produce it has told you something.

Accommodations, and When a Test Is a Pre-Employment Medical Exam252 → 255 words · avg 17.2 → 13.6

Before
A reasonable accommodation is any change to your hiring process, or to the way the test normally runs, that lets a qualified candidate with a disability show what they can actually do. Extended time is the common one, along with a question read aloud when a screen reader will not talk to your platform.

The part teams miss is that a request rarely announces itself. A candidate never has to say disability, accommodation, or ADA. An email asking for more time to complete the pre-interview online assessment is a request [c678], and your exposure begins the moment a coordinator reads it as a scheduling question.

### When a personality test counts as a medical examination

Before you extend an offer, the ADA bars disability-related inquiries and medical examinations. That ban reaches instruments nobody on your team would file under medical. Personality tests are named directly among the things you cannot require before a conditional offer [c771].

The case to know is Karraker v. Rent-A-Center. Rent-A-Center made management candidates sit the MMPI, chose the vendor's vocational scoring protocol rather than the clinical one, and had no psychologist read the results. The court held that none of those choices mattered. However the test is scored, it hurts candidates with mental disorders, so it is best categorized as a medical examination [c799].

Read that as a placement rule. A personality instrument that can surface a mental health condition belongs after a conditional offer, run the same way for every entering candidate in that job category.

What a Proctoring Flag Is Worth, and What It Isn't202 → 197 words · avg 16.8 → 12.3

Proctoring means watching a candidate while they sit the test, and a remote system does it through the webcam, the microphone, and the screen. It looks for a second monitor, a phone, a copied question, another person in the room. What it cannot do is judge whether any of that mattered.

So a flag is evidence for a person to review, never a verdict. Automated proctoring scales best and misfires most, because an algorithm that scores suspicion produces a high volume of false positives [c479].
