A Skills Assessment Glossary for Recruiters Running Tests

What a Pre-Employment Assessment Is When You Run It

A pre-employment assessment is a validated test you send before an offer that measures whether a candidate can do the job, scored the same way for every candidate 1. It is not a quiz you attach to a job post. It is a scored step you configure, launch, and later have to explain.

The case for adding one is turnover. A 2023 Society for Human Resource Management (SHRM) study found organizations using validated pre-employment assessments cut turnover by an average of 65% against those relying on resumes and interviews alone 2. That matters most where you hire in volume, because one weak screening rule repeats across every hire.

Cost moves too. The U.S. Department of Labor estimates a bad hire costs about 30% of first-year salary, roughly $9,000 on a $30,000 hourly role that leaves inside 12 months 2. Automated scoring also surfaces top scorers without a recruiter reading every resume, so a small desk can handle a hiring push without loosening its standard.

Sorting resumes is judgment nobody audits. Running a scored test makes you the owner of a selection decision a candidate, a hiring manager, or a regulator can ask you to justify. The vendor built the test, but you defend it. Everything that follows, validity, reliability, norms, cut scores, adverse impact, accommodations, and proctoring flags, is vocabulary that exists to protect that decision.

The Five Test Types, and What Each One Predicts

That decision starts with which instrument you run. Five categories carry validated predictive power in hiring: cognitive ability, skills-based, personality, situational judgment, and work samples 3. Each one predicts a different failure. Pick two or three per role rather than running all five, because a long battery costs you candidates without adding signal 4.

Cognitive ability shows the highest standalone predictive validity across role types, so it is the one to add if you can only add one 3.

Match the Test to the Role and the Stage

Pick the job first, the test second. A test only predicts performance when it maps to what the role actually demands. A mismatch produces scores that mean nothing while still exposing you to adverse impact scrutiny 6. So write down the two or three skills without which the hire fails in the first 90 days, then choose the test that measures those 4.

Cognitive ability tests suit roles that turn on learning speed and problem-solving, like engineering and analytics. Skills assessments and work-sample tests fit hands-on work: coding, design, operations. Personality and situational judgment tests read how someone handles a real workplace situation 7. A sales role should be measured on persuasion, objection handling, and drive, not abstract math 7. High-volume frontline hiring needs fast, automated, self-serve assessments 8.

Now the stage. Place the assessment at step two, right after the application and before recruiter review, so scores build the shortlist instead of thinning it later 4. Placed too early for passive candidates it costs you top-of-funnel volume, and placed after several interview rounds it wastes recruiter hours on people who would have screened out on day one 9. For senior or specialist roles, run the first conversation first, then use a deeper assessment later to separate candidates who look equally qualified 7.

The forty-minute ceiling, and what drop-off costs you

Cap the screening stage at 40 minutes. Criteria analyzed roughly half a million tests. Completion holds around 80% up to that mark, then falls to about 60% once total testing runs past an hour 10. Every candidate who abandons the test is a candidate you never scored, so a long assessment quietly deletes your best applicants along with your worst.

Budget by stage, not in total: under 10 minutes pre-application, under 45 minutes after the recruiter screen, and under 90 minutes before a panel 8. If the role needs more depth, add a second assessment after the shortlist rather than stretching the first one.

Validity: Which of the Four Kinds a Vendor Is Claiming

Type and stage settled, the open question is whether the test predicts anything, and validity is the word a vendor makes that claim in. Validity is not a property the test owns. It belongs to the interpretation of the scores and the actions that follow from them 11. So a test is never validated in the abstract: what gets validated is the inference you draw when you screen someone out at a given score 12.

Type of validityThe question it answersHow it is establishedWhat it is evidence for in a hiring decision
Face validityDoes the test look job-related at first glance? 13Candidate and reviewer perception only 13Candidate buy-in, nothing more. A test can look job-relevant and have no measured link to who succeeds 13
Content validityDo the items cover the knowledge and skills the job requires? 14Subject matter experts review whether the items reflect the role 15; 3-5 experts rate each competency 16The job-relatedness rule the Uniform Guidelines and the EEOC require 14
Construct validityDoes it measure the trait it names, such as general intelligence or extraversion? 14Correlate it against established measures of the same trait 14Scores reflect real ability, not test-format familiarity or connection speed 17
Criterion validityDo higher scorers actually perform better? 13Correlate scores against performance data: supervisor ratings, sales per hour, retention 14The strongest demonstration a testing program can offer 14

Criterion evidence also carries a timestamp. Predictive means candidates were scored before hire and measured 6-12 months later 13. Concurrent means current employees sat the test and their known performance was checked against it 13. Concurrent is faster 18, but those employees already survived selection, so the picture narrows 13; ask which one you are being shown.

How to Check a Vendor's "Validated" Claim

The word validated on a vendor page tells you a study exists somewhere. It does not tell you the study covered your job. A vendor can truthfully say a test "has been validated in accordance with the Uniform Guidelines" and one legal analysis calls that statement "irrelevant at best and potentially misleading" 19.

So ask for the paperwork. Request the technical manual and the validation studies, and read them for criterion-related validity evidence from industries close to yours 9. If nobody can point you to a published criterion validity study for the specific things the platform claims to measure, the performance predictions are marketing copy 17.

Then read the coefficient (the correlation number linking test scores to job performance) with the right yardstick in hand. Above roughly 0.30 supports using a test to predict performance in future candidates 20. That sounds low next to a source calling 0.7 to 1 "strong and perfect" 21, and those two benchmarks are measuring different things.

In selection work, values above 0.35 are only occasionally seen, lower ones are very common, and the benefit of a 0.35 correlation can still be substantial 22. A vendor quoting 0.33 is not lowballing you. A vendor quoting 0.85 is describing something else.

Put those questions to the right person. Sales reps are often unfamiliar with the Uniform Guidelines, let alone the case law applying them 19. Ask instead for the industrial or organizational psychologist on staff, who knows whether the instruments were properly validated elsewhere and what validating for your jobs would take 19. Few teams check at all: in our own SHRM Labs and Testlify survey of 128 practitioners, 50.8% have no internal check on assessment validity.

Validated for whose job? The call centre problem

Unless a test has been validated for your specific job, it is not valid as to that job 19. Evidence gathered on other companies in other industries typically means nothing 19.

There is one exception, called transportability. Validity results for Employer A's job can transfer to Employer B's, but only with a proper underlying study of A's jobs plus a formal job analysis of B's 19. Transportability removes the criterion study. It does not remove the job analysis.

Identical job titles are the trap. Some call center roles are inbound: taking calls, answering questions, pulling up data on request 19. Others are outbound sales, needing persistence, resilience, and unscripted dialogue with an outgoing personality 19. Same title, two different jobs, and evidence gathered on one will not defend the other.

Which is why an off-the-shelf test with no applicable validity evidence may be worse, from a risk standpoint, than running no test at all 19.

Reliability, and Why a Score Is a Range Not a Number

Reliability is consistency: whether the same person, measured twice under comparable conditions, would land on roughly the same score. It sits underneath the validity evidence you just checked. If the number moves for no reason, nothing you build on it holds.

Three types show up in vendor documentation, and they are not interchangeable, so ask which one you are being quoted 2324. Test-retest confirms scores hold steady over time. Internal consistency, usually reported as Cronbach's alpha, checks that every question inside one assessment measures the same thing 17. Alternate form confirms two versions of a test give consistent scores, the mechanism behind giving different candidates different item sets.

The practical floor is.70, on a scale where 1.00 is perfect consistency, though you should look for estimates much closer to the top 23.

No score is a fact. Every score is an estimate, and the standard error of measurement (SEM) tells you how wide the band around it is 25. A candidate's observed score is their real ability plus noise from item sampling, fatigue, and chance 26.

SEM falls out of two inputs: the spread of scores in the group, and the reliability estimate.

SEM = standard deviation × √(1 − r)

With a score spread of 15 points, a reliability of.90 gives a band of about 4 points. Drop reliability to.70 and the same spread produces a band of about 8. Nearly double, from one weaker coefficient.

So a 68 and a 72 may be the same candidate on two different mornings. Within roughly one standard error of any line you draw, sorting is partly chance 27. That makes where you put the line a decision, not a discovery.

Norm Groups: Why the Same Score Lands at Two Percentiles

Before you place that line, check what the number on it means. A percentile is not a percentage, and mixing the two is the most common scoring error in pre-employment assessment 28.

A raw score is the count of questions a candidate answered correctly: 24 right out of 30 is a raw score of 24 29. A percentile rank is a position, telling you what share of a comparison group scored at or below that candidate 30. Norming is the process that converts the first into the second.

The provider gives the test to a large sample, and that sample becomes the norm group. It maps the distribution of their scores, then places each new candidate's raw score onto it 29. The raw score itself never appears in the percentile output 28.

That is why reading 70 as "answered 70% correctly" costs you people. On a hard test, a candidate at the 70th percentile may have answered fewer than 60% of the questions right 28. Treat the percentile as a percentage and you reject candidates who cleared the bar you meant to set 28.

The group behind the number moves it just as much. A raw score of 28 out of 40 lands at the 80th percentile against general population norms and at the 55th against graduate norms 29. Same answers, different verdict.

So the useful question is never what a candidate scored, but scored relative to whom. Confirm which norm group produced a percentile before it decides anything 28.

Cut Score: How to Set the Line You Screen On

A cut score is the threshold that splits candidates into pass and fail, and it is decided by people rather than found in the data 27. A test contains no natural dividing line between competent and not competent 27. Cut-off score, cutoff score, passing score, and cut point all name the same setting 27.

That makes it the most consequential thing you configure. A candidate one mark below the line is treated differently from one at the line, even though their performance is almost identical 27. Set it too high and you screen out people you needed. Set it too low and the assessment stops doing any work at all 31.

Two habits get in the way. The first is the round number: 70% passes, below 70% fails, because that is what school looked like. That convention is not legally defensible and does not meet international standards 32.

Difficulty decides where a line belongs. A cut set without accounting for how hard the items are says nothing about what it is screening for 3334.

The second habit is the universal score used everywhere. A single fixed percentile applied across every role over-screens entry-level jobs and under-screens senior ones at the same time 28. Generic cut-offs are also hard to defend, because you cannot show one number is job-related and necessary for every position 35.

So start with volume, not with the score. Deciding the threshold before you know how many people will sit the test runs the logic backwards 31.

If you need 10 hires and expect 80 candidates, a cut at the 90th percentile passes roughly eight people, before drop-off, interview rejections, and candidates who take other offers 31. The process is broken before anyone applies. Work from pool size and hire count to a pass rate, then to a score.

Then calibrate against your own people. Pull 90-day performance data on current employees and find the percentile range where your strong performers cluster. If they sit between the 72nd and 88th percentile on an assessment, the evidence points to a cut near the 70th, not an arbitrary 75th written into policy 28.

Then track 30, 60, and 90-day outcomes for hires made above and below the line. If people who cleared the threshold outperform those who did not, it is validated. If the link is weak, move it 28. Roles change, so revisit the number instead of inheriting it 35.

Adverse Impact and the Four-Fifths Rule

Adverse impact happens when identical selection standards produce substantially different pass rates across demographic groups. It is a property of the outcome, not of anyone's motive. You measure it on your own selection data rather than judge it from the content of the test.

A cut score can screen out candidates from a particular group without anyone intending it to 31. Under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the result is what the law looks at 36.

The four-fifths rule puts a number on that result. It comes from the Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures, issued in 1978. A selection rate for any race, sex, or ethnic group that falls below four-fifths of the rate for the highest group will generally be regarded by federal enforcement agencies as evidence of adverse impact 3637.

Run it on your own numbers in three steps. Divide each group's selections by that group's total candidates. Take the highest rate as your reference. Divide every other rate by it.

Here is the arithmetic on a scored test for entry-level engineers. The organization hires 30 of 50 female candidates, a 60% rate, and 80 of 100 male candidates, an 80% rate. Dividing 60 by 80 gives 75%, under the threshold, so the rule is violated 15. Absent a valid defense, that is adverse impact 15.

The 80% figure is a rule of thumb, not a legal definition 15. A smaller gap can still count where it is significant in both statistical and practical terms 37. A larger gap may not count where it rests on small numbers with no statistical significance 37. So the ratio is a first check, never a verdict.

Clearing or failing it triggers the same question anyway: is the selection procedure job-related and consistent with business necessity? That phrase came from the Supreme Court in Griggs v. Duke Power (1971) and was written into the Civil Rights Act of 1991 19. A test that adversely impacts a group is discriminatory under the Uniform Guidelines unless it has been validated 37.

The cut score from the last section is the lever on this ratio. The higher the line, the greater the adverse impact tends to be 31. So ask any provider for pass rate data broken down by demographic group at the threshold you are considering 31. A provider who cannot produce it has told you something 31.

Accommodations, and When a Test Is a Pre-Employment Medical Exam

Group-level fairness is one exposure. The other arrives one candidate at a time. A reasonable accommodation is any change to your hiring process, or to the way the test is normally run, that lets a qualified candidate with a disability show their actual ability 38. The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) requires a testing entity to run its exam so that scores reflect the skill being measured rather than the candidate's impairment 39.

The Job Accommodation Network handles each situation case by case, because the right method depends on the candidate's limitations and the type of test 40. In practice the requests cluster: extended time on an online assessment, questions read aloud when a screen reader will not work with your platform, interview questions sent in advance with written answers permitted 41. The accommodation has to be effective, not preferred. A recording of the test directions is acceptable even when the candidate wanted a live reader 42.

Two limits. You do not have to grant something that creates an undue hardship, meaning significant difficulty or expense, and offering a workable alternative instead is not a denial 43. You are also only required to accommodate disabilities you know about 38.

Recognizing the request matters most: the candidate never has to say disability, accommodation, or ADA 41. "I need more time to complete the pre-interview online assessment; is there a way to extend the time?" is a request. Dismissing an imperfect request instead of talking it through is where the legal exposure starts. You may tell candidates what the process involves and ask whether they will need an accommodation for it, but they have to start the conversation 41.

When a personality test counts as a medical examination

Before you extend a job offer, the ADA bars all disability-related inquiries and medical exams, including ones related to the job 44. That ban reaches instruments you might not file under "medical." A medical examination is any procedure or test that seeks information about a person's physical or mental impairments or health 45. Personality tests are named directly among the things you cannot require pre-offer 46.

The EEOC uses seven factors to decide whether a test crosses the line. Among them: whether it is designed to reveal an impairment, whether a health care professional interprets it, and whether it measures physiological responses 47. Any one of them can be enough on its own 48.

Karraker v. Rent-A-Center (7th Cir. 2005) is the case to know. Rent-A-Center required candidates for management to sit the MMPI, chose the vendor's vocational scoring protocol rather than the clinical one, and had no psychologist read the results 47. The court held that none of that mattered. However the test is scored, it hurts candidates with mental disorders, so it is best categorized as a medical examination 47.

So an instrument that can surface a mental health condition belongs after a conditional offer. There you may run it, as long as every entering candidate in that job category takes the same one 40.

The risk is not limited to candidates who have a disability. In April 2025 the Seventh Circuit recognized that employers can face damages for violating the ADA's inquiry and exam limits even when the person involved is not disabled 46. Fitness and agility tests stay clear of the line, provided they do not measure heart rate, blood pressure, or any other physiological response 45.

What a Proctoring Flag Is Worth, and What It Isn't

One control is left to configure: the one watching while the candidate sits the test. A flag is evidence for a person to review, never a verdict 49. Proctoring (called invigilation in the UK) means monitoring candidates during an assessment to deter cheating. Remote systems do it through the webcam, the microphone, and the screen 50.

The software confirms identity, then watches for a second screen, a phone, a copied question, or another person in the room 51. What it cannot do is judge whether any of that mattered.

Three modes exist, and they differ in cost more than in kind. Live proctoring puts a real person on the webcam for the whole session, able to interrupt or end the test 52. It buys the highest apparent security at the highest price, and it is still open to human error and bias 52.

Recorded proctoring captures the session and sends it to a reviewer afterward 52. Automated proctoring hands the watching to an algorithm that scores suspicion and writes an incident report 52. That last one scales best and misfires most, because heavy reliance on artificial intelligence produces a high volume of false positives 52.

A false positive is a flag raised by behavior that correlates with cheating but is ordinary test-taking 53. Looking away to think, muttering while reading, a family member crossing the frame, a connection dropping: all of these trigger flags 54.

The University of Louisville studied roughly 357 students and found candidates with darker skin tones were significantly more likely to be marked as needing review. Women with the darkest skin tones were flagged far more often than anyone else 55. For technical roles the noise gets worse still, since copy-paste, tab-switching, and a second monitor are how engineers work 49.

So match the strictness to what the assessment gates. Light monitoring fits an early screen; stricter proctoring fits a final round that decides the hire 51. Only 26% of candidates trust artificial intelligence to assess them fairly, so rules written for a testing center push away people who were never going to cheat 49.

Write the flag review policy before launch: who looks at a flag, within what window, and what threshold justifies action 49. Decide it later and you will decide it under pressure, on a real candidate.
