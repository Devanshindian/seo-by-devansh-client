A Skills Assessment Glossary for Recruiters Running Tests

# A Skills Assessment Glossary for Recruiters Running Tests

You pick the skills assessment, send it to forty applicants, and by Friday you are staring at a column of numbers that decides who gets a phone call. Nobody handed you the vocabulary that arrived with it. Validity, norm group, cut score, adverse impact: those words were written by psychologists for other psychologists. Now they are settings on your screen.

Getting one of them slightly wrong is expensive, and it is quiet. A recruiter reads a percentile as a percentage, decides 70 looks like a weak pass, and rejects a candidate who outscored seven applicants in ten.

Quick answer

A skills assessment is a scored test you configure, launch, and later have to defend. Match the test type to the two or three abilities the role turns on, and put it right after the application. Ask a vendor which validity study covers your job, not whether one exists.

Then set the pass mark from your own pool size and your own performance data. Check the pass rates it produces across demographic groups before it goes live.

What a Skills Assessment Is When You Run It

A skills assessment is a test you send before an offer, to find out whether someone can actually do the job. Every candidate is marked the same way. That sameness is what lets you explain a rejection six months later.

Teams add one because resume sorting is judgment nobody audits. A recruiter skims sixty applications on a Tuesday afternoon and forms an opinion, and there is no record of what she weighed. Organizations that put a validated assessment in front of that step cut turnover by an average of 65% against teams relying on resumes and interviews alone 1. Turnover is what moves, because the test catches the mismatch before the hire rather than six months into it.

The Five Test Types, and What Each One Predicts

Five categories of test carry validated predictive power in hiring: cognitive ability, skills or job knowledge, personality, situational judgment, and work samples 2. Each catches a different way a hire goes wrong. Run two or three per role rather than all five.

Test typeThe failure it catches
Cognitive abilityCannot pick up an unfamiliar problem
Skills or job knowledgeCannot yet do the task the role is paid for
Work sample or simulationInterviews well and produces poorly
Situational judgmentMakes the wrong call in an ordinary moment
Personality and behavioralWorking style will not survive the team

Cognitive ability shows the highest standalone predictive validity across role types, so if you can only add one, add that one 2.

Pick the job first and the instrument second. Write down the two or three skills without which the hire fails in the first ninety days, then choose the test that measures those 3. A developer gets a code review. A support hire gets a scenario about a refund that never arrived.

The forty-minute ceiling, and what drop-off costs you

Where the test sits in the funnel decides how many people ever sit it. Put it at step two, right after the application and before anyone reads a resume 3. Scores then build your shortlist instead of thinning it three weeks later.

Then watch the clock. Criteria analyzed roughly half a million assessments. Completion holds near 80% up to about forty minutes, then falls to around 60% once total testing runs past an hour 4. Every candidate who abandons the test is one you never scored, and the people with somewhere else to be are the ones holding competing offers.

So cap the screening stage at forty minutes. If the role needs more depth, add a second short assessment after the shortlist.

Validity: What a Vendor Is Claiming When They Say Validated

Validity is not a property the test owns. It belongs to the interpretation of the scores, and to the actions you take because of them 5. The thing that will need defending is your decision to screen someone out at a given score.

The kind that settles the argument is criterion validity, which asks the blunt question. Do the people who scored higher actually do the job better? A vendor establishes it by lining scores up against real performance data: supervisor ratings, sales per hour, retention past a year.

The word validated on a pricing page does not mean any of that. A vendor can truthfully say a test has been validated in accordance with the Uniform Guidelines. One legal analysis calls that statement irrelevant at best and potentially misleading 6.

So ask for the technical manual, and ask for the psychologist on staff rather than the account executive. Almost nobody does. In our own survey with SHRM Labs of 128 practitioners, 50.8% had no internal check on assessment validity at all.

Validated for whose job? The call centre problem

Unless a test has been validated for your specific job, it is not valid as to that job 6. Evidence gathered on other companies, in other industries, does nothing for you.

Identical job titles are where this bites. Picture two call centre roles advertised in almost the same words. One is inbound: the agent takes the call and pulls up account data while the customer waits. The other is outbound sales, where the same person keeps dialling after nine rejections. Persistence carries the second job and barely registers in the first.

A test validated on the inbound team will not defend a rejection on the outbound one. You have replaced judgment nobody wrote down with a documented rule you cannot explain.

Reliability, and Why a Score Is a Range Not a Number

Reliability asks a simpler question than validity. If the same person sat this test again next month, would they land on roughly the same score? When the answer is no, nothing else about the instrument matters. You are not measuring a person, you are measuring a mood.

Vendors report it as a coefficient that tops out at 1.00, meaning perfect consistency. There is a conventional minimum below which an estimate is too weak to build a decision on. What you want on the page is a number well clear of it 7.

So a score is a range, not a point. A 68 and a 72 can be the same candidate on two different mornings, one of them after a bad night.

Norm Groups: Why the Same Score Lands at Two Percentiles

A raw score is the count of questions a candidate got right. A percentile rank is a position: it tells you what share of a comparison group scored at or below them.

This is where it gets expensive. On a hard test, a candidate at the 70th percentile may have answered fewer than 60% of the questions correctly 8. A recruiter who reads that 70 as a percentage sees a weak pass, and rejects someone who outperformed seven applicants in ten.

The comparison group moves the result as much as the answers do. A raw score of 28 out of 40 lands at the 80th percentile against general population norms, and at the 55th against graduate norms 9. Same person, same answers, two verdicts. So the useful question is never what somebody scored, but what they scored relative to whom.

Cut Score: How to Set the Line You Screen On

A cut score is the line that splits pass from fail. It is set by a person rather than discovered in the data, because no test contains a natural border between competent and not competent.

Start from volume instead. Say you need 10 hires and expect 80 candidates. A cut at the 90th percentile passes roughly eight people 10, before drop-off, before interview rejections, before the two who take other offers. Work backwards: pool size, hire count, the pass rate that leaves a workable shortlist, and only then a score.

Then calibrate against people you already trust. Pull ninety-day performance data on your current team and find where the strong ones cluster. If they sit between the 72nd and 88th percentile, the evidence points to a cut near the 70th 8. Not the arbitrary 75th somebody wrote into the policy three years ago.

Adverse Impact and the Four-Fifths Rule

Adverse impact is what happens when one selection standard, applied identically to everybody, produces substantially different pass rates across demographic groups. It is a property of your outcome, not of anyone's intent, and you measure it on your own hiring data.

The four-fifths rule puts a number on it. Take the selection rate for any race, sex, or ethnic group. If it falls below four-fifths of the rate for the highest group, federal enforcement agencies will generally regard that as evidence of adverse impact 11.

Here is the arithmetic on a scored test for entry-level engineers. The company hires 30 of 50 female candidates, a 60% rate, and 80 of 100 male candidates, an 80% rate. Divide 60 by 80 and you get 75%, under the threshold, so the rule is violated 12. Nobody in that hiring loop did a single deliberate thing. The cut score did it.

Which makes the cut score your lever on this ratio. Ask any provider for pass rate data broken down by group at the score you are considering. A provider who cannot produce it has told you something.

Accommodations, and When a Test Is a Pre-Employment Medical Exam

A reasonable accommodation is any change to your hiring process, or to the way the test normally runs, that lets a qualified candidate with a disability show what they can actually do. Extended time is the common one, along with a question read aloud when a screen reader will not talk to your platform.

The part teams miss is that a request rarely announces itself. A candidate never has to say disability, accommodation, or ADA. An email asking for more time to complete the pre-interview online assessment is a request, and your exposure begins the moment a coordinator reads it as a scheduling question.

When a personality test counts as a medical examination

Before you extend an offer, the ADA bars disability-related inquiries and medical examinations. That ban reaches instruments nobody on your team would file under medical. Personality tests are named directly among the things you cannot require before a conditional offer 13.

The case to know is Karraker v. Rent-A-Center. Rent-A-Center made management candidates sit the MMPI, chose the vendor's vocational scoring protocol rather than the clinical one, and had no psychologist read the results. The court held that none of those choices mattered. However the test is scored, it hurts candidates with mental disorders, so it is best categorized as a medical examination 14.

Read that as a placement rule. A personality instrument that can surface a mental health condition belongs after a conditional offer, run the same way for every entering candidate in that job category.

What a Proctoring Flag Is Worth, and What It Isn't

Proctoring means watching a candidate while they sit the test, and a remote system does it through the webcam, the microphone, and the screen. It looks for a second monitor, a phone, a copied question, another person in the room. What it cannot do is judge whether any of that mattered.

So a flag is evidence for a person to review, never a verdict. Automated proctoring scales best and misfires most, because an algorithm that scores suspicion produces a high volume of false positives 15. Looking away to think, a housemate crossing the frame, a connection dropping for eight seconds: all of it trips the same wire.

The misfires are not spread evenly either. The University of Louisville studied roughly 357 students. Candidates with darker skin tones were significantly more likely to be marked as needing review, and women with the darkest skin tones were flagged far more often than anyone else 16. Act on flags without a human reading them and that becomes your rejection pattern.

So write the review policy before launch: who looks at a flag, inside what window, and what justifies acting on one. Decide it later and you will decide it under pressure, on a live candidate.

Frequently asked questions

What is involved in a skills assessment?

Three things. A short timed test of the abilities the role needs, automatic scoring against a comparison group, and a pass mark you set in advance. Keep the whole thing under forty minutes 4.

Can you give me an example of a skills assessment?

A support candidate cleaning and sorting a messy spreadsheet in ten minutes. A developer reviewing a pull request. A written scenario asking how they would answer an angry customer.

What is the difference between a skills assessment and an aptitude test?

A skills test measures what someone can already do, like Excel or SQL. An aptitude or cognitive test measures how fast they learn something new 2. Use aptitude for trainees, skills for defined roles.

Can a candidate retake a skills assessment they failed?

Only if the retake rule is written down and applied to everybody identically. Set a waiting period and use a different set of questions, because a second sitting on the same items measures memory.

Do I have to tell candidates the assessment is being recorded?

Yes, and before they start: what is watched, what is stored, and for how long. Illinois regulates biometric data, and New York City requires notice for automated hiring tools.
