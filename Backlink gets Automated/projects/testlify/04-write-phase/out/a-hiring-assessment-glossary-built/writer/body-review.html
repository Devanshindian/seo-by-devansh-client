What a Pre-Employment Assessment Is When You Run It wrote 251 words, was asked for 180 +39% used 3 facts · 2 of them numbers · 2 sources
What this section was asked to do: Define a pre-employment assessment from the seat of the person who administers it, then price what adding one changes: turnover, screening cost at volume, and the fact that the recruiter now owns a selection decision they can be asked to justify. That last point sets up every psychometric and legal term in the rest of the article, because each of them exists to protect that decision.
What the writer actually wrote

A pre-employment assessment is a validated test you send before an offer that measures whether a candidate can do the job, scored the same way for every candidate [c2630]. From your seat it is not a quiz you attach to a job post. It is a scored step you configure, launch, and later have to explain.

The case for adding one is turnover. A 2023 Society for Human Resource Management (SHRM) study found organizations using validated pre-employment assessments cut turnover by an average of 65% against those relying on resumes and interviews alone [c1922]. That matters most where you hire in volume, because the same weak screening rule repeats across every hire in the pool.

Cost moves too. The U.S. Department of Labor estimates a bad hire costs about 30% of first-year salary, roughly $9,000 on a $30,000 hourly role that leaves inside 12 months [c1921]. Automated scoring also surfaces top scorers without a recruiter reading every resume, so a small desk can handle a hiring push without loosening its standard.

Then comes the part nobody warns you about. Sorting resumes is judgment nobody audits. Running a scored test makes you the owner of a selection decision a candidate, a hiring manager, or a regulator can ask you to justify. The vendor built the test, but you defend it. Everything that follows, validity, reliability, norms, cut scores, adverse impact, accommodations, and proctoring flags, is vocabulary that exists to protect that decision.

The Five Test Types, and What Each One Predicts wrote 278 words, was asked for 216 +29% used 5 facts · 5 of them numbers · 5 sources
What this section was asked to do: Separate the five test types the reader will be asked to choose between, and fix what each measures, which roles it suits and how long it runs, in a table they can hold open during a vendor call. Everything later in the article, from validity through to proctoring, gets applied to whichever type is chosen here.
What the writer actually wrote

Five categories carry validated predictive power in hiring: cognitive ability, skills-based, personality, situational judgment, and work samples [c2723]. Each one predicts a different failure. Pick two or three per role rather than running all five, because a long battery costs you candidates without adding signal [c2683]. Keep the combined sitting under 40 minutes, where completion holds near 80% and drops toward 60% past an hour [c9001].

| Test type | What it measures | Best-fit roles | Typical candidate time | |---|---|---|---| | Cognitive ability | Reasoning, problem-solving, learning speed [c2723] | Any role needing judgment or adaptability; trainees, early-career, operations [c2723] [c2648] | 8-12 min [c2648] | | Skills / job knowledge | Job-specific technical or functional ability: coding, Excel, writing, typing [c2637] | Defined-skill roles: data entry, customer support, technical [c2648] | 10-15 min [c2648] | | Work sample or job simulation | Actual task performance on a real deliverable: code review, case study, writing exercise [c2723] | High-stakes, hard-to-observe, senior or specialist roles [c2723] | - | | Situational judgment test (SJT) | Decision-making under realistic role scenarios [c2723] | Call center, hospitality, retail, management [c2648] | 8-15 min [c2648] | | Personality and behavioral | Work style, reliability, service orientation, team fit [c2648] | Retail, sales, frontline; retention-sensitive roles [c2648] | 5-10 min [c2648] |

Match the Test to the Role and the Stage wrote 405 words, was asked for 396 +2% used 10 facts · 6 of them numbers · 6 sources
What this section was asked to do: Settle which test goes with which role, and at which point in the funnel it fires, then cap the time budget with the completion data. This resolves the catalogue-first mistake the type table could otherwise invite: the job comes first and the product list second. It also gives the reader the stage and length settings every later section assumes.
What the writer actually wrote

Pick the job first, the test second. A test only predicts performance when it maps to what the role actually demands, and a mismatch produces scores that mean nothing while still exposing you to adverse impact scrutiny (when a hiring step rejects one group at a much higher rate than another) [c2740]. So write down the two or three skills without which the hire fails in the first 90 days, then choose the test that measures those [c2684].

The pairings follow from that analysis. Cognitive ability tests suit roles that turn on learning speed and problem-solving, like engineering and analytics. Skills and work-sample tests fit hands-on work: coding, design, operations. Personality and situational judgment tests read how someone handles a real workplace situation [c2714]. A sales role should be measured on persuasion, objection handling, and drive, not abstract math [c2712].

Volume decides the shape as much as the role does. High-volume frontline hiring needs fast, automated, self-serve assessments; low-volume specialist roles can afford a richer work sample with longer per-candidate review [c1931].

Now the stage. Place the assessment at step two, right after the application and before recruiter review, so scores build the shortlist instead of thinning it later [c2684]. An assessment dropped in after phone screens adds a step rather than saving one [c2680]. Placed too early for passive candidates it costs you top-of-funnel volume, and placed after several interview rounds it wastes recruiter hours on people who would have screened out on day one [c2296]. For senior or specialist roles, run the first conversation first, then use a deeper assessment later to separate candidates who look equally qualified [c2704].

Cap the screening stage at 40 minutes. Criteria analyzed roughly half a million tests and found completion holds around 80% up to that mark, then falls to about 60% once total testing runs past an hour [c2457]. Every candidate who abandons the test is a candidate you never scored, so a long assessment quietly deletes your best applicants along with your worst.

Budget by stage, not in total: under 10 minutes pre-application, under 45 minutes after the recruiter screen, and under 90 minutes before a panel [c1911]. If the role needs more depth, add a second assessment after the shortlist rather than stretching the first one. That protects the completion rate the ranking depends on.

Validity: Which of the Four Kinds a Vendor Is Claiming wrote 360 words, was asked for 252 +43% used 18 facts · 11 of them numbers · 8 sources
What this section was asked to do: Define validity, then separate content, construct, criterion and face validity so the reader can tell which one a claim is made of, and which timing (predictive or concurrent) the evidence came from. Rules out the common misreading that validity is a property the test owns rather than of the inference drawn from a score. Hands the next section its yardstick.
What the writer actually wrote

Validity is not a property the test owns. It belongs to the interpretation of the scores and the actions that follow from them [c2219]. So a test is never validated in the abstract: what gets validated is the inference you draw when you screen someone out at a given score [c1313]. That distinction decides what "validated" on a vendor page is actually worth, because the four kinds below do not promise the same thing.

Read the table by starting at the last column. That is the one that tells you what a claim buys you at the moment you reject a candidate.

| Type of validity | The question it answers | How it is established | What it is evidence for in a hiring decision | |---|---|---|---| | Face validity | Does the test look job-related at first glance? [c1222] | Candidate and reviewer perception only [c1222] | Candidate buy-in, nothing more. A test can look job-relevant and have no measured link to who succeeds [c1221] | | Content validity | Do the items cover the knowledge and skills the job requires? [c12] | Subject matter experts review whether the items reflect the role [c1601]; 3-5 experts rate each competency [c2569] | The job-relatedness rule the Uniform Guidelines and the EEOC require [c14] | | Construct validity | Does it measure the trait it names, such as general intelligence or extraversion? [c4] | Correlate it against established measures of the same trait [c6] | Scores reflect real ability, not test-format familiarity or connection speed [c2333] | | Criterion validity | Do higher scorers actually perform better? [c1228] | Correlate scores against performance data: supervisor ratings, sales per hour, retention [c21] | The strongest demonstration a testing program can offer [c22] |

Criterion evidence also carries a timestamp. Predictive means candidates were scored before hire and measured 6-12 months later [c1234]. Concurrent means current employees sat the test and their known performance was checked against it [c1237]. Concurrent is faster [c1238], but those employees already survived selection, so the picture narrows [c1239]. Ask which one you are being shown.

How to Check a Vendor's "Validated" Claim wrote 538 words, was asked for 396 +36% used 16 facts · 16 of them numbers · 7 sources
What this section was asked to do: Turn the previous section's definitions into a buying test. Name what the word validated hides on a vendor page, fix which coefficient values mean anything and which yardstick a vendor is using, then rule out evidence built on somebody else's job. The reader leaves with the questions to put to a vendor and the person to put them to.
What the writer actually wrote

The word *validated* on a vendor page tells you a study exists somewhere. It does not tell you the study covered your job, and that gap is where recruiters most often assume they have bought something they have not. A vendor can truthfully say a test "has been validated in accordance with the Uniform Guidelines" and one legal analysis calls that statement "irrelevant at best and potentially misleading" [c2224]. Popularity is no substitute either. A tool can be easy to administer and good-looking without meeting a serious validation standard [c1372].

So ask for the paperwork. Request the technical manual and the validation studies, and read them for criterion-related validity evidence from industries close to yours [c2014]. If nobody can point you to a published criterion validity study for the specific things the platform claims to measure, the performance predictions are marketing copy [c1369].

Then read the coefficient (the correlation number linking test scores to job performance) with the right yardstick in hand. Above roughly 0.30 supports using a test to predict performance in future candidates [c1387]. That sounds low next to a source calling 0.7 to 1 "strong and perfect" [c1141], and those two benchmarks are measuring different things. In selection work, values above 0.35 are only occasionally seen, lower ones are very common, and the benefit of a 0.35 correlation can still be substantial [c1388]. A vendor quoting 0.33 is not lowballing you. A vendor quoting 0.85 is describing something else.

Put those questions to the right person. Sales reps are often unfamiliar with the Uniform Guidelines, let alone the case law applying them [c1361]. Ask instead for the industrial or organizational psychologist on staff, who knows whether the instruments were properly validated elsewhere and what validating for your jobs would take [c2012]. This matters more than most teams treat it: in our own SHRM Labs and Testlify survey of 128 practitioners, 50.8% have no internal check on assessment validity at all [c8012].

Unless a test has been validated for your specific job, it is not valid as to that job [c2223]. Evidence from other companies in other industries, filling positions that may or may not resemble yours, typically means nothing [c1864].

There is one exception, called transportability. Validity results for Employer A's job can transfer to Employer B's, but only with a proper underlying study of A's jobs plus a formal job analysis of B's [c1865]. Transportability removes the criterion study. It does not remove the job analysis.

Identical job titles are the trap. Some call center roles are inbound: taking calls, answering questions, pulling up data on request [c1869]. Others are outbound sales, needing persistence, resilience, and unscripted dialogue with an outgoing personality [c1359]. Same title, two different jobs, and evidence gathered on one will not defend the other.

One more reason to do this work yourself: practitioners who deal with the legal side will tell you the burden of proving a test is job-related sits with the employer, never with the vendor who sold it. An off-the-shelf test with no applicable validity evidence may be worse, from a risk standpoint, than running no test at all [c1368].

Reliability, and Why a Score Is a Range Not a Number wrote 352 words, was asked for 252 +40% used 11 facts · 8 of them numbers · 6 sources
What this section was asked to do: Define reliability and standard error of measurement, and establish that a reported score is a band rather than a fact. This is the section that makes the cut score section possible: once the reader accepts that candidates within one standard error of any line are sorted partly by chance, the placement of that line becomes a decision they have to make deliberately.
What the writer actually wrote

Reliability is consistency: whether the same person, measured twice under comparable conditions, would land on roughly the same score. A candidate who sits an assessment twice two weeks apart and gets radically different results is being measured by a tool that cannot be trusted [c2307]. That is why reliability comes before validity. If the number moves for no reason, nothing you build on it holds.

It is not one number. The types you will meet in vendor documentation are test-retest reliability, internal consistency reliability, and alternate form reliability [c2309]. Test-retest confirms scores hold steady over time. Internal consistency, usually reported as Cronbach's alpha, checks that every question inside one assessment is measuring the same thing [c2310]. Alternate form reliability confirms two versions of a test give consistent scores, which is the mechanism behind giving different candidates different item sets. Ask which one a vendor is quoting, because they are not interchangeable [c1382].

The practical floor is .70, on a scale where 1.00 is perfect consistency, though the advice is to look for estimates much closer to the top [c2311].

Now the part that changes how you read a result. No score is a fact. Every score is an estimate, and the standard error of measurement (SEM) tells you how wide the band around it is [c112]. A candidate's observed score is their real ability plus noise from item sampling, fatigue, and chance [c356].

SEM falls out of two inputs: the spread of scores in the group, and the reliability estimate.

**SEM = standard deviation × √(1 − r)** [c118]

With a score spread of 15 points, a reliability of .90 gives a band of about 4 points. Drop reliability to .70 and the same spread produces a band of about 8 [c120][c121]. Nearly double, from one weaker coefficient.

So a 68 and a 72 may be the same candidate on two different mornings. Within roughly one standard error of any line you draw, sorting is partly chance [c358]. That makes where you put the line a decision, not a discovery.

Norm Groups: Why the Same Score Lands at Two Percentiles wrote 259 words, was asked for 252 +3% used 11 facts · 9 of them numbers · 3 sources
What this section was asked to do: Define norming, raw score, percentile and norm group, and kill the percentile-as-percentage error that inflates rejections. Show the same raw score landing at two different percentiles depending on the group behind it, so the reader checks which comparison group produced a number before acting on it. Feeds directly into where the cut goes.
What the writer actually wrote

A percentile is not a percentage, and mixing the two is the most common scoring error in pre-employment assessment [c201]. A raw score is the count of questions a candidate answered correctly: 24 right out of 30 is a raw score of 24 [c238]. A percentile rank is a position, telling you what share of a comparison group scored at or below that candidate [c354]. Norming is the process that converts the first into the second.

Here is how the conversion happens. The provider gives the test to a large sample, and that sample becomes the norm group [c193]. It maps the distribution of their scores, then places each new candidate's raw score onto it [c194][c195]. The raw score itself never appears in the percentile output [c199].

That is why reading 70 as "answered 70% correctly" costs you people. On a hard test, a candidate at the 70th percentile may have answered fewer than 60% of the questions right [c202]. Treat the percentile as a percentage and you reject candidates who cleared the bar you meant to set [c204].

The group behind the number moves it just as much. A raw score of 28 out of 40 lands at the 80th percentile against general population norms and at the 55th against graduate norms [c218]. Same answers, different verdict. So the useful question is never what a candidate scored, but scored relative to whom, and you should confirm which norm group produced a percentile before it decides anything [c223].

Cut Score: How to Set the Line You Screen On wrote 503 words, was asked for 468 +7% used 18 facts · 18 of them numbers · 7 sources
What this section was asked to do: Define the cut score, then rule out the two ways recruiters usually set one: the inherited round number and the universal score applied across every role. Replace both with a method the reader can run, working back from applicant volume to a pass rate and then calibrating the line against their own ninety-day performance data.
What the writer actually wrote

A cut score is the threshold that splits candidates into pass and fail, and it is decided by people rather than found in the data [c284][c288]. A test contains no natural dividing line between competent and not competent [c289]. You draw that line, through a defined process, for a purpose you can state out loud. Cut-off score, cutoff score, passing score, and cut point all name the same setting [c287].

That makes it the most consequential thing you configure. A candidate one mark below the line is treated differently from one at the line, even though their performance is almost identical [c285]. Set it too high and you screen out people you needed. Set it too low and the assessment stops doing any work at all [c1457].

Two habits get in the way. The first is the round number: 70% passes, below 70% fails, because that is what school looked like. That convention is not legally defensible and does not meet international standards [c1452]. Difficulty decides where a line belongs, and a cut set without accounting for how hard the items are carries no information about what it is screening for [c1454][c1455].

The second is the universal score used everywhere. A single fixed percentile applied across every role over-screens entry-level jobs and under-screens senior ones at the same time [c259]. Generic cut-offs are also hard to defend, because you cannot show one number is job-related and necessary for every position [c1458]. The higher you set a line, the more confident you need to be that the test measures something the role genuinely requires [c2463].

Now the method. Start with volume, not with the score. Deciding the threshold before you know how many people will sit the test runs the logic backwards [c1543]. If you need 10 hires and expect 80 candidates, a cut at the 90th percentile passes roughly eight people, before drop-off, interview rejections, and candidates who take other offers [c1544]. The process is broken before anyone applies. Work from pool size and hire count to a pass rate, then to a score.

Then calibrate against your own people. Pull 90-day performance data on current employees and find the percentile range where your strong performers cluster. If they sit between the 72nd and 88th percentile on an assessment, the evidence points to a cut near the 70th, not an arbitrary 75th written into policy [c331]. Match the norm group to the role too: a data analyst gets compared against a data and analytics group, not a general population [c262].

Finally, check the line held. Track 30, 60, and 90-day outcomes for hires made above and below it. If people who cleared the threshold outperform those who did not, it is validated. If the link is weak, move it [c264]. Roles change, so revisit the number instead of inheriting it [c304]. And note who carries that work: the vendor supplies the test, but defending the line you set on it is yours.

Adverse Impact and the Four-Fifths Rule wrote 426 words, was asked for 396 +8% used 15 facts · 14 of them numbers · 5 sources
What this section was asked to do: Define adverse impact, run the four-fifths calculation on selection rates, and state both its limits and the job-related and business-necessity defence it triggers. Then name the cut score set two sections earlier as the lever that moves the ratio, and tell the reader exactly what pass-rate data to demand from a provider.
What the writer actually wrote

Adverse impact happens when identical selection standards produce substantially different pass rates across demographic groups [c2132]. It is a property of the outcome, not of anyone's motive, and you measure it on your own selection data rather than judge it from the content of the test. A cut score can screen out candidates from a particular group without anyone intending it to [c2060]. Under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the result is what the law looks at [c414].

The four-fifths rule puts a number on that result. The Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures, issued in 1978, say a selection rate for any race, sex, or ethnic group below four-fifths of the rate for the highest group will generally be regarded by federal enforcement agencies as evidence of adverse impact [c423][c2064]. Run it on your own numbers in three steps. Divide each group's selections by that group's total candidates. Take the highest rate as your reference. Divide every other rate by it.

Here is the arithmetic on a scored test for entry-level engineers. The organization hires 30 of 50 female candidates, a 60% rate, and 80 of 100 male candidates, an 80% rate. Dividing 60 by 80 gives 75%, under the threshold, so the rule is violated [c1569]. Absent a valid defense, that is adverse impact [c1570].

The 80% figure is a rule of thumb, not a legal definition [c1568]. A smaller gap can still count where it is significant in both statistical and practical terms [c2065]. A larger gap may not count where it rests on small numbers with no statistical significance [c2066]. So the ratio is a first check, never a verdict.

Clearing or failing it triggers the same question anyway: is the selection procedure job-related and consistent with business necessity? That phrase came from the Supreme Court in *Griggs v. Duke Power* (1971) and was written into the Civil Rights Act of 1991 [c1596]. A test that adversely impacts a group is discriminatory under the Uniform Guidelines unless it has been validated [c1560]. Practitioners will tell you the vendor's validation study is not a shield here, because defending the decision falls on the employer.

The cut score you set two sections back is the lever on this ratio. The higher the line, the greater the adverse impact tends to be [c1625]. So ask any provider for pass rate data broken down by demographic group at the threshold you are considering [c1629]. A provider who cannot produce it has told you something [c2145].

Accommodations, and When a Test Is a Pre-Employment Medical Exam wrote 684 words, was asked for 396 +73% used 20 facts · 11 of them numbers · 11 sources
What this section was asked to do: Define reasonable accommodation on a test, establish what counts as a request even when nobody says disability or ADA, and fix the limits. Then settle the timing rule most recruiters have never been told: an instrument that can surface a medical condition belongs after the conditional offer, not in the screen.
What the writer actually wrote

A reasonable accommodation is any change to your hiring process, or to the way the test is normally run, that lets a qualified candidate with a disability show their actual ability [c650]. The standard administration of your assessment is the way things are usually done: the 30-minute timer, the audio prompts, the on-screen reading, the video interview. Each of those is adjustable. The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) requires a testing entity to run its exam so that scores reflect the skill being measured rather than the candidate's impairment [c1178].

There is no standing menu you can apply to everyone and call it done. The Job Accommodation Network's guidance is that the method depends on the individual candidate's limitations and the type of test involved, so each situation is handled case by case [c1690]. In practice the requests cluster: extended time on a pre-interview online assessment, questions read aloud when a screen reader will not work with your platform, or interview questions sent in advance with written answers permitted [c714]. The accommodation has to be effective, not preferred. A recording of the test directions is acceptable even when the candidate wanted a live reader [c1092].

Two limits. You do not have to grant something that creates an undue hardship, meaning significant difficulty or expense, and offering a workable alternative instead is not a denial [c1802]. You are also only required to accommodate disabilities you know about [c668].

Which is why recognizing the request matters more than any of it. The candidate never has to say disability, accommodation, or ADA [c669]. "I need more time to complete the pre-interview online assessment; is there a way to extend the time?" is a request [c678]. So is a line about a screen reader not working with your platform. Candidates are not expected to know the right terminology, and dismissing an imperfect request instead of talking it through is where the legal exposure starts. You may tell candidates what the process involves and ask whether they will need an accommodation for it, but they have to start the conversation [c667].

Before you extend a job offer, the ADA bars all disability-related inquiries and medical exams, including ones related to the job [c1104]. That ban reaches instruments you might not file under "medical." A medical examination is any procedure or test that seeks information about a person's physical or mental impairments or health [c780]. Personality tests are named directly among the things you cannot require pre-offer [c771].

The EEOC uses seven factors to decide whether a test crosses the line, among them whether it is designed to reveal an impairment, whether a health care professional interprets it, and whether it measures physiological responses [c782]. The factor that should change how you buy: any one of them can be enough on its own [c783]. Four "no" answers out of seven do not save an instrument.

*Karraker v. Rent-A-Center* (7th Cir. 2005) is the case to know. Rent-A-Center required candidates for management to sit the MMPI, chose the vendor's vocational scoring protocol rather than the clinical one, and had no psychologist read the results [c797]. The court held that none of that mattered. However the test is scored, it hurts candidates with mental disorders, so it is best categorized as a medical examination [c799]. How you score it and who reads it does not launder the instrument.

Two practical rules follow. An instrument that can surface a mental health condition belongs after a conditional offer, where you may run it as long as every entering candidate in that job category takes the same one [c1680]. And the risk is not limited to candidates who have a disability: in April 2025 the Seventh Circuit recognized that employers can face damages for violating the ADA's inquiry and exam limits even when the person involved is not disabled [c778]. Fitness and agility tests stay clear of the line, provided they do not measure heart rate, blood pressure, or any other physiological response [c787].

What a Proctoring Flag Is Worth, and What It Isn't wrote 423 words, was asked for 396 +7% used 17 facts · 17 of them numbers · 7 sources
What this section was asked to do: Define the proctoring vocabulary the reader configures: the three modes, the flag, the false positive. Settle what a flag is worth, which is evidence for a person and never a verdict, match the strictness to what the assessment gates, and force the review policy to be written down before the first candidate sits the test.
What the writer actually wrote

A flag is evidence for a person to review, never a verdict [c2492]. Proctoring (called invigilation in the UK) means monitoring candidates during an assessment to deter cheating, and remote systems do it through the webcam, the microphone, and the screen [c1947]. The software confirms identity, then watches for a second screen, a phone, a copied question, or another person in the room [c470]. What it cannot do is judge whether any of that mattered.

Three modes exist, and they differ in cost more than in kind. Live proctoring puts a real person on the webcam for the whole session, able to interrupt or end the test [c474]. It buys the highest apparent security at the highest price, and it is still open to human error and bias [c475]. Recorded proctoring captures the session and sends it to a reviewer afterward [c476]. Automated proctoring hands the watching to an algorithm that scores suspicion and writes an incident report [c478]. That last one scales best and misfires most, because heavy reliance on artificial intelligence produces a high volume of false positives [c479].

A *false positive* is a flag raised by behavior that correlates with cheating but is ordinary test-taking [c516]. Looking away to think, muttering while reading, a family member crossing the frame, a connection dropping: all of these trigger flags [c9014]. The University of Louisville studied roughly 357 students and found candidates with darker skin tones were significantly more likely to be marked as needing review, with women who had the darkest skin tones flagged far more often than anyone else [c9007]. For technical roles the noise gets worse still, since copy-paste, tab-switching, and a second monitor are how engineers work [c2197].

So match the strictness to what the assessment gates. Light monitoring fits an early screen; stricter proctoring fits a final round that decides the hire [c510]. Over-proctoring a first screen frustrates honest people and raises drop-off, while under-proctoring a final round invites fraud [c511]. Only 26% of candidates trust artificial intelligence to assess them fairly, so rules written for a testing center exclude people who were never going to cheat [c2178].

Write the flag review policy before launch: who looks at a flag, within what window, and what threshold justifies action [c1204]. Decide it later and you will decide it under pressure, on a real candidate. Treating flags as verdicts without naming a reviewer is the most common way a proctored setup ends up strict and useless at once [c2493].
