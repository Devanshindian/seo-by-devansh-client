What a Pre-Employment Assessment Is When You Run It declared

A pre-employment assessment is a validated test you send before an offer that measures whether a candidate can do the job, scored the same way for every candidate [c2630]. From your seat it It is not a quiz you attach to a job post. It is a scored step you configure, launch, and later have to explain. The case for adding one is turnover. A 2023 Society for Human Resource Management (SHRM) study found organizations using validated pre-employment assessments cut turnover by an average of 65% against those relying on resumes and interviews alone [c1922]. That matters most where you hire in volume, because the same one weak screening rule repeats across every hire in the pool. hire. Cost moves too. The U.S. Department of Labor estimates a bad hire costs about 30% of first-year salary, roughly $9,000 on a $30,000 hourly role that leaves inside 12 months [c1921]. Automated scoring also surfaces top scorers without a recruiter reading every resume, so a small desk can handle a hiring push without loosening its standard. Then comes the part nobody warns you about. Sorting resumes is judgment nobody audits. Running a scored test makes you the owner of a selection decision a candidate, a hiring manager, or a regulator can ask you to justify. The vendor built the test, but you defend it. Everything that follows, validity, reliability, norms, cut scores, adverse impact, accommodations, and proctoring flags, is vocabulary that exists to protect that decision.

Test-retest confirms scores hold steady over time. Internal consistency, usually reported as Cronbach's alpha, checks that every question inside one assessment is measuring measures the same thing [c2310]. Before you place that line, check what the number on it means. A percentile is not a percentage, and mixing the two is the most common scoring error in pre-employment assessment [c201]. A raw score is the count of questions a candidate answered correctly: 24 right out of 30 is a raw score of 24 [c238]. A percentile rank is a position, telling you what share of a comparison group scored at or below that candidate [c354]. Norming is the process that converts the first into the second. Here is how the conversion happens. The provider gives the test to a large sample, and that sample becomes the norm group [c193]. It maps the distribution of their scores, then places each new candidate's raw score onto it [c194][c195]. The raw score itself never appears in the percentile output [c199]. That is why reading 70 as "answered 70% correctly" costs you people. On a hard test, a candidate at the 70th percentile may have answered fewer than 60% of the questions right [c202]. Treat the percentile as a percentage and you reject candidates who cleared the bar you meant to set [c204]. The group behind the number moves it just as much. A raw score of 28 out of 40 lands at the 80th percentile against general population norms and at the 55th against graduate norms [c218]. Same answers, different verdict. So the useful question is never what a candidate scored, but scored relative to whom, and you should confirm whom. Confirm which norm group produced a percentile before it decides anything [c223].

A cut score is the threshold that splits candidates into pass and fail, and it is decided by people rather than found in the data [c284][c288]. A test contains no natural dividing line between competent and not competent [c289]. You draw that line, through a defined process, for a purpose you can state out loud. Cut-off score, cutoff score, passing score, and cut point all name the same setting [c287]. That makes it the most consequential thing you configure. A candidate one mark below the line is treated differently from one at the line, even though their performance is almost identical [c285]. Set it too high and you screen out people you needed. Set it too low and the assessment stops doing any work at all [c1457]. Two habits get in the way. The first is the round number: 70% passes, below 70% fails, because that is what school looked like. That convention is not legally defensible and does not meet international standards [c1452]. Difficulty decides where a line belongs, and a belongs. A cut set without accounting for how hard the items are carries no information says nothing about what it is screening for [c1454][c1455]. The second habit is the universal score used everywhere. A single fixed percentile applied across every role over-screens entry-level jobs and under-screens senior ones at the same time [c259]. Generic cut-offs are also hard to defend, because you cannot show one number is job-related and necessary for every position [c1458]. The higher you set a line, the more confident you need to be that the test measures something the role genuinely requires [c2463]. Now the method. Start So start with volume, not with the score. Deciding the threshold before you know how many people will sit the test runs the logic backwards [c1543]. If you need 10 hires and expect 80 candidates, a cut at the 90th percentile passes roughly eight people, before drop-off, interview rejections, and candidates who take other offers [c1544]. Proctoring (called invigilation in the UK) means monitoring candidates during an assessment to deter cheating, and remote cheating. Remote systems do it through the webcam, the microphone, and the screen [c1947]. The software confirms identity, then watches for a second screen, a phone, a copied question, or another person in the room [c470]. What it cannot do is judge whether any of that mattered. Three modes exist, and they differ in cost more than in kind. Live proctoring puts a real person on the webcam for the whole session, able to interrupt or end the test [c474]. It buys the highest apparent security at the highest price, and it is still open to human error and bias [c475]. Recorded proctoring captures the session and sends it to a reviewer afterward [c476]. Automated proctoring hands the watching to an algorithm that scores suspicion and writes an incident report [c478]. That last one scales best and misfires most, because heavy reliance on artificial intelligence produces a high volume of false positives [c479]. A *false positive* is a flag raised by behavior that correlates with cheating but is ordinary test-taking [c516]. Looking away to think, muttering while reading, a family member crossing the frame, a connection dropping: all of these trigger flags [c9014]. The University of Louisville studied roughly 357 students and found candidates with darker skin tones were significantly more likely to be marked as needing review, review. Women with women who had the darkest skin tones were flagged far more often than anyone else [c9007]. For technical roles the noise gets worse still, since copy-paste, tab-switching, and a second monitor are how engineers work [c2197]. So match the strictness to what the assessment gates. Light monitoring fits an early screen; stricter proctoring fits a final round that decides the hire [c510]. Over-proctoring a first screen frustrates honest people and raises drop-off, while under-proctoring a final round invites fraud [c511]. Only 26% of candidates trust artificial intelligence to assess them fairly, so rules written for a testing center exclude push away people who were never going to cheat [c2178]. Write the flag review policy before launch: who looks at a flag, within what window, and what threshold justifies action [c1204]. Decide it later and you will decide it under pressure, on a real candidate. Treating flags as verdicts without naming a reviewer is the most common way a proctored setup ends up strict and useless at once [c2493].
