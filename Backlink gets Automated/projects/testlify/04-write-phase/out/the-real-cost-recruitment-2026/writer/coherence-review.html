Which SHRM Number Is Current, and Which Is Stale several-scales
What it did: Changed "three SHRM figures are in circulation right now, and only one of them is current" to "four SHRM editions are in circulation right now, and two of them are stale", and made the follow-on sentence read "Past those sit two accurate but incompatible current sets."
Why: The count collided with the article's own table and with the very next sentence: the table lists four editions (FY2015, April 2022, 2025, 2026) and the paragraph immediately describes TWO current sets, not one.
How to Calculate Cost Per Hire, and What It Returns numbers-disagree
What it did: Dated the $4,700 figure in place ("the 2022 number rather than the current one") and changed the closing line from "none of them is $4,700" to "none of them is the $5,475 benchmark".
Why: Quick answer says the current non-executive average is $5,475 and the older figure is stale; the next section calls $4,700 superseded. Undated here, the reader met $4,700 as the live benchmark and then measured three worked examples against a retired number.
Which SHRM Number Is Current, and Which Is Stale breaks-own-rule
What it did: Rewrote the comparison rule to turn on the statistic (median vs median, average vs average, never one against the other) and added that medians are SHRM's cross-year series [c34], which is why the next section's trend figures are all medians.
Why: As written the rule demanded the edition AND sample match too, which outlaws the article's own central claim: the intro's $8,800 to $15,000 line, the $5,000 / $8,800 / $15,000 series, the $1,600 to $1,300 series, and the 63.5 vs 67.7 time-to-fill comparison all cross editions and samples. The genuinely unsafe comparison, average against median, stays fully forbidden.
Hard Costs vs Soft Costs: Which Half Gets Invoiced / What Interview Hours, Recruiter Time and R numbers-disagree
What it did: Scoped the three-to-four-times-salary total ("well past the recruiting spend cost per hire measures", "each half of that recruiting spend"), and flagged the $62,000 figure as a narrower count that does not stack with it.
Why: Three to four times salary puts a $75,000 role at $225,000-$300,000 with soft costs at 60-70%, while the interview section prices the uninvoiced half of a $75,000 role at $62,000. Both stood unqualified, so the reader could not tell they count different things, and could have added them together.
Frequently asked questions own-warning

Why: The volume section explicitly warns "the threshold is not clean" and names those two conditions; the FAQ recommended crossing it as a flat rule with no mention of the warning. The warning wins, so the recommendation is qualified.
Why Hiring Volume Changes Your Cost Per Hire numbers-disagree

Why: Three sentences earlier the section's own 40-person example lands at $1,875, well below average, and the article attributes that to role mix. And a 50-hire employer had already appeared at $8,000 on $400,000; a second 50-hire employer at $3,500 on $175,000 read as the same company contradicting itself.
The Hard Costs You Can Point to on an Invoice numbers-disagree

Why: The list directly underneath contradicts it: job boards run $800-$1,500 a month against background checks at $20-$100, assessments at ~$50, and relocation at $21,792-$63,685.
How Much Recruitment Agencies Charge, Model by Model numbers-disagree
What it did: Removed the word "executive" from "A $200,000 executive hire runs $40,000 to $60,000" (figures untouched) and added one sentence noting the retained and C-suite rates above run higher on the same salary.
Why: $40,000-$60,000 on $200,000 is 20-30%, below the section's own retained rate (30-35%) and far below its C-suite rate (40-50%), and below the role-band table's $50,000-$250,000 executive floor at its low end. A CHRO could have budgeted $40,000 for a search the article prices at $80,000-$100,000.
