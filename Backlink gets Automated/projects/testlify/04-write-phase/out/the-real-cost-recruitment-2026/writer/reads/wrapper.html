Cost Per Hire in 2026: The Real Number, Not the 2022 One

# Cost Per Hire in 2026: The Real Number, Not the 2022 One

The cost per hire number in your budget deck is probably from 2022. The median for an executive hire has gone from $8,800 that year to $15,000 now. Hiring below that level barely moved, so one blended average is roughly right at the bottom and badly wrong at the top. The roles it gets most wrong are the expensive ones you have to defend to finance.

The current figures exist, each dated to its own edition and statistic. So does a way to rebuild the number around your own role bands and hiring volume.

Quick answer

Cost per hire is what you spent on recruiting, inside the organization and outside it, divided by the hires you made in that period. The current benchmark average for non-executive hires is $5,475, not the older figure still doing the rounds.

Treat that as a starting point, not an answer. A blended average cannot price one opening, because role band and hiring volume both pull the number hard. And the formula stops at the accepted offer, so ramp time, vacancy and onboarding never appear in it at all.

How to Calculate Cost Per Hire, and What It Returns

Cost per hire has one authoritative definition: total internal recruiting costs plus total external recruiting costs, divided by the number of hires in the same period 1. That formula comes from SHRM (Society for Human Resource Management) and ANSI (American National Standards Institute). Every benchmark in this article is built on it. Run it on US data and the most-quoted average lands near $4,700 for non-executive roles 1.

Internal costs are the ones you already pay for: recruiter salaries, hiring manager and interviewer hours, HR admin time, recruiting software, and referral bonuses 1. External costs are the ones you buy: job board postings, agency or search fees, background checks, assessments, sign-on bonuses, and relocation 1. Add both, divide by hires, and you have your cost per hire.

The arithmetic is simple. What it returns is not stable.

Take a mid-sized employer making 15 hires in a quarter, with $30,000 spent internally and $25,000 externally. That is $55,000 across 15 hires, or $3,667 each 1.

Change the inputs and the same formula moves. A small accounting firm filling five positions on $20,000 internal and $5,000 external spend lands at $5,000 per hire 2. An employer spending $300,000 internally and $100,000 externally to make 50 hires gets $8,000 3.

Three organizations, one formula, three answers, and none of them is $4,700. The average is real. It just cannot tell you what your own hiring costs, or why.

Which SHRM Number Is Current, and Which Is Stale

The average has a second problem: three SHRM figures are in circulation right now, and only one of them is current. The $4,129 everyone treats as a benchmark dates to fiscal year 2015 4. The $4,700 most decks quote comes from SHRM's April 2022 reporting, which SHRM's own 2025 release describes as superseded 5.

Past that sit two accurate but incompatible sets. The 2025 report gives averages and the 2026 brief gives medians, which SHRM uses to avoid distortion from very high or very low values 6.

That single methodological choice explains the gap that makes these numbers look broken. SHRM's 2025 average for executive hires is $35,879, while the median for roughly the same period is $10,600 5. Both are correct. Compared side by side, they are also meaningless, because an average and a median are not the same statistic.

Executive Hiring Costs Doubled, Non-Executive Barely Moved

One split inside those editions is the whole correction: executive hiring got much more expensive, and everything below it did not. SHRM's median cost-per-hire for executive roles ran $5,000 in 2017, $8,800 in 2022, and $15,000 in 2026 6. Non-executive hiring over the same span stayed close to where it started, roughly $1,600 in 2017 and $1,300 in 2026 6.

That gap is why a 2022 citation aged so unevenly. Quote the old executive figure today and you are budgeting a senior search at a little over half its current median. Quote the old non-executive figure and you are close enough that nobody in the room would challenge you.

SHRM gives its own reason for the divergence, and it is not a change in how the survey counts things. Leadership and managerial development ranked as a top CHRO priority in both 2025 and 2026. That pushed organizations to absorb higher acquisition costs for senior talent in what SHRM calls a constrained talent market 6. On the non-executive side, SHRM describes the figure as relatively stable and points to a cooling job market rather than any methodology shift 6.

So a single benchmark now carries two different error rates depending on the role band you apply it to. Near the bottom of the org chart, the average is roughly right and slowly aging. At the top, it is off by thousands per hire on the roles that draw the most scrutiny.

What Cost Per Hire Looks Like by Role Band

A single blended average cannot price a single open role. The same market that fills a frontline position for $1,500 charges $50,000 to $250,000 for a retained executive search 1. That is a spread of more than a hundredfold inside one metric.

Five things move a role up the range: seniority, the number of interview rounds, how scarce the skill is, the industry, and the role type. Senior and specialized searches take longer, pull in more interviewers, and lean harder on agencies, which raises internal recruiter hours and external spend at the same time 11. Scarcity does this independently of level, so a hard-to-fill engineer can cost more than the manager they report to.

Why Hiring Volume Changes Your Cost Per Hire

Role band is one axis. The second is arithmetic: a large share of recruiting spend is fixed, while the number of hires it gets divided across is not. Job board subscriptions, ATS licenses, and recruiter salaries cost roughly the same whether you fill five roles or 50. Divide them across more hires and cost per hire falls, with no change in what you actually spend 11.

Run it on a small employer. A 40-person organization filling eight roles in a quarter, on $6,000 of external cost and $9,000 of internal cost, lands at $1,875 per hire 10. That sits well below the SHRM average, and not because the company is efficient. It is missing the executive and specialized technical searches that pull a large enterprise's blended average up.

Change the mix and the same arithmetic runs the other way. The five-hire accounting firm from earlier comes to $5,000 per hire 2. An employer making 50 hires against $175,000 of annual recruiting spend lands at $3,500 12. Smaller organizations pay more per hire on far less total spend, because they have no dedicated recruiting team and lean harder on agencies and job board postings 13.

That is also where the in-house versus agency question gets decided. The breakeven sits near 15-20 hires a year. Below it, agencies win: their cost is purely variable, while an internal team is a fixed cost whether or not roles are open 14. Above it, building internal capacity brings the per-hire number down.

The threshold is not clean, though. It moves on whether your hiring demand is steady enough to keep a recruiter busy, and whether your roles are specialized enough that a generalist cannot reach the candidates.

One structure inverts the usual direction. Flat-rate hiring events charge a fixed price while the number of hires they produce varies, so cost per hire drops as volume climbs 15. That only holds for multi-role, hourly, and clinical hiring, where interview volume is the constraint.

Hard Costs vs Soft Costs: Which Half Gets Invoiced

Role band and volume both divide a pool of spend. That pool is itself incomplete: the invoiced half of a hire is the smaller half. Edie Goldberg, SHRM Foundation chair-elect, puts the split at 30% to 40% hard costs and the rest soft 16. So a budget built from vendor bills alone accounts for roughly a third of what the hire actually consumed.

Two different splits are in play here, and treating them as one is where budgets go wrong. The first is internal versus external, the SHRM/ANSI formula's own division: what your own organization spends 1 against what leaves the building 1.

The second split is hard versus soft, and it sorts by visibility rather than by direction. Hard costs are the line items finance can trace to an invoice or a card statement. Soft costs sit in calendars and output: interview and debrief time, the hours a stretched team loses, and the ramp before a new hire reaches full speed 17.

The two splits overlap but do not match. Most external spend is hard, yet plenty of internal spend is too, including the ATS subscription and referral bonuses that arrive as real bills.

Counted in full, the total runs three to four times the position's salary, so a $60,000 role can cost $180,000 to fill 8. The rest of this article prices each half in turn, starting with the invoiced one.

The Hard Costs You Can Point to on an Invoice

Hard costs attach to one hire and one recruitment cycle, which makes them the easiest part of the budget to forecast 17. Job board advertising is usually the most visible and smallest of them for roles that need no specialist search. Here is what each line typically runs.

  • Job board and ad spend. Posting fees run $50-$500 per posting, and a business advertising across three boards can expect $800-$1,500 a month 1318.
  • Background checks. Basic screening runs $20-$40, comprehensive checks pass $100, and each employment or education verification adds $15-$25 19.
  • Skills assessments. Pre-employment skills assessments and coding tests cost roughly $50 per candidate, with bulk subscriptions a few hundred dollars monthly 20.
  • Applicant Tracking System (ATS) and recruiting software. Subscriptions run $250-$3,000 a month depending on the platform and team size, prorated across the hires made 19.
  • Relocation and signing bonuses. Domestic relocation averages $21,792 for renters and $63,685 for homeowners; signing bonuses sit in the same closing-cost category 193.

Two of these behave differently from the rest. The ATS is a fixed subscription, so its per-hire share falls as hiring volume rises, down to about $50 a hire at reasonable volume 20. Ad spend is bought per click rather than per hire, so the same budget produces a different number of hires each quarter. That makes it a weak input to a fixed model, and worth naming as an estimate when you present the column.

How Much Recruitment Agencies Charge, Model by Model

Agency fees outweigh every line in that list. For organizations filling professional or senior roles, the published benchmark runs 15-30% of first-year salary 2122.

Where you land inside that band depends on the market, not on negotiation skill. Fees run higher in technology, financial services, and health sciences, where the talent is scarce, and higher again in New York, San Francisco, and Chicago than in smaller markets 23. The quoted range is a starting point, and role seniority and the size of the candidate pool move the number within it 24.

The engagement model matters as much as the percentage, because three different structures price the same hire three different ways. Contingency is the common arrangement for mid-level roles at 15-25% of first-year salary, payable only when someone is hired 3. You can run several firms at once and pay only whichever one makes the placement. In return you get less dedicated attention and thinner reach into candidates who are not actively looking 25.

Retained search is exclusive, and that exclusivity buys the firm's sustained time on one client. It runs around 30-35% of salary, billed in installments as the search hits its stages 25. Container search sits between the two at 25-33% of first-year compensation, with part paid upfront to secure recruiter time and the balance on placement 24. That middle option usually shows up at director and VP level, where a team wants exclusivity without funding a full retained engagement 23.

C-suite searches sit above all of this, reaching 40-50% of first-year income at the top end 23. Fewer people have run a company, so the search takes more outreach rounds and deeper assessment 24.

Converted to dollars, the arithmetic explains the role-band gap. A 20% fee on a $75,000 placement is $15,000 22. At $120,000, the 15-30% range means $18,000 to $36,000 21. A $200,000 executive hire runs $40,000 to $60,000 26, more than the entire cost of filling a junior role.

What Interview Hours, Recruiter Time and Ramp Cost You

The uninvoiced half of a hire is measurable, and on a $75,000 role it runs past $62,000 once vacancy, bad-hire risk, and onboarding are counted alongside the direct spend 13. That figure is not a mystery cost. It is calendar time, salary, and lost output, each of which your finance team already pays for under a different heading.

Start with interviews, because they consume the most senior time. Business roles now average 11.7 interviews per hire, up 36% from roughly eight in 2021 27. Technical roles average 17.6 27. In hours, that is 23.3 for a technical hire against 12.2 for a business one, nearly twice as much 27.

Every one of those hours belongs to an engineer, a manager, or a director who is not doing their own job that afternoon.

Recruiter salary is the easier line to allocate. An in-house talent acquisition specialist earning $60,000-$80,000 and handling about 40 hires a year costs roughly $2,000 per hire in salary alone, before any panel time is added 20.

That number is stable only if the workload behind it is. It is not. Applications per hire tripled between 2021 and 2024, and the average recruiter now processes 291 for every hire made 27. Talent acquisition teams are carrying that with about 14% less headcount than they had during the last hiring boom 28.

More screening hours per hire, spread across fewer recruiters, is a cost increase that never reaches an invoice. It is also mostly unmeasured. In our own topline research with SHRM Labs, 25.2% of HR and talent acquisition practitioners said they have never measured how much recruiter time goes to screening versus strategic work.

Then the hire starts, and the meter keeps running. A new hire in a professional role typically reaches full productivity three to nine months after the start date, according to research cited by the Association for Talent Development 22. Through that stretch you pay full salary for partial output 22. Leave it out of the calculation and you understate the true cost of hire by a wide margin 22.

What the SHRM Formula Leaves Out, and What Vacancies Cost

None of that ramp cost appears in the benchmark. The SHRM/ANSI formula stops at the accepted offer, so every dollar spent after the signature sits outside the number 15. Onboarding is the obvious omission, and organizations that track it do so separately 15. Ramp, vacancy, and the cost of a hire who leaves at month six are all outside the boundary too.

None of that makes the figure wrong. It makes it narrower than the budget question most people ask it to answer.

Vacancy is the largest of the excluded costs. An average professional role loses roughly $500 a day in output while it sits empty, and revenue-generating roles lose far more 1. That daily rate runs against a time-to-fill of 63.5 days at the 2026 median, down from 67.7 days in 2025 1. Do the arithmetic and the empty seat can outrun the recruiting spend it took to fill it.

Four counting errors distort the figure most often:

  • Undercounting the inputs. Organizations applying the full ANSI/SHRM method reported costs 34% higher than their own earlier self-reported numbers 1.
  • Counting only successful searches. The standard divides all recruiting spend in a period by all hires in that period 1.
  • Mismatching the periods. Annual costs measured against a quarterly hire count distort the result 10.
  • Comparing across organizations without controls. Industry, role mix, and geography all move the number 1.

The last failure is the one that costs money. Only 20% of organizations track quality of hire alongside cost per hire, according to SHRM 13. A recruiter can lower cost per hire by lowering the bar, and the number improves until the hire quits.

Cutting the Uninvoiced Half of Your Cost Per Hire

You now have a current figure, its edition, and the two things that move it: role band and hiring volume. You also have the larger half of the cost, the one that never reaches finance. That half is interview hours, recruiter time and the empty seat.

Frequently asked questions

What is the cost of hiring?

Hiring one person costs far more than recruiting them. Counted in full, the total runs three to four times the position's salary, so a $60,000 role can cost $180,000 to fill 8. Cost per hire counts only the recruiting spend.

What is a good cost per hire?

There is no universal target. A defensible figure is one segmented by role band and hiring volume, then read next to quality of hire. Only 20% of organizations track both 13. A falling number can hide worse hires.

How do you lower cost per hire without lowering hire quality?

Cut the inputs, not the bar. Bringing hiring in-house past 15-20 hires a year removes agency fees on most roles 14. Screening for skills earlier shortens interview loops, and that is where the uninvoiced hours sit.

How is cost per hire different from time to fill?

Cost per hire measures money spent per filled seat. Time to fill measures days, at a 2026 median of 63.5 1. They move together: a longer search adds recruiter hours and loses output while the seat sits empty.
