Cost per hire has one authoritative definition: total internal recruiting costs plus total external recruiting costs, divided by the number of hires in the same period [c311]. That formula comes from SHRM (Society for Human Resource Management) and ANSI (American National Standards Institute), and it is the version every Institute). Every benchmark in this article is built on. on it. Run it on current US data and the most-quoted average lands near $4,700 for non-executive roles [c311]. Internal costs are the ones you already pay for: recruiter salaries, hiring manager and interviewer hours, HR admin time, recruiting software, and referral bonuses [c311]. External costs are the ones you buy: job board postings, agency or search fees, background checks, assessments, sign-on bonuses, and relocation [c311]. Add both, divide by hires, and you have the number. your cost per hire. The arithmetic is simple. What it returns is not stable. Take a mid-sized employer making 15 hires in a quarter, with $30,000 spent internally and $25,000 externally. That is $55,000 across 15 hires, or $3,667 each [c306]. Change the inputs and the same formula moves. A small accounting firm filling five positions on $20,000 internal and $5,000 external spend lands at $5,000 per hire [c51]. An employer spending $300,000 internally and $100,000 externally to make 50 hires gets $8,000 [c49]. Three organizations, one formula, three answers, and none of them is $4,700. The average is real. It just cannot tell you what your own hiring costs, or why.

The Hard Costs You Can Point to on an Invoice declared
• deleted a repeat explanation: Hard costs were defined one section earlier as what finance can trace to an invoice. Deleted the second definition outright and opened on the pricing.
Hard costs are the hiring expenses that arrive with a price tag attached, on an invoice, a contract, or a finance report [c424]. They attach to one hire and one recruitment cycle, which makes them the easiest part of the budget to forecast [c419]. Job board advertising is usually the most visible and smallest of them for roles that need no specialist search [c63]. Here is what each line typically runs. - **Job board and ad spend.** Posting fees run $50-$500 per posting, and a business advertising across three boards can expect $800-$1,500 a month [c65][c66]. - **Background checks.** Basic screening runs $20-$40, comprehensive checks pass $100, and each employment or education verification adds $15-$25 [c83]. - **Skills assessments.** Pre-employment skills assessments and coding tests cost roughly $50 per candidate, with bulk subscriptions a few hundred dollars monthly [c85]. - **Applicant Tracking System (ATS) and recruiting software.** Subscriptions run $250-$3,000 a month depending on the platform and team size, prorated across the hires made [c88]. - **Relocation and signing bonuses.** Domestic relocation averages $21,792 for renters and $63,685 for homeowners; signing bonuses sit in the same closing-cost category [c90][c91]. Two of these behave differently from the rest. The ATS is a fixed subscription, so its per-hire share falls as hiring volume rises, down to about $50 a hire at reasonable volume [c89]. Ad spend is bought per click rather than per hire, so the same budget produces a different number of hires each quarter. That makes it a weak input to a fixed model, and worth naming as an estimate when you present the column.

None of that ramp cost appears in the benchmark. The SHRM/ANSI formula stops at the accepted offer, so every dollar spent after the signature sits outside the number [c47]. Onboarding is the obvious omission, and organizations that track it do so separately [c47]. Ramp, vacancy, and the cost of a hire who leaves at month six are all outside the boundary too. None of that makes the figure wrong. It makes it narrower than the budget question most people ask it to answer. Vacancy is the largest of the excluded costs. An average professional role loses roughly $500 a day in output while it sits empty, and revenue-generating roles lose far more [c308]. That daily rate runs against a time-to-fill of 63.5 days at the 2026 median, down from 67.7 days in 2025 [c308]. Do the arithmetic and the empty seat can outrun the recruiting spend it took to fill it. Four counting errors distort the figure most often: - **Undercounting the inputs.** Organizations applying the full ANSI/SHRM method reported costs 34% higher than their own earlier self-reported numbers [c310]. - **Counting only successful searches.** The standard divides all recruiting spend in a period by all hires in that period [c310]. - **Mismatching the periods.** Annual costs measured against a quarterly hire count distort the result [c182]. - **Comparing across organizations without controls.** Industry, role mix, and geography all move the number [c310]. The last failure is the one that costs money. Only 20% of organizations track quality of hire alongside cost per hire, according to SHRM [c297]. A recruiter can lower cost per hire by lowering the bar, and the number improves until the hire quits.
