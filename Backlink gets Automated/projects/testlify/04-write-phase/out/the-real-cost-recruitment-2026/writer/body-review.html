How to Calculate Cost Per Hire, and What It Returns wrote 248 words, was asked for 252 -2% used 4 facts · 4 of them numbers · 3 sources
What this section was asked to do: Settle the definition and the arithmetic in one pass so every later section can assume both. State the SHRM/ANSI formula, name the current ~$4,700 non-executive benchmark, then run the same formula on real inputs to show it returning $3,667, $5,000 and $8,000, which sets up the article's case that one blended average cannot answer a budget question.
What the writer actually wrote

Cost per hire has one authoritative definition: total internal recruiting costs plus total external recruiting costs, divided by the number of hires in the same period [c311]. That formula comes from SHRM (Society for Human Resource Management) and ANSI (American National Standards Institute), and it is the version every benchmark in this article is built on. Run it on current US data and the average lands near $4,700 for non-executive roles [c311].

Internal costs are the ones you already pay for: recruiter salaries, hiring manager and interviewer hours, HR admin time, recruiting software, and referral bonuses [c311]. External costs are the ones you buy: job board postings, agency or search fees, background checks, assessments, sign-on bonuses, and relocation [c311]. Add both, divide by hires, and you have the number.

The arithmetic is simple. What it returns is not stable.

Take a mid-sized employer making 15 hires in a quarter, with $30,000 spent internally and $25,000 externally. That is $55,000 across 15 hires, or $3,667 each [c306]. Change the inputs and the same formula moves. A small accounting firm filling five positions on $20,000 internal and $5,000 external spend lands at $5,000 per hire [c51]. An employer spending $300,000 internally and $100,000 externally to make 50 hires gets $8,000 [c49].

Three organizations, one formula, three answers, and none of them is $4,700. The average is real. It just cannot tell you what your own hiring costs, or why.

What the writer actually wrote

Three SHRM (Society for Human Resource Management) figures are in circulation right now, and only one of them is current. The $4,129 everyone treats as a benchmark dates to fiscal year 2015 [c10]. The $4,700 most decks quote comes from SHRM's April 2022 reporting, which SHRM's own 2025 release describes as superseded [c9004]. Past that sit two accurate but incompatible sets: the 2025 report gives averages, the 2026 brief gives medians, and SHRM says medians are used to avoid distortion from very high or very low values [c16].

That single methodological choice explains the gap that makes these numbers look broken. SHRM's 2025 average for executive hires is $35,879, while the median for roughly the same period is $10,600 [c17]. Both are correct. Compared side by side, they are also meaningless, because an average and a median are not the same statistic.

Executive Hiring Costs Doubled, Non-Executive Barely Moved wrote 319 words, was asked for 315 +1% used 4 facts · 3 of them numbers · 1 sources
What this section was asked to do: Deliver the headline correction. Show the executive median moving from $8,800 in 2022 to $15,000 in 2026 while the non-executive median stayed near flat, $1,600 in 2017 to $1,300 in 2026, and give SHRM's own reason for the split. Explains why a stale 2022 citation looked harmless for junior roles and was badly wrong for senior ones. Depends on the previous section having established these are medians.
What the writer actually wrote

The split is the whole correction: executive hiring got much more expensive, and everything below it did not. SHRM's median cost-per-hire for executive roles ran $5,000 in 2017, $8,800 in 2022, and $15,000 in 2026 [c32]. Non-executive hiring over the same span stayed close to where it started, roughly $1,600 in 2017 and $1,300 in 2026 [c189].

That gap is why a 2022 citation aged so unevenly. Quote the old executive figure today and you are budgeting a senior search at a little over half its current median. Quote the old non-executive figure and you are close enough that nobody in the room would challenge you.

SHRM gives its own reason for the divergence, and it is not a change in how the survey counts things. Leadership and managerial development ranked as a top CHRO priority in both 2025 and 2026, which pushed organizations to absorb higher acquisition costs to secure senior talent in what SHRM calls a constrained talent market [c9]. On the non-executive side, SHRM describes the figure as relatively stable and points to a cooling job market rather than any methodology shift [c6].

The practical consequence is that the flat half of the data protected the stale citation. For most of the roles a recruiting team actually fills, the number genuinely had not moved much year over year, so an article written in 2023 or 2024 quoting "SHRM's benchmark" was not obviously wrong at the time. It was only wrong where the money is largest.

So a single benchmark now carries two different error rates depending on the role band you apply it to. Near the bottom of the org chart, the average is roughly right and slowly aging. At the top, it is off by thousands per hire, and the roles it misprices are the ones a CHRO is most often asked to justify line by line.

What Cost Per Hire Looks Like by Role Band wrote 357 words, was asked for 410 -13% used 4 facts · 4 of them numbers · 3 sources
What this section was asked to do: Price the full range the blended average hides, from roughly $1,500 at entry level to $50,000-$250,000 for a retained executive search, and name what moves a role up it: seniority, interview rounds, scarce skills, industry, role type. Reconcile these market ranges against SHRM's lower medians by stating what each figure counts, so the two sets of numbers do not read as a contradiction.
What the writer actually wrote

A single blended average cannot price a single open role. The same market that fills a frontline position for $1,500 charges $50,000 to $250,000 for a retained executive search, where the fee runs 30-35% of first-year salary [c307]. That is a spread of more than a hundredfold inside one metric.

Five things move a role up the range: seniority, the number of interview rounds, how scarce the skill is, the industry, and the role type. Senior and specialized searches take longer, pull in more interviewers, and lean harder on agencies, which raises internal recruiter hours and external spend at the same time [c160]. Scarcity does this independently of level, so a hard-to-fill engineer can cost more than the manager they report to.

Why Hiring Volume Changes Your Cost Per Hire wrote 355 words, was asked for 346 +3% used 7 facts · 5 of them numbers · 7 sources
What this section was asked to do: Show the second axis the average hides: the same fixed spend divided by a different number of hires. Price a 40-person employer at $1,875 a hire and a small firm at $5,000 against an enterprise at $2,000-$3,500, explain why smaller employers pay more per hire on far less total spend, set the in-house versus agency breakeven near 15-20 hires a year, and name flat-rate hiring events as the one structure where per-hire cost falls as volume climbs.
What the writer actually wrote

The second axis is arithmetic: a large share of recruiting spend is fixed, while the number of hires it gets divided across is not. Job board subscriptions, ATS licenses, and recruiter salaries cost roughly the same whether you fill five roles or 50. Divide them across more hires and the per-hire figure falls, with no change in what you actually spend [c206].

Run it on a small employer. A 40-person organization filling eight roles in a quarter, on $6,000 of external cost and $9,000 of internal cost, lands at $1,875 per hire [c169]. That sits well below the SHRM average, and not because the company is efficient. It is filling roles that a large enterprise fills alongside executive and specialized technical searches, which is what pulls a blended average up.

Change the mix and the same arithmetic runs the other way. A small accounting firm filling five positions on $20,000 of internal time and $5,000 of external cost comes to $5,000 per hire [c170]. An employer making 50 hires against $175,000 of annual recruiting spend lands at $3,500 [c176]. Smaller organizations pay more per hire while spending far less in total, because they have no dedicated recruiting team and lean harder on agencies and job board postings to fill each role [c173].

That is also where the in-house versus agency question gets decided. The breakeven sits near 15-20 hires a year: below it, agencies win because their cost is purely variable, while an internal team is a fixed cost whether or not roles are open [c251]. Above it, building internal capacity brings the per-hire number down. The threshold is not clean, though. It moves on whether your hiring demand is steady enough to keep a recruiter busy, and whether your roles are specialized enough that a generalist cannot reach the candidates.

One structure inverts the usual direction. Flat-rate hiring events charge a fixed price while the number of hires they produce varies, so cost per hire drops as volume climbs [c178]. That only holds for multi-role, hourly, and clinical hiring, where interview volume is the constraint.

Hard Costs vs Soft Costs: Which Half Gets Invoiced wrote 264 words, was asked for 252 +5% used 6 facts · 5 of them numbers · 4 sources
What this section was asked to do: Separate the two ways of splitting the number and rule out treating them as one. Define internal versus external costs, which is the formula's own division, then hard versus soft, which is a visibility division, and land the 30-40% / 60-70% split that makes the invoiced figure less than half the real cost, up to 2-4x salary counted in full. Sets up the two sections that price each half.
What the writer actually wrote

The invoiced half of a hire is the smaller half. Edie Goldberg, SHRM Foundation chair-elect, puts the split at 30% to 40% hard costs and the rest soft [c103]. So a budget built from vendor bills alone accounts for roughly a third of what the hire actually consumed.

Two different splits are in play here, and treating them as one is where budgets go wrong. The first is internal versus external, which is the SHRM/ANSI formula's own division. Internal costs are what your own organization spends: recruiter salaries and benefits, hiring managers' interview hours, HR administrative time, recruiting software, and referral bonuses [c304]. External costs are what leaves the building: job ads, agency and search fees, background checks, assessments, sign-on bonuses, and relocation [c305].

The second split is hard versus soft, and it sorts by visibility rather than by direction. Hard costs are the line items finance can trace to an invoice or a card statement [c59]. Soft costs sit in calendars and output: interview and debrief time, the hours a stretched team loses, and the ramp before a new hire reaches full speed [c435].

The two splits overlap but do not match. Most external spend is hard, yet plenty of internal spend is too, including the ATS subscription and referral bonuses that arrive as real bills.

Counted in full, the total runs three to four times the position's salary, so a $60,000 role can cost $180,000 to fill [c56]. The next two sections price each half in turn, starting with the invoiced one.

The Hard Costs You Can Point to on an Invoice wrote 276 words, was asked for 221 +25% used 11 facts · 8 of them numbers · 6 sources
What this section was asked to do: Price the invoiced half line by line so the reader can build the hard-cost column of a budget: job board and ad spend per posting and per hire, background checks and skills assessments per candidate, an ATS amortised across hires, relocation and signing bonuses. Leaves agency fees to the next section, because that single line outweighs everything listed here.
What the writer actually wrote

Hard costs are the hiring expenses that arrive with a price tag attached, on an invoice, a contract, or a finance report [c424]. They attach to one hire and one recruitment cycle, which makes them the easiest part of the budget to forecast [c419]. Job board advertising is usually the most visible and smallest of them for roles that need no specialist search [c63]. Here is what each line typically runs.

  • **Job board and ad spend.** Posting fees run $50-$500 per posting, and a business advertising across three boards can expect $800-$1,500 a month [c65][c66].
  • **Background checks.** Basic screening runs $20-$40, comprehensive checks pass $100, and each employment or education verification adds $15-$25 [c83].
  • **Skills assessments.** Pre-employment skills assessments and coding tests cost roughly $50 per candidate, with bulk subscriptions a few hundred dollars monthly [c85].
  • **Applicant Tracking System (ATS) and recruiting software.** Subscriptions run $250-$3,000 a month depending on the platform and team size, prorated across the hires made [c88].
  • **Relocation and signing bonuses.** Domestic relocation averages $21,792 for renters and $63,685 for homeowners; signing bonuses sit in the same closing-cost category [c90][c91].

Two of these behave differently from the rest. The ATS is a fixed subscription, so its per-hire share falls as hiring volume rises, down to about $50 a hire at reasonable volume [c89]. Ad spend is bought per click rather than per hire, so the same budget produces a different number of hires each quarter. That makes it a weak input to a fixed model, and worth naming as an estimate when you present the column.

How Much Recruitment Agencies Charge, Model by Model wrote 337 words, was asked for 346 -3% used 15 facts · 15 of them numbers · 7 sources
What this section was asked to do: Price the largest external line and explain the role-band gap set out earlier. Fix the benchmark at 15-30% of first-year salary, separate contingency, container and retained engagements so the reader knows which one they are buying, take C-suite searches to 40-50% of first-year income, and convert the percentages into dollars at entry, mid and senior salary bands.
What the writer actually wrote

Agency fees are the single largest external cost for organizations filling professional or senior roles, and the published benchmark runs 15-30% of first-year salary [c151][c192]. Where you land inside that band depends on the market, not on negotiation skill. Fees run higher in technology, financial services, and health sciences, where the talent is scarce, and higher again in New York, San Francisco, and Chicago than in smaller markets [c203]. The quoted range is a starting point, and role seniority and the size of the candidate pool move the number within it [c195].

The engagement model matters as much as the percentage, because three different structures price the same hire three different ways. Contingency is the common arrangement for mid-level roles at 15-25% of first-year salary, payable only when someone is hired [c71]. You can run several firms at once under it, and pay only whichever one makes the placement, but you get less dedicated attention and thinner reach into candidates who are not actively looking [c81]. Retained search is exclusive, which is what buys the firm's sustained time on one client, and it runs around 30-35% of salary, billed in installments as the search hits its stages [c72][c82]. Container search sits between the two at 25-33% of first-year compensation, with part paid upfront to secure recruiter time and the balance on placement [c194]. That middle option usually shows up at director and VP level, where a team wants exclusivity without funding a full retained engagement [c197].

C-suite searches sit above all of this, reaching 40-50% of first-year income at the top end [c198]. Fewer people have run a company, so the search takes more outreach rounds and deeper assessment [c200].

Converted to dollars, the arithmetic explains the role-band gap. A 20% fee on a $75,000 placement is $15,000 [c191]. At $120,000, the 15-30% range means $18,000 to $36,000 [c157]. A $200,000 executive hire runs $40,000 to $60,000 [c247], more than the entire cost of filling a junior role.

What the SHRM Formula Leaves Out, and What Vacancies Cost wrote 279 words, was asked for 221 +26% used 5 facts · 4 of them numbers · 4 sources
What this section was asked to do: Name what the formula excludes by design, so the reader stops defending a number that measures less than they think. Establish that the SHRM formula stops at the accepted offer, price vacancy at roughly $500 a day against a rising median time to fill, list the counting errors that distort the figure, and close on the finding that only 20% track quality of hire alongside cost.
What the writer actually wrote

The SHRM/ANSI formula stops at the accepted offer, so every dollar spent after the signature sits outside the number [c47]. Onboarding is the obvious omission, and organizations that track it do so separately [c47]. Ramp, vacancy, and the cost of a hire who leaves at month six are all outside the boundary too. None of that makes the figure wrong. It makes it narrower than the budget question most people ask it to answer.

Vacancy is the largest of the excluded costs. An average professional role loses roughly $500 a day in output while it sits empty, and revenue-generating roles lose far more [c308]. That daily rate runs against a time-to-fill of 63.5 days at the 2026 median, down from 67.7 days in 2025 [c308]. Do the arithmetic and the empty seat can outrun the recruiting spend it took to fill it.

Four counting errors distort the figure most often:

  • **Undercounting the inputs.** Organizations applying the full ANSI/SHRM method reported costs 34% higher than their own earlier self-reported numbers [c310].
  • **Counting only successful searches.** The standard divides all recruiting spend in a period by all hires in that period [c310].
  • **Mismatching the periods.** Annual costs measured against a quarterly hire count distort the result [c182].
  • **Comparing across organizations without controls.** Industry, role mix, and geography all move the number [c310].

The last failure is the one that costs money. Only 20% of organizations track quality of hire alongside cost per hire, according to SHRM [c297]. A recruiter can lower cost per hire by lowering the bar, and the number improves until the hire quits.
