# Voices from the field, The Real Cost of Recruitment in 2026: The Numbers Behind the 2022 Figure Everyone Still Quotes

> This file is what practitioners say in public, gathered from open discussions. It is never a fact, it never carries a source tag, and it never quotes or names a person. Treat every line as a pattern to check, not a figure to cite.

**Coverage.** 9 discussion(s) read and 99 comments, from 2 on linkedin, 7 on reddit. 226 results were looked at across 3 search round(s).

## Agency fees are quoted as 20-30% and rarely negotiated down
**What they say.** Practitioners on both sides of the table treat 20% to 30% of first-year salary as the standard quote, and 25% comes up repeatedly as the middle of that band. Agency-side people are near-unanimous that a fee agreed before the search is not up for renegotiation once a candidate is chosen, and several say they would rather lose the client than cut it. Where a discount does happen, it is traded for something else: exclusivity, a batch of extra roles, a shorter rebate period, or a contract-to-hire arrangement instead of a straight placement.
**Strength.** Strong. The 20-30% band appears in four separate discussions across two platforms, with the same numbers coming from agency recruiters, in-house recruiters and small-business owners.
**Serves.** Section 6, and section 5's decision to hold agency fees back as the largest line.
**What this changes.** Present 15-25% as the published range but note that quoted deals in practice cluster at the top of it, and add the levers that actually move a fee, since a CHRO defending a budget will be asked "can we negotiate this down?"

## Job board spend is charged per click, not per hire
**What they say.** Recruiters describe job board budgets draining on clicks rather than applications, with one figure of $85 a day surfacing and several people saying their reps could not explain how the daily budget worked. The complaint is consistent: the money buys volume, not fit, so the same spend produces more applications and fewer usable ones than it did a few years ago. Several say the only response left is to set the budget as low as possible and shift effort to outbound sourcing.
**Strength.** Moderate. It appears in three separate discussions and the direction is consistent, but this is a venting-heavy community and the dollar figures are single anecdotes, not a range.
**Serves.** Section 5, on advertising spend.
**What this changes.** When pricing the advertising line, say that job board spend is a per-click cost with no guaranteed output, so the same budget buys a different number of hires each quarter. That makes it a poor input to a fixed cost-per-hire model and is worth flagging.

## Applicant volume has risen 5-10x with no rise in usable candidates
**What they say.** Recruiters report several hundred applications in the first week on a single role, and figures in the thousands for common technical roles, described as five to ten times the volume of two years ago. The same people say half or more of that volume is fake, duplicated or auto-generated, and that the share of applications that turn into an offer has fallen from roughly two percent to well under one. Nobody describes getting extra headcount to handle it, so the screening hours per hire have risen while the recruiting team stayed the same size.
**Strength.** Strong. It appears in four separate discussions, from recruiters, hiring managers and job seekers, with consistent direction even where the exact numbers differ.
**Serves.** Section 7, on the collapse in applicant-to-hire ratios inflating recruiter workload.
**What this changes.** Frame the ratio collapse as a screening-hours cost, not a funnel statistic. The soft-cost argument lands harder when the reader can see that the same recruiting headcount now processes several times the volume for the same number of hires.

## Practitioners split on whether in-house recruiting is actually cheaper
**What they say.** The volume argument runs both ways and the split is genuine. One side does the arithmetic out loud: five roles at 20% of a £60,000 salary is £60,000 in fees, which buys a full-time internal recruiter instead. The other side answers that an internal hire is a fixed cost that keeps running when hiring stops, that one generalist cannot cover marketing, engineering, legal and sales at the same depth, and that five simultaneous roles through one person creates a bottleneck. The disagreement is really about whether recruiting demand is steady or lumpy, and about whether the roles are specialised enough to need a network the internal hire will not have.
**Strength.** Strong on the disagreement itself. It runs across two discussions with multiple named positions on each side, though the LinkedIn side is public positioning by people who sell one model or the other.
**Serves.** Section 9, on the in-house versus agency breakeven.
**What this changes.** Do not present the breakeven as a clean threshold. Give the arithmetic, then name the two things that move it: whether hiring volume is steady enough to keep a fixed recruiter busy, and whether the roles are specialised enough that an internal generalist cannot fill them.

## AI screening tools cut hours but almost never cut cost per hire
**What they say.** Asked directly whether AI recruiting tools reduce hiring cost at scale, most practitioners say no. The things that work are narrow: interview transcript summaries, scheduling automation, and pre-filtering a large inbound pile down to a shortlist. Everything else is described as demos that break under real volume, and several people note that the tools still need close checking, so the saved time reappears as review time. The few claimed cost savings come with a volume floor, and one person is explicit that scheduling automation only pays off above about thirty hires a month.
**Strength.** Moderate. It appears in two discussions with many independent voices in the main one, and the direction is consistent, but it is largely one thread carrying the weight.
**Serves.** Section 4, on why the soft-cost half resists reduction, and section 9's volume argument.
**What this changes.** If the article suggests tooling as a lever on cost per hire, attach the volume condition. Automation moves hours, and hours only convert to money at enough hiring volume to redeploy or avoid headcount.

## What did not show up
- **No one names a cost-per-hire number.** Across all nine discussions, not a single practitioner cites $4,700, $4,129, $10,600 or any benchmark figure. The SHRM edition confusion the article is built on does not exist in these conversations, which means the provenance argument in sections 1 and 2 is genuinely unclaimed ground.
- **Nothing on the hard-versus-soft split.** No one describes their hiring cost as 30-40% invoiced and 60% time. Practitioners talk about individual line items and about their own hours, but never add them up or name the ratio.
- **No pricing for background checks, assessments or ATS.** ATS tools get named and called cheap or expensive, but no one gives a subscription figure, a per-check cost, or a relocation or signing bonus range. Section 5 will have to price those lines entirely from published sources.
- **Vacancy cost never quantified.** Long-open roles come up, but nobody puts a daily or weekly dollar figure on an empty seat, and nobody connects time-to-fill to lost revenue. Section 10's $500-a-day figure has no field corroboration either way.
- **Retained versus contingency barely discussed.** Contingency arrangements dominate; retained and container models are mentioned only in passing by one or two people, with no fee mechanics. Section 6's separation of the three models gets no support here.
