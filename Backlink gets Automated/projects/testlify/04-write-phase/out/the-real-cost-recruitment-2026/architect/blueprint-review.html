The facts are settled. This page is the plan for the article itself: what it argues, which sections carry it, and how long each one gets.

The spine — the one argument this article makes

Every section below exists to move this sentence forward. If a section does not serve it, it should not be here.

For a CHRO building or defending a hiring budget: the cost-per-hire figure most decks still quote is a stale edition of a SHRM number, and even the current one is a blended average that hides two axes the budget actually moves on, role band and hiring volume. This article dates every figure in circulation to its edition and statistic, then rebuilds the number from the invoiced hard costs (30-40%) and the uninvoiced soft costs (60-70%), so the reader leaves able to state a current, sourced benchmark, calculate their own segmented figure, and say exactly what it excludes.

What it chose to leave out

The architect is shown the topics every ranking page covers. It is allowed to drop any that do not serve this article. Watch for the opposite of a problem here: if it never drops anything, it has stopped judging.

None left out. Four of the five expected topics get their own section (formula in section 1, benchmarks in section 2, factors in section 4, the internal/external and hard/soft breakdown in section 6). The world — what this article is and is not about

Written in the research phase, before any searching started, and never changed since. It is one of the stated reasons the architect benches material below, so read it before judging whether a bench was right.

IS about
Cost per hire as a recruitment budgeting metric: the current public benchmark figure, the formula, and the hard costs (job ads, agency fees, tools) plus soft costs (recruiter and hiring-manager time, onboarding drag) that make it up, broken down by role type and by hiring volume rather than given as one blended average.
is NOT about
This is about the dollar cost of filling one open role and how that cost accounting varies by role and volume. It is NOT about the emotional or burnout toll on recruiters and HR teams (SHRM's wellbeing tangent, not a cost figure), NOT a vendor or product cost-stack comparison like manual vs. AI vs. a specific tool (Metaview's framing), NOT employee compensation or salary benchmarking (what you pay the person once hired, a separate number from what it costs to find them), and NOT the cost of turnover or retention as its own topic — that's a related but distinct HR metric from cost per hire, even though the two get lumped together under 'hiring costs.'

Rules and warnings it states61
The cost per hire number in your budget deck is probably from 2022.
Hiring below that level barely moved, so one blended average is roughly right at the bottom and badly wrong at the top.
The roles it gets most wrong are the expensive ones you have to defend to finance.
Treat that as a starting point, not an answer.
A blended average cannot price one opening, because role band and hiring volume both pull the number hard.
And the formula stops at the accepted offer, so ramp time, vacancy and onboarding never appear in it at all.
Add both, divide by hires, and you have your cost per hire.
The arithmetic is simple. What it returns is not stable.
The average is real. It just cannot tell you what your own hiring costs, or why.
The average has a second problem: three SHRM figures are in circulation right now, and only one of them is current.
Both are correct. Compared side by side, they are also meaningless, because an average and a median are not the same statistic.
