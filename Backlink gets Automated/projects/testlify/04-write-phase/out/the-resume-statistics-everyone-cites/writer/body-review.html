This is the article before anyone repaired it. Every section was written by its own separate AI call, and all of them ran at the same time, so no writer could see what any other one wrote. Each was handed only its own heading, the job that heading has to do, a word budget, and the fact cards for it. That is why the sections arrive reading like strangers, repeating each other and referring to things that are not there — every later step exists to fix that. What you are judging here is the raw material: did each writer use the evidence it was given, and did it write to the length the plan asked for.

Each writer is shown far more facts than it may use, and told to keep at most 3 SOLID statistics — a figure the argument leans on. Years, numbers inside names, and small numbers in passing do not count against that, and zero is an acceptable answer. So a section listing eight facts below and using two of them is the rule working, not evidence being wasted. Whether a figure is solid is a judgment, so nothing here counts it for you; the fact list under each section is where you check what was offered against what was taken.

The Six-Second Resume Scan: A 2012 Number Revised in 2018 wrote 436 words, was asked for 405 +8% used 15 facts · 12 of them numbers · 7 sources
What this section was asked to do: Opens the article by dating its most-cited figure. Establishes that the six-second scan comes from a 2012 TheLadders eye-tracking study, that the same publisher put the average at 7.4 seconds in its own 2018 follow-up, and that the retired number was still being printed as current in 2023. Sets the pattern every later section follows: name the study, name the year, name what it measured.
What the writer actually wrote

The six-second resume scan comes from a single eye-tracking study TheLadders ran in 2012, and the same publisher retired the number six years later [c85]. Its 2018 follow-up put the average initial screen at 7.4 seconds, about one second longer than the original finding [c88]. That is the whole life of the statistic: one study, one revision, one publisher.

The 2018 version was built in two stages. First it timed recruiters who did not know they were being timed, then it used eye-tracking to map where their attention landed and in what order [c87]. Thirty professional recruiters were monitored over 10 weeks, and the output was rendered as heat maps marking the parts of a page recruiters read and the parts they skipped entirely [c180][c184].

None of that stopped the retired figure. Articles were still printing six seconds as current in 2023, more than a decade after the study behind it and years after its own publisher moved the number [c178]. One explanation is simply that six reads as a better headline than 7.4, and the claim now supports a small industry of beat-the-six-second-scan challenges and templates, mostly published by sites selling resume services rather than by people who recruit [c179]. Plenty of the pages repeating it never link to a study at all [c4].

Neither report says enough for anyone to check it. The 2012 study disclosed a sample of 30 recruiters; the 2018 update does not state how many took part in the eye-tracking phase at all [c110]. The methodology never explains how recruiters were selected, how many resumes each one reviewed, how the timing was run, or which eye-tracking technology produced the heat maps [c111].

The material being screened is missing too. The job specifications and sample resumes were never published, so the seniority, industry, and requirements of the roles stay unknown [c112]. Those are exactly the factors that move screening time. Nothing was published in a peer-reviewed journal either, which rules out independent replication [c113].

Read the number literally and it strains further. A steady six or 7.4 seconds per resume implies about 10 resumes a minute, or 300 in half an hour, which is not a description of how recruiting actually runs [c187]. The figure is an average across a wide distribution, not a fixed ceiling: a clear mismatch gets rejected almost instantly, while a close-fit candidate gets a longer read [c190]. Treat it as a rough marker of screening speed, and never as evidence that anything on the page was verified [c259].

How Long Recruiters Spend on a Resume, and on Which Pass wrote 402 words, was asked for 405 -1% used 9 facts · 7 of them numbers · 4 sources
What this section was asked to do: Replaces the retired figure with what has actually been measured since. Reports Tegze's 2023 finding that review time scales with CV length and InterviewPal's 11.2-second first scan against a 1 minute 34 second median, then resolves why these do not contradict the older figures: they time different passes on different samples. Stops the reader quoting four numbers as if they were one.
What the writer actually wrote

Screening time is not one number, it is a function of how long the resume is. When Jan Tegze put three CVs of different lengths in front of 114 recruiters in 2023 and timed the reviews, the shortest averaged 17 seconds and the longest averaged 46 seconds [c5]. One reviewer spent 2 minutes and 27 seconds on the longest document [c106]. Every figure in that study sits above the six-second benchmark, and the gap between the fastest and slowest read is the finding, not the noise around it.

The same study heat-mapped where reviewers looked. Attention concentrated on the first page, with later pages only skim-read [c107]. So a longer CV buys more seconds, but almost none of them land past page one.

A second dataset pushes the first pass higher still. Recruiters reviewing candidates through InterviewPal's interface, across tech, finance, marketing, healthcare, and operations roles in Southeast Asia, averaged 11.2 seconds on the initial scan [c197]. Their median total review time per resume was 1 minute 34 seconds [c199]. The writeup attributes the longer first pass to structured review tools that show the job post and key skills beside the resume, giving recruiters more to read in one view [c198].

They measure different passes. The 1 minute 34 second median covers the full review, including the time spent checking quantified results and job titles after a candidate has already cleared the initial fit check [c123]. The sub-10-second figures describe only that first check. Quoted side by side, they look like a contradiction. Laid on a timeline, one is a subset of the other.

Sample and role type explain most of what is left. Highly regulated or technical jobs that require several qualifications take longer to screen than roles asking only for general industry experience [c122]. A study of three CVs read by 114 recruiters and a study of live reviews in five industries were never going to converge on one figure.

So the sequence recruiters actually quote runs six seconds from 2012 data, 7.4 seconds from Ladders in 2018, and 12 to 46 seconds depending on length from Tegze in 2023 [c9]. Four measurements, at least three years, one sentence. Cite whichever one matches the pass you are describing, and say which pass that is.

Where Recruiters Actually Look During Those Seconds wrote 246 words, was asked for 225 +9% used 6 facts · 6 of them numbers · 2 sources
What this section was asked to do: Turns time into attention. Breaks the scan down by where recruiter gaze actually lands, confirms the F-pattern held in the 2025 hidden eye-tracking replication, and names what stretches the read or ends it, including the 43% of rejected resumes dropped on formatting. Gives the reader the finding behind the seconds, which is the part a slide actually needs.
What the writer actually wrote

Nearly 80% of resume-review time lands on five elements: name, current title and company, previous title and company, employment dates, and education [c93]. The rest of the page gets whatever is left. Cursor-tracking data breaks that attention down further, and the split explains why some resumes hold a reader while others end the review early.

Here is where the gaze time went, section by section:

  • **Experience: 38%.** The largest single share, and the reason a buried or vague work history costs more than any other omission [c98].
  • **Summary and headline: 24%.** Almost a quarter of the read happens before the first job entry [c98].
  • **Skills: 18%.** Roughly half the weight of experience [c98].
  • **Education: 11%.** Read, but not where the decision forms [c98].
  • **Layout and design: 9%.** Design draws real attention, though not the kind a candidate wants [c98].

The 2025 eye-tracking experiment run by Jerry Lee of Wonsulting, using hidden tracking equipment worn by recruiters, found the same F-pattern reading behavior documented since 2012 [c194][c109]. That matters for anyone citing this: the pattern is not an artifact of one lab's method.

What changes the length of the read is content, not effort. Clear metric-based achievements stretched reading time by 27%, keyword matches by 19%, and a single-column layout by 14% [c207]. The read ends early for the opposite reason. Dense text blocks and inconsistent formatting accounted for 43% of rejected resumes [c208].

Who Admits to Lying on a Resume, and When They Were Asked wrote 401 words, was asked for 405 -1% used 12 facts · 12 of them numbers · 4 sources
What this section was asked to do: Prices the honest range for resume lying and dates both ends of it. Sets ResumeBuilder's 2025 44% against StandOut CV's 64.2%, shows the age skew driving the higher figure, then traces the older rates people still quote as current back to a 2021 survey and a 2022 baseline that exists only as a citation inside a 2025 report.
What the writer actually wrote

The honest range for resume lying in 2025 runs from 44% to 64%, and both ends are real. ResumeBuilder put it at 44% of job seekers admitting to a lie at some point in the hiring process [c63]. StandOut CV surveyed 2,102 U.S. adults the same year and got 64.2% [c60]. Same subject, same year, 20 points apart.

The gap comes from what each survey asked and who answered. StandOut CV's number is heavily age-skewed. It peaks at 80.4% among 18-to-25-year-olds, the highest of any age group, and drops to 46.9% among older respondents [c62]. A sample weighted toward early-career candidates will land near the top of the range no matter how careful the fielding is.

That matters for how you quote it. If you cite 64% as "workers," you are largely citing people under 26. If you cite 44%, you are citing a narrower question about the hiring process rather than the resume alone. Practitioners themselves do not share a definition here: plenty treat rounded titles and stretched dates as normal behavior, while others count any inaccuracy as a lie, and a survey question inherits that disagreement. What both surveys agree on is the most common lie, with 38% of resume liars inflating their years of experience [c18].

The older rates still quoted as current are older than they look. ResumeBuilder.com fielded a survey in July 2021, polling 1,250 U.S. adults aged 18 and over, and found that 32% admitted to lying on a resume [c40] [c39]. That figure is four years old. It is also a different question from the firm's own 2025 number, so the two are not a trend line.

Its breakdown is worth keeping, because it is dated and traceable. Respondents lied most about years of experience (46%), educational background (44%), length of time in a previous role (43%), and skills or abilities (40%) [c41] [c42] [c43] [c44].

The 55% figure is the one to drop. It appears only inside StandOut CV's 2025 report, as the retrospective baseline its own number is measured against: "up from 55% in 2022" [c52]. No independent 2022 survey with its own sample and method sits behind it [c53]. Repeating it as a standalone 2022 statistic cites a comparison point, not a study.

The UK CV Figures Come From Fraud Cases, Not a Survey wrote 243 words, was asked for 180 +35% used 4 facts · 4 of them numbers · 1 sources
What this section was asked to do: Separates the UK figures from the US ones, which most roundups blend without saying so. Shows that the 2023 UK numbers come from Cifas fraud-case data rather than a self-report survey, gives the 10% admission rate and the falling insider-fraud share, then adds the employer-side detection rates. Segments the article's central claim by region so one figure is not read as global.
What the writer actually wrote

The UK numbers most roundups fold into the US totals are not survey data at all. The 2023 figures come from Cifas, the UK fraud prevention body, and specifically from its Internal Fraud Database, which records confirmed cases reported by member organizations [c54]. That is a different measurement from asking people what they have done. A case file counts a candidate who was caught. A self-report counts a candidate willing to admit it.

The one self-report figure that does circulate is smaller than the US rates. Cifas found that about 10% of UK respondents said they knew someone who had lied on a CV, up from 8% the year before [c56]. Read that as awareness, not as an admission rate.

The case data moved the other way. False employment applications, meaning candidates using false identities or credentials to get a job, made up around 33% of insider fraud cases reported to Cifas in 2023, down from 44% in 2022 [c55]. A falling share of a fraud caseload is not a falling lying rate, though. It tells you what proportion of caught internal fraud started at the application stage.

Employers report catching more than the case files show. In the UK, 56% say they have caught a candidate lying on a resume, and 26% have run into fake references [c15]. Quote any of these three as UK figures, never as global ones.

What the writer actually wrote

Four of the most repeated resume-lying rates carry no survey year, no publisher, and no sample size, so none of them belongs in a deck. The 85% figure circulates as "85% of job seekers are dishonest," but the page it traces back to actually says 85% of hiring managers have caught a lie, a different measurement entirely [c9003]. The same list carries the claim that over 60% of candidates lie about their abilities and that roughly 33% admit to lying about their education, sourced to nothing more specific than "according to several surveys" [c9003]. A fourth figure, 25%, describes executives caught lying, not the fabricated references it is usually attached to [c9003].

Some of the undated numbers do have a real survey behind them, just not a stated year. How Many Resumes Are Now Written With AI wrote 321 words, was asked for 315 +2% used 4 facts · 4 of them numbers · 3 sources
What this section was asked to do: Opens the AI half of the article by charting adoption year by year, from a negligible 2022 base through the late-2024 tech-sector estimate to 52% of applicants in 2025. Holds the employer-side numbers back for a later section. Establishes how many resumes are AI-touched before the article turns to what that AI is being used to do.
What the writer actually wrote

Just over half of applicants now say they use artificial intelligence to write or polish their resumes. The Employ 2025 Job Seeker Nation Report puts that figure at 52% [c65]. That single number is the baseline for everything the rest of this article says about AI on a resume, so it matters where it came from and how fast it got there.

Three years ago the number was close to zero. In 2022, ChatGPT-assisted resumes made up a negligible share of applications [c146]. The tool most people now use had not been released to the public for most of that year, so there was nothing to measure.

The climb after that is steep but uneven, and the middle of it is an estimate rather than a survey. By late 2024, ResumeLab and Jobscan put AI-assisted resumes at 30-40% of applications at some tech companies [c147]. Read that carefully: it describes a slice of employers, not the whole market. It is the best available marker for that year, but it is not comparable with a national applicant survey, and stacking it against the 52% as if the two measured the same population is exactly the error this article is about.

There is one more caveat on the 52% itself. Employ's own reporting puts the 2024 equivalent higher, which means adoption did not simply rise year on year [c9012]. Neither year states a sample size, fieldwork window, or country in the coverage that carries the figure. Cite it as a 2025 self-report from applicants, not as a measured count of resumes.

Track the direction rather than the decimal. A negligible base in 2022, a tech-sector estimate in the 30-40% range by late 2024, and roughly half of applicants in 2025 is a real shift, whatever the exact rate. What none of these numbers can tell you is what the AI was asked to do.

When AI Polish Crosses Into Inventing Skills wrote 536 words, was asked for 495 +8% used 6 facts · 6 of them numbers · 4 sources
What this section was asked to do: Delivers the figure competing roundups omit entirely: 83% of candidates using AI on their resume say they used it to exaggerate or lie. Separates AI polish from AI fabrication, which the adoption numbers in the previous section cannot distinguish, and names the patterns fabricated content leaves behind. This is the section the article exists to add.
What the writer actually wrote

Among job seekers who use AI tools in their search, 83% say they have used AI to exaggerate or lie about their skills on a resume, cover letter, job application, or skills assessment [c66]. That comes from Capterra's 2024 Job Seeker AI Survey of nearly 3,000 job seekers across 12 countries [c9029]. Read the denominator carefully. The 83% sits inside the 58% who use AI at all, not inside every candidate who applies [c9029].

That distinction is the whole point of separating this figure from the adoption numbers. Counting how many resumes were touched by AI tells you nothing about what the AI was asked to do. A candidate who runs a real work history through a model to fix grammar and tighten bullets has done nothing a proofreader would not have done. A candidate who asks the same model to supply experience they never had has done something else entirely, and both land in the same adoption percentage.

Capterra's own analyst drew the line in the same place. Brian Westfall, principal HR analyst at Capterra, said most employers will not object to AI refining a resume or cover letter, and that the moment to be on guard is when AI is used to mask a skills deficiency [c9030]. It is a useful test for a talent leader writing screening policy: the question is not whether a model touched the document, but whether it invented the qualification.

Fabricated content does leave patterns, though none of them prove anything on their own. Three show up often enough to be worth naming:

  • **Uniform bullet structure.** Every line opens with an action verb, because the models producing this text were trained on action-verb bullets [c170].
  • **Round-number achievements.** Claims like reducing costs by 30% or lifting conversions by 25% recur because round percentages dominate the training data [c171].
  • **Claims the role could not support.** A model has no way to know that leading a team of 40 is implausible for an intern, so it writes the phrase anyway [c172].

Treat those as prompts to ask a better question, not as evidence. Plenty of careful human writers use action verbs and round their numbers. The recruiters closest to this problem will tell you the tell is almost never the phrasing, it is a specific claim that cannot survive a follow-up question.

Which is why the practical response has moved away from reading the page. Pick one line on the resume and ask the candidate to walk through it end to end: the metric, how it was measured, what broke along the way. A fabricated project has no second layer of detail underneath it, and a short structured conversation or a live skills task surfaces that faster than any close reading of the document.

None of this makes AI use itself a red flag. The 83% figure describes people already reaching for AI, and a large share of that group is using it the way a spellchecker gets used. What it does establish is that polish and fabrication now arrive in the same file, written in the same register, and no adoption statistic can tell you which one you are holding.

Employers Reject AI Resumes They Can't Reliably Spot wrote 421 words, was asked for 405 +4% used 7 facts · 7 of them numbers · 3 sources
What this section was asked to do: Settles what employers do about AI-written resumes and whether they can actually tell. Places the three 2025 rejection rates side by side and explains what makes them differ, then resolves the detection contradiction in one place: 74% claiming in 2025 they can spot AI content against 82% failing to spot it in 2023, with false-positive rates that undercut both claims.
What the writer actually wrote

Employers discard AI-written resumes at rates between 49% and 62%, depending entirely on what the survey asked. Resume.io's 2025 survey of 3,000 respondents found 49% of US hiring managers auto-dismiss resumes they suspect are AI-generated [c152]. Resume Now, surveying 925 people the same year, put rejection at 62%, but only for AI resumes that lack personalization [c153].

Those are not the same question. One measures suspicion of AI use; the other measures a judgment about a generic application. Read as a single number, they produce a range that means nothing. Read separately, they say employers punish the output that reads like it was written for nobody in particular.

Employers cannot reliably identify the resumes they say they are rejecting. A 2023 ResumeBuilder survey of 1,000 hiring managers found 82% could not spot a ChatGPT-written cover letter [c36]. Two years later, 74% of hiring managers say they can spot AI-generated content [c37].

Treat that as a straight two-year improvement and you are guessing. The 2023 study tested people against real writing samples and scored whether they were right. The 2025 figure records what hiring managers believe about themselves, which is a different measurement with no scoring attached. Confidence went up; nobody has published evidence that accuracy did.

The tools are worse. AI-detection software applied to resumes carries false positive rates of 30-50%, meaning it flags a large share of human-written resumes as machine-made [c155]. A candidate who generates a resume with AI and then edits the word choices, sentence structure, and personal details usually passes the same check [c156]. So the software fails in both directions at once: it accuses honest candidates and clears the ones who took thirty seconds to cover their tracks.

The stated giveaway does not hold up either. Half of hiring managers name unnatural phrasing as their main tell [c154]. Phrasing is the one thing an editing pass fixes first, and polished language now arrives regardless of how well a candidate actually communicates. The signal recruiters used to read, awkward wording, has been erased by the same tools they are trying to catch.

Which is why the people running these processes have mostly stopped trying. What you hear from recruiters is not that they have found a better detector, but that they have moved the judgment off the page: pick one claim on the resume and ask the candidate to walk it through end to end.

Who Runs a Background Check, and What a Caught Lie Costs wrote 384 words, was asked for 405 -5% used 11 facts · 11 of them numbers · 5 sources

What the writer actually wrote

Verification is now close to universal: 94% of employers run background checks, so a fabricated line has a real chance of meeting a document that contradicts it [c24][c79]. That is the answer to the question every lying statistic raises. The checking is not hypothetical, and the rate at which it turns something up is high enough to plan around.

Degree verification is narrower than the headline suggests. 53% of employers always verify a degree and another 24% do so sometimes, which leaves roughly a quarter of employers not verifying at all [c27]. Social-media checks add a second layer on top, run by 70% of employers [c25][c81]. So the exposure a candidate faces depends less on whether anyone checks than on which claim they picked to inflate.

Two separate sources agree on how often checking finds something, and they are worth keeping separate rather than averaging. HireRight's 2025 Global Benchmark Report found more than 75% of businesses turned up at least one candidate discrepancy in the previous 12 months, with education and employment history falsified most often [c265]. A different figure, one in four background checks revealing a discrepancy, circulates with no publication year attached, so it cannot be cited alongside HireRight's [c22]. Note also that a discrepancy is not automatically a lie: the most common complaint you hear from practitioners is that verification records themselves get dates wrong, and people misremember their own start months.

Confidence in catching fabrication is lower than the checking rate implies. An Equifax study of 254 HR professionals and executives found 71% had already met fake or misleading candidate details, while only 20% called themselves very confident in spotting fabricated information [c30].

The penalty lands mostly before the start date. Once a lie surfaces, 41% of offers get rescinded and 18% of people already hired are fired [c28][c82]. ResumeLab's finding sits in the same range: 65% of caught liars were either not hired or terminated [c84].

That is the number to carry into a talent review. Verification catches enough to matter, and the cost of a caught lie is paid at the offer stage far more often than after someone is on the payroll.
