The Quick answer is the whole piece in 60 to 110 words, for the reader who reads nothing else — it has to ANSWER, not list takeaways. The opening follows problem, agitate, solution, and the solution is the article's own answer, never the product. The FAQ takes real searched questions first, and it is allowed to answer things the article does not cover, because a question the article already answers under a heading is a wasted slot. Adding an opening and a closing creates two new seams, so it may also repair those two joins and nothing else.

The H1 and the intro

H1
The CV Statistics Everyone Cites That Are Out of Date
intro
Most curriculum vitae statistics travel without the one thing that makes them citable: the year they were measured. The six-second resume scan is the clearest case, a 2012 finding the publisher itself replaced with 7.4 seconds in 2018. It is still quoted as current, more than a decade on. Quote a number like that in a leadership deck, and one question about its source ends the point you were making. Every headline figure below is dated to the survey behind it, with its publisher, its sample and what it measured. The ones that trace back to no study at all are named, so you can stop using them.
quick answer
A resume statistic is only usable if you can name its publisher, its survey year, its sample and the question it asked. Most of the ones in circulation fail that test. Screening-time figures disagree because they measure different passes, not because one of them is wrong. Lying rates disagree because one survey asks about a whole career and another about a single hiring process. Several of the most repeated numbers, including the 75% applicant tracking system rejection rate, trace back to no study at all. Date every figure you quote, or drop it.

The FAQ — 5 question(s)

Can an ATS detect a ChatGPT resume? from real searches33 words
Not reliably. Applicant tracking systems store and rank resumes, they do not run detection [c129]. Bolt-on AI detectors carry 30-50% false positive rates, and a lightly edited AI resume usually passes them [c155][c156].
What are red flags on a CV? from real searches39 words
A title that does not match the responsibilities described, round-number achievements with no method behind them, and claims the role could not support [c171][c172]. None of them prove anything. Treat each as a question to ask, not a rejection.
Is it illegal to lie on a CV? the editor found this one39 words
Lying on a CV is not usually a crime by itself. It becomes fraud when it involves forged documents or false credentials in regulated roles, like medicine or law. Employers can rescind an offer or dismiss at any point.
How long should a CV be? the editor found this one36 words
Two pages at most. Longer CVs do buy more reading time, from 17 seconds to 46 in one study of 114 recruiters [c5]. But attention stays on page one, and later pages are only skimmed [c107].
How do you verify a candidate's degree? the editor found this one38 words
Contact the registrar or the National Student Clearinghouse, which covers most US institutions. Ask for dates attended and the credential awarded, not just enrollment. Only 53% of employers always verify a degree, so the gap is common [c27].

Seam repairs it made — 1 section(s)

The Six-Second Resume Scan: A 2012 Number Revised in 2018 touch-up
• fixed re-intro: The intro already introduces the six-second scan and its 2018 revision, so the section now refers back instead of announcing it again.
The That six-second resume scan figure comes from a single eye-tracking study TheLadders ran in 2012, and the same publisher retired the number it six years later [c85]. Its 2018 follow-up put the average initial screen at 7.4 seconds, about one second longer than the original finding [c88]. That is the whole life of the statistic: one study, one revision, one publisher. The 2018 version was built in two stages. First it timed recruiters who did not know they were being timed, then it used eye-tracking to map where their attention landed and in what order [c87]. Thirty professional recruiters were monitored over 10 weeks. The output was rendered as heat maps marking the parts of a page recruiters read and the parts they skipped entirely [c180][c184]. None of that stopped the retired figure. Articles were still printing six seconds as current in 2023, more than a decade after the study behind it and years after its own publisher moved the number [c178]. One explanation is simply that six reads as a better headline than 7.4. The claim now supports a small industry of beat-the-six-second-scan challenges and templates, mostly published by sites selling resume services rather than by people who recruit [c179]. Plenty of the pages repeating it never link to a study at all [c4]. ### What neither Ladders study ever disclosed Neither report says enough for anyone to check it. The 2012 study disclosed a sample of 30 recruiters; the 2018 update does not state how many took part in the eye-tracking phase at all [c110]. The methodology never explains how recruiters were selected, how many resumes each one reviewed, how the timing was run, or which eye-tracking technology produced the heat maps [c111]. The material being screened is missing too. The job specifications and sample resumes were never published, so the seniority, industry, and requirements of the roles stay unknown [c112]. Those are exactly the factors that move screening time. Nothing was published in a peer-reviewed journal either, which rules out independent replication [c113]. Read the number literally and it strains further. A steady six or 7.4 seconds per resume implies about 10 resumes a minute, or 300 in half an hour. That is not a description of how recruiting actually runs [c187]. The figure is an average across a wide distribution, not a fixed ceiling: a clear mismatch gets rejected almost instantly, while a close-fit candidate gets a longer read [c190]. Treat it as a rough marker of screening speed, and never as evidence that anything on the page was verified [c259].
