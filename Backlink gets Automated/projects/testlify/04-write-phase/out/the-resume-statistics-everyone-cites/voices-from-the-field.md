# Voices from the field, The Resume Statistics Everyone Cites That Are Already Out of Date

> What practitioners say in public about resume screening, ATS rejection and AI-written resumes, cut to what this article can use. Nothing here is a fact to cite, nothing carries a source tag, and nobody is named or quoted at length.

**Coverage.** 12 discussion(s) read and 124 comments, from 4 on linkedin, 8 on reddit. 164 results were looked at across 1 search round(s).

## Recruiters split hard on whether the ATS rejects anyone
**What they say.** One camp says applicant tracking software never auto-rejects on content, that they open every application, and that the whole idea is folklore. The other camp says the software scores and ranks, the recruiter works down that ranked list, and the job closes long before the bottom of it is reached, which lands the applicant in the same place as a rejection. Several people land in the middle: the software does not throw resumes away, but "nobody ever sees it" is the practical result, so the argument is really about the word reject.
**Strength.** Strong. The same split appears in four separate discussions, and on both sides it comes from people who say they run these systems for a living, not from applicants guessing.
**Serves.** Section 5, and section 4.
**What this changes.** Do not just retire the 75% figure. Replace it with the distinction practitioners themselves draw: no content-based auto-reject, but ranked order plus a closed requisition produces an unseen resume anyway. That is the mechanism the section needs.

## Volume, not scoring, is what buries an application
**What they say.** Practitioners describe hundreds of applications inside the first hours of a posting, and a working method of pulling the first ten or fifteen that look qualified, screening those, and only going back for more if nobody sticks. Several say the posting gets switched off once a target number of applicants is reached. The recurring line is that review systems were built for a couple of hundred applicants and are now facing thousands.
**Strength.** Strong. It appears in five discussions, from recruiters, in-house hiring managers and agency staffers, and it is stated as routine practice rather than as a complaint.
**Serves.** Section 5, and section 2.
**What this changes.** When section 5 replaces the rejection statistic with a mechanism, make supply the mechanism. It also explains the short screening times in section 2 without needing the reader to accept that anyone is being careless.

## Nobody claims to detect AI writing; they redesign around it
**What they say.** Almost nobody in these discussions says they can reliably spot an AI-written resume. What they say instead is that AI polish has flattened the signal, so every application now reads the same and the resume has stopped being useful for sorting. Their response is not detection. It is to move the judgment somewhere else: pick one line and ask the candidate to walk through it end to end, ask for the metric and what broke, ask for the actual artifact, or run a short live scenario. A few say they screen on the source domain instead, because bulk auto-apply services cluster.
**Strength.** Strong. Four discussions, including recruiters who openly say they do not mind AI-assisted writing at all, which makes the abandonment of detection more telling than a complaint would be.
**Serves.** Section 10, and section 9.
**What this changes.** Section 10 can rule out detection using practitioner behaviour, not just the misfiring-tool statistics. The people closest to the problem have already stopped trying to detect and moved to verification. That is the conclusion the HR leader can actually plan around.

## The tell is unverifiable specifics, not AI phrasing
**What they say.** The pattern people describe is not robotic wording. It is a resume that matches the posting too well, or carries metrics that cannot survive a follow-up question. Several say a candidate who hits every requirement is now a signal to look harder, and that they deliberately favour the middle of the pile at roughly seventy percent fit. Others describe impressive resumes collapsing on a basic exercise in the person's own claimed specialty. One recurring line: the resume is written by AI, the numbers on it are the candidate's own invention.
**Strength.** Strong. Five discussions across recruiting, engineering hiring and management communities, described the same way each time.
**Serves.** Section 9.
**What this changes.** Section 9 draws the polish-versus-fraud line the article needs. Field practice draws it in the same place, and adds a marker the surveys do not: the fabricated part is usually the quantified claim, not the prose around it.

## Practitioners disagree on what counts as resume lying
**What they say.** A large group treats stretched employment dates, rounded titles and inflated scope as ordinary, expected behaviour, on the grounds that a gap gets a resume discarded anyway. A smaller group treats any inaccuracy as disqualifying regardless of whether it mattered to the decision, and describes offers pulled over discrepancies that had no bearing on the hire. Several people on both sides note that background check data itself is often wrong on dates, and that people genuinely do not remember their own start and end months.
**Strength.** Moderate. Three discussions, with a clear split, but the "everyone does it" side is loudest in job-seeker-heavy threads, so treat it as a stated position rather than a measured rate.
**Serves.** Section 6, and section 11.
**What this changes.** Give section 6 the reason two 2025 surveys can disagree by twenty points: respondents do not share a definition of lying. And note in section 11 that a discrepancy is not automatically a lie, since verification records carry their own errors.

## What did not show up
- Nobody cited the six-second figure, defended it, or argued about how long they spend on a resume. Practitioners describe how they triage, never how many seconds they scan for. Sections 1, 2 and 3 get no support here and should not reach for any.
- No discussion of AI-fabricated-resume prevalence as a number. People describe the pattern constantly and never once put a rate on it. Section 8's scale figures have to stand on published sources alone.
- Nothing on where attention lands on the page, and nothing on ATS adoption rates or Fortune 500 penetration. Sections 2 and 4 have no field material.
- No practitioner traced any circulating statistic to its origin except the 75% one, and even that appeared as an assertion rather than as someone showing their working. The article's dating method has no field corroboration.
