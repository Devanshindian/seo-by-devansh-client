The facts are settled. This page is the plan for the article itself: what it argues, which sections carry it, and how long each one gets.

The spine — the one argument this article makes

Every section below exists to move this sentence forward. If a section does not serve it, it should not be here.

Almost every resume statistic in circulation is quoted without the year, sample or definition that produced it, so figures measured five years and four methods apart get stacked as if they described one timeless reality. This article dates each headline figure to the survey behind it, separates the ones measuring different things, names the ones with no traceable source at all, and adds the AI-fabrication figures that competing roundups omit. An HR or talent leader should finish able to cite any of these numbers with its year, publisher and method attached, and to drop the ones that cannot survive that test.

What it chose to leave out

The architect is shown the topics every ranking page covers. It is allowed to drop any that do not serve this article. Watch for the opposite of a problem here: if it never drops anything, it has stopped judging.

Left out only "Key takeaways / summary block" — that is the TL;DR, which a later step adds, so building it here would duplicate it. The other three expected topics each get a section of their own: recruiter screening time opens the article (S1, with S2 and S3 extending it), rejection rates and reasons get S11, and application-volume gets S12. Google's own SERP answer is ignored entirely: it defines CV as the statistical coefficient of variation, a homonym with no bearing on the hiring document.

The world — what this article is and is not about

Written in the research phase, before any searching started, and never changed since. It is one of the stated reasons the architect benches material below, so read it before judging whether a bench was right.

IS about
Statistics about resumes as used in hiring — how often job seekers lie or exaggerate on resumes, how long recruiters actually spend screening one, how ATS software filters them, and (the added angle) how often resumes now contain AI-fabricated or AI-polished content — each stat tied to the specific year/survey it came from instead of blended together as if timeless.
is NOT about
This is about resume/CV content and how it's screened in hiring, not about resume WRITING advice (how to format a resume, resume templates, resume builder tools/products). It is NOT about broader job-market or labor statistics (unemployment rates, salary data, time-to-hire) that don't specifically concern the resume document itself. It is NOT about interview fraud broadly — deepfake video interviews, proxy test-takers, or candidates using AI live during interviews are a neighboring world; this piece stays on the resume document, not the interview process. It is NOT about ATS software as a technology category (parsing accuracy benchmarks, vendor comparisons, buying guides) — ATS only appears here as it affects resume-screening statistics. It is NOT about academic CV statistics (publication counts, academic curriculum vitae norms) — 'resume' here means the hiring document, not the academic one. And it is not about 'resume' the verb (to resume/continue) — a homonym with no bearing on this subject.

Reading the article whole

Every section was written by a different writer at the same time, so faults BETWEEN sections are invisible to every other step. This is the one pass that reads the finished article end to end. It first LISTS every rule, scale and number (below), then edits with that list in front of it. The changes shown are measured by comparing the before and after, not taken from what it claims. If any safety check fails, the whole edit is thrown away and the original publishes.
