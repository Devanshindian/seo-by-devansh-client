The H1 and the intro

H1
AI Recruitment Tools That Can Survive a Bias Audit
intro
Ask a vendor for the bias audit behind their AI recruitment tools, and you will usually get a responsible-AI web page. That page is not evidence a regulator will accept. When a hiring tool selects one group at a much lower rate, the employer answers for it, not the vendor. In New York City, running one unaudited tool for a month carries roughly $15,000 to $45,000 in penalties. The tools you can defend are the ones an outside auditor has tested and published. Judged that way, four of the five best-known tools leave a blank where the numbers should be.
quick answer
Judge an AI recruiting tool on published audit evidence, not on a fairness claim. The record you need names an independent auditor and states the method it used. It reports selection rates by sex, by race, and by the two combined. The bar is one number: a group selected at under 80% of the top group's rate signals adverse impact. Measured that way, only Eightfold AI publishes a summary a regulator could read end to end. HireVue gates its audits, Workday publishes none, and Testlify posts a page with no impact ratios. Either way the employer, not the vendor, answers for the outcome.

Use them to rank and sequence candidates, never to auto-reject. Keep hard requirements as fixed rules, have a person sign off on every advance and rejection, and log the model version behind each ranking [c123][c97][c91].

Yes. Recruitment and candidate screening count as high risk. That brings duties on risk management, data governance, human oversight, and record keeping. Candidates must also be told an AI system is evaluating them [c30].

Plan for four to eight weeks. Most of that is data work: pulling selection outcomes by role, matching demographic fields, and explaining the gaps. The auditor's own analysis is usually the fastest part.

The Four Kinds of AI Hiring Tools, and Which Ones Decide touch-up
• added transition: The intro ends on missing audit evidence, so the section needed one line linking that to why the four categories are separated first.
Before any of that evidence can be judged, the tools have to be told apart. Four functional categories sit inside what most vendors sell as a single AI recruitment tool, and only some of them make a decision about a candidate. Parsing pulls data out of a resume and drops it into fields, which is data cleanup rather than a judgment. Screening applies criteria and decides who moves forward. Assessments measure skills or simulate the job, and their scores feed screening; conversational AI runs a chat or SMS flow to capture things like availability [c68]. That distinction is the whole reason an audit has to work tool by tool. A vendor bundles all four under one contract, one login, and one responsible-AI page. The four are still separate systems, with separate training data, separate thresholds, and separate outputs. An audit opinion covering the assessment engine tells you nothing about the screening rule that sits on top of it. The spread is wide enough that few readers will find only one of these in their stack. 88% of organizations worldwide now use AI somewhere in hiring [c720]. A single job posting draws around 500 applications, and automated screening tools read those resumes with natural language processing and machine learning [c722]. They rank candidates on how closely the text matches the job description [c722]. That matching step is where the first failure mode shows up, and it has nothing to do with a protected class. AI screening often scans for particular keywords, so a candidate with the skill but not the vocabulary gets filtered out before a human sees the file [c775]. Matching tools compare candidate profiles against job requirements and produce a ranked shortlist [c746]. Video interview platforms score recorded answers, including body language and vocal tone [c723]. So keep the four labels: parsing, screening, assessment, and conversational AI. Every yardstick, audit record, and verdict that follows attaches to one of them, not to a vendor name.
