The Four Kinds of AI Hiring Tools, and Which Ones Decide declared
• split a sentence: The 34-word applications-and-parsing sentence carried two facts; the ranking claim now stands on its own.
Four functional categories sit inside what most vendors sell as one product, a single AI recruitment tool, and only some of them make a decision about a candidate. Parsing pulls data out of a resume and drops it into fields, which is data cleanup rather than a judgment. Screening applies criteria and decides who moves forward. Assessments measure skills or simulate the job, and their scores feed screening; conversational AI runs a chat or SMS flow to capture things like availability [c68]. That distinction is the whole reason an audit has to work tool by tool. A vendor bundles all four under one contract, one login, and one responsible-AI page. The four are still separate systems, with separate training data, separate thresholds, and separate outputs. An audit opinion covering the assessment engine tells you nothing about the screening rule that sits on top of it. The spread is wide enough that few readers will find only one of these in their stack. 88% of organizations worldwide now use AI somewhere in hiring [c720]. A single job posting draws around 500 applications, and automated screening tools read those resumes with natural language processing and machine learning, ranking learning [c722]. They rank candidates on how closely the text matches the job description [c722]. That matching step is where the first failure mode shows up, and it has nothing to do with a protected class. AI screening often scans for particular keywords, so a candidate with the skill but not the vocabulary gets filtered out before a human sees the file [c775]. Matching tools compare candidate profiles against job requirements and produce a ranked shortlist [c746]. Video interview platforms score recorded answers, including body language and vocal tone [c723]. So keep the four labels: parsing, screening, assessment, and conversational AI. Every yardstick, audit record, and verdict that follows attaches to one of them, not to a vendor name.

The Four-Fifths Rule Every Bias Audit Runs declared
2 edits declared here:
• split a paragraph: Six sentences held two ideas: the three definitions, then Title VII and intent. Split at the seam.
• cut fluff: 'Where the liability sits is the part worth reading twice' announced the point instead of making it, and left the paragraph a sentence over.
Every tool in this article is scored against one number: 0.80. That is the four-fifths rule, the federal benchmark that treats a group's selection rate below 80% of the top group's rate as evidence of adverse impact [c282]. Before the number makes sense, three terms have to come apart. Adverse impact is the statistical evidence: one group gets selected at a much lower rate than another. Disparate impact is the legal theory that evidence triggers. Disparate treatment is the separate theory covering intentional discrimination [c312]. Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 recognizes the last two as distinct routes to liability, and the Supreme Court established disparate impact in Griggs v. Duke Power Co. in 1971 [c313]. The distinction matters because a disparate-impact claim needs no intent. Any test, screening rubric, interview format, or algorithm can produce a disparity that trips the rule, whatever the vendor meant to build [c283]. Nobody has to prove your recruiter wanted a certain outcome. Where the liability sits is the part worth reading twice. On May 18, 2023, the EEOC issued technical assistance applying the disparate-impact framework directly to algorithmic hiring tools, with the four-fifths rule as its rule of thumb [c315]. That guidance holds employers liable for discriminatory outcomes from third-party AI hiring products [c316]. A licensed tool's bias does not transfer to the vendor who sold it. You bought it, so you own the result. ### The four-fifths rule, and where the 80% number comes from The number comes from the Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures (UGESP), the 1978 federal rules governing how employers may screen candidates. The wording is exact: agencies will generally treat a selection rate below four-fifths of the rate for the highest-selected group as a substantially different rate of selection [c22]. UGESP itself defines adverse impact as a rate that works to the disadvantage of a race, sex, or ethnic group [c314]. A ratio under 0.80 is a trigger for investigation, not a verdict [c23]. Auditors often run statistical significance tests alongside it [c80]. The threshold also misfires on small pools, where a handful of candidates can swing a ratio [c112]. It is still the number every audit in this article reports.
How to Run the Four-Fifths Rule on Your Own Funnel declared
• split a sentence: The 38-word scoring-rate sentence jammed the rule and the median mechanic together.
Run the math yourself, in four steps. Divide the number selected by the number who applied to get each group's selection rate [c284]. Take the highest rate among the groups as your benchmark [c285]. Divide every other group's rate by that benchmark to get its impact ratio, then compare each ratio to 0.80 [c286][c287]. Two details decide whether the calculation holds up. Where the tool returns a fit score rather than a pass or reject, NYC Local Law 144 tests a scoring rate instead: scores instead. Scores are split at the sample median, and everyone above it counts as a pass [c105]. And the ratio has to be computed separately for every cohort, because one hiring round can clear on gender and fail on race [c296]. ### A worked example: the same pool fails on race and clears on sex Here is one pool of 135 candidates who sat the same written screen, cut two ways [c288]. | Group | Applicants | Selected | Selection rate | Impact ratio vs highest group | Clears 0.80? | |---|---|---|---|---|---| | Non-minority | 72 | 7 | 9.7% [c289] | 1.00 (benchmark) [c289] | Yes | | Minority | 63 | 3 | 4.8% [c289] | 0.49 [c289] | No [c290] | | Female | 51 | 4 | 7.8% [c292] | 1.00 (benchmark) [c292] | Yes | | Male | 84 | 6 | 7.1% [c292] | 0.91 [c292] | Yes [c293] | Same candidates, same screen, opposite verdicts. Read only the sex rows and the tool looks clean at 0.91 [c293]. The race cut lands at 0.49, well under the threshold, which is why a single headline ratio from a vendor tells you almost nothing [c290]. ### Intersections, unknowns, and the tests beyond adverse impact Group-level math still misses the people who sit at the crossing point. A system can look balanced on sex and balanced on race while disadvantaging one specific sex-by-race subgroup, such as Black women [c129]. So the calculation runs on the intersections too, not only on each axis alone [c107]. Then there are the candidates you cannot classify. Local Law 144 makes you publish the count of individuals whose race and gender are unknown and therefore excluded from the math [c234]. Do not drop them silently, because excluding them tends to understate adverse impact for smaller groups [c268]. The four-fifths rule is one instrument, not the whole toolkit. A counterfactual test swaps names, standardizes formatting, or alters accents while holding qualifications fixed, and a swing in the result points to bias the ratios never surfaced [c34].
