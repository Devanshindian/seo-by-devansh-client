The Four Kinds of AI Hiring Tools, and Which Ones Decide wrote 318 words, was asked for 324 -2% used 6 facts · 4 of them numbers · 4 sources
What this section was asked to do: Set the boundary of what this article audits by naming the four functional categories of AI recruiting tool and stating what each one actually decides about a candidate. This gives the reader the vocabulary every later section reuses, and it establishes that these are separate systems even when one vendor bundles them, which is why an audit has to cover them one at a time.
What the writer actually wrote

Four functional categories sit inside what most vendors sell as one product, and only some of them make a decision about a candidate. Parsing pulls data out of a resume and drops it into fields, which is data cleanup rather than a judgment. Screening applies criteria and decides who moves forward. Assessments measure skills or simulate the job, and their scores feed screening; conversational AI runs a chat or SMS flow to capture things like availability [c68].

That distinction is the whole reason an audit has to work tool by tool. A vendor bundles all four under one contract, one login, and one responsible-AI page. The four are still separate systems, with separate training data, separate thresholds, and separate outputs. An audit opinion covering the assessment engine tells you nothing about the screening rule that sits on top of it.

The spread is wide enough that few readers will find only one of these in their stack. 88% of organizations worldwide now use AI somewhere in hiring [c720]. A single job posting draws around 500 applications, and automated screening tools read those resumes with natural language processing and machine learning, ranking candidates on how closely the text matches the job description [c722].

That matching step is where the first failure mode shows up, and it has nothing to do with a protected class. AI screening often scans for particular keywords, so a candidate with the skill but not the vocabulary gets filtered out before a human sees the file [c775]. Matching tools compare candidate profiles against job requirements and produce a ranked shortlist [c746]. Video interview platforms score recorded answers, including body language and vocal tone [c723].

So keep the four labels: parsing, screening, assessment, and conversational AI. Every yardstick, audit record, and verdict that follows attaches to one of them, not to a vendor name.

Which of Your Hiring Tools Count as an AEDT wrote 412 words, was asked for 324 +27% used 13 facts · 7 of them numbers · 8 sources
What this section was asked to do: Rule which of the reader's existing systems are in audit scope. Establish that the test is functional (does it screen, rank or score in a way a human relies on) rather than technical, walk the inventory step that surfaces covered tools, and name the gray area where an employer's own scope call is the only thing standing between them and a violation. Everything after this assumes the reader knows which of their tools count.
What the writer actually wrote

A tool falls under New York City's Local Law 144 based on what it does, not how advanced it is: if it screens, ranks, or scores candidates, it counts [c186]. The final rules cover machine learning, statistical modeling, data analytics, or artificial intelligence that generates a prediction or a classification, where a computer at least in part decides which inputs matter [c5]. That reaches resume screening, assessment scoring, video-interview analysis, and candidate ranking [c210].

The obligations attach earlier than most buyers expect. A bias audit is required even when the tool only screens candidates at the top of the funnel, not just at the final hiring decision [c7]. Coverage follows the role too, applying to hiring and promotion for any position based in New York City, including a remote position connected to the city [c4]. Performance evaluation and workforce management tools sit inside the same definition [c4].

Work out your own scope before you book an auditor. Inventory the HR technology stack and rule on every system that touches candidate selection [c208]. For a large organization, that inventory is the foundation of a defensible compliance position, because you cannot prove a tool is out of scope if you never examined it [c211]. Document, per tool, what data it ingests, whether it produces a score or classification, and whether that output substantially assists or replaces a human decision [c194].

The line runs through your Applicant Tracking System (ATS), and you are the one who draws it. A platform limited to storage and manual keyword search sits outside Local Law 144; add weighted resume parsing or ranking and it sits inside [c408]. The awkward part is that nobody draws that line for you.

Researchers documented employers and vendors narrowing the definition until most companies considered their tools exempt from the law altogether [c522]. The regulator struggles to identify non-compliance partly because of that ambiguity [c523]. Posting and candidate disclosure are triggered only once an employer determines a tool is in scope, so an employer who never makes the call leaves little to inspect [c524]. The most common failure is quieter than a deliberate dodge: scoring functionality buried inside a broader HR platform that the compliance team never flagged during inventory, and therefore never audited [c514]. In practice, most teams will tell you the buried module is what bites them, not the obvious one.

The Four-Fifths Rule Every Bias Audit Runs wrote 375 words, was asked for 364 +3% used 11 facts · 11 of them numbers · 7 sources
What this section was asked to do: Define the legal test the rest of the article scores tools against. Separate adverse impact, disparate impact and disparate treatment, state where employer liability sits under Title VII and the EEOC's guidance, and land the four-fifths rule as the benchmark number. This is the criteria half of the methodology: the reader cannot read the tool grid without it.
What the writer actually wrote

Every tool in this article is scored against one number: 0.80. That is the four-fifths rule, the federal benchmark that treats a group's selection rate below 80% of the top group's rate as evidence of adverse impact [c282]. Before the number makes sense, three terms have to come apart.

Adverse impact is the statistical evidence: one group gets selected at a much lower rate than another. Disparate impact is the legal theory that evidence triggers. Disparate treatment is the separate theory covering intentional discrimination [c312]. Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 recognizes the last two as distinct routes to liability, and the Supreme Court established disparate impact in Griggs v. Duke Power Co. in 1971 [c313].

The distinction matters because a disparate-impact claim needs no intent. Any test, screening rubric, interview format, or algorithm can produce a disparity that trips the rule, whatever the vendor meant to build [c283]. Nobody has to prove your recruiter wanted a certain outcome.

Where the liability sits is the part worth reading twice. On May 18, 2023, the EEOC issued technical assistance applying the disparate-impact framework directly to algorithmic hiring tools, with the four-fifths rule as its rule of thumb [c315]. That guidance holds employers liable for discriminatory outcomes from third-party AI hiring products [c316]. A licensed tool's bias does not transfer to the vendor who sold it. You bought it, so you own the result.

The number comes from the Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures (UGESP), the 1978 federal rules governing how employers may screen candidates. The wording is exact: agencies will generally treat a selection rate below four-fifths of the rate for the highest-selected group as a substantially different rate of selection [c22]. UGESP itself defines adverse impact as a rate that works to the disadvantage of a race, sex, or ethnic group [c314].

A ratio under 0.80 is a trigger for investigation, not a verdict [c23]. Auditors often run statistical significance tests alongside it [c80]. The threshold also misfires on small pools, where a handful of candidates can swing a ratio [c112]. It is still the number every audit in this article reports.

How to Run the Four-Fifths Rule on Your Own Funnel wrote 438 words, was asked for 405 +8% used 16 facts · 11 of them numbers · 8 sources
What this section was asked to do: Show the reader how to run the calculation on their own funnel, not just read someone else's summary. Give the four steps from selection rate to benchmark group to impact ratio to the 0.80 comparison, render one worked pool that fails on race and clears on sex, then close on the intersectional and unknown-demographic slices that plain group math misses. This is the object the angle promises.
What the writer actually wrote

Run the math yourself, in four steps. Divide the number selected by the number who applied to get each group's selection rate [c284]. Take the highest rate among the groups as your benchmark [c285]. Divide every other group's rate by that benchmark to get its impact ratio, then compare each ratio to 0.80 [c286][c287].

Two details decide whether the calculation holds up. Where the tool returns a fit score rather than a pass or reject, NYC Local Law 144 tests a scoring rate instead: scores are split at the sample median, and everyone above it counts as a pass [c105]. And the ratio has to be computed separately for every cohort, because one hiring round can clear on gender and fail on race [c296].

Here is one pool of 135 candidates who sat the same written screen, cut two ways [c288].

Same candidates, same screen, opposite verdicts. Read only the sex rows and the tool looks clean at 0.91 [c293]. The race cut lands at 0.49, well under the threshold, which is why a single headline ratio from a vendor tells you almost nothing [c290].

Group-level math still misses the people who sit at the crossing point. A system can look balanced on sex and balanced on race while disadvantaging one specific sex-by-race subgroup, such as Black women [c129]. So the calculation runs on the intersections too, not only on each axis alone [c107].

Then there are the candidates you cannot classify. Local Law 144 makes you publish the count of individuals whose race and gender are unknown and therefore excluded from the math [c234]. Do not drop them silently, because excluding them tends to understate adverse impact for smaller groups [c268].

The four-fifths rule is one instrument, not the whole toolkit. A counterfactual test swaps names, standardizes formatting, or alters accents while holding qualifications fixed, and a swing in the result points to bias the ratios never surfaced [c34].

The Six Yardsticks Every Tool Is Judged On wrote 332 words, was asked for 324 +2% used no research at all
What this section was asked to do: State the scoring method before the picks so the grid that follows is checkable. Name the six yardsticks every tool below is judged on, say that the review rests on published audit documents, court filings and regulatory records rather than hands-on testing, and state the inclusion rule: a tool earns a section only where the public record speaks to enough of the yardsticks to compare it fairly.
What the writer actually wrote

Every tool below is scored on the same six yardsticks, and each one is a document you can ask a vendor to hand over. This section states the method before the picks, so you can check the grid against the same evidence we read rather than take the ranking on faith. Nothing here rests on hands-on testing. The scoring comes from published audit summaries, court filings, regulatory complaints, and vendor disclosure pages, because those are the records a legal review will actually accept.

Here is what each tool is measured against:

  • **Published bias-audit evidence.** Is there an audit summary anyone can read, or only a claim that one exists?
  • **Auditor independence.** Who ran it, what credential do they hold, and does the fee arrangement leave them free to fail the vendor?
  • **Stated methodology.** Does the summary say what was tested, on whose data, and how selection rates were calculated?
  • **Impact ratios by group.** Are selection rates reported by sex, by race and ethnicity, and by the intersections of both, or only in aggregate?
  • **Candidate notice and disclosure tooling.** Does the product give you what you need to send the required notice and prove you sent it?
  • **Contract terms on liability.** What do the vendor's indemnity clauses, liability caps, and compliance-with-law language commit them to when a tool produces adverse impact?

One rule governs which tools get a section: a vendor earns one only where the public record speaks to enough of these six to make a fair comparison. That rule cuts both ways. It keeps a vendor with no record from being judged on rumor, and it stops a thin marketing page from standing in for evidence.

The practitioners arguing about this in public are already skeptical of vendor fairness claims, and the yardsticks are built to be checkable rather than persuasive. Take this list into your next vendor call and ask for all six in writing.

HireVue: Two Audits, Neither One You Can Read wrote 515 words, was asked for 356 +45% used 18 facts · 13 of them numbers · 7 sources

What the writer actually wrote

No fully public bias audit with disclosed impact ratios exists for HireVue's video-interview scoring [c458]. The company has been audited twice by O'Neil Risk Consulting & Algorithmic Auditing and once by DCI Consulting Group, and none of that work is available in a form a compliance review could check [c459].

Start with what the system measures. It films candidate responses and analyzes gestures, pose, lean, tone, cadence, and content to produce an employability score [c140]. HireVue's chief industrial-organizational psychologist told the Washington Post that facial actions alone can make up 29% of that score [c142]. That matters because facial action is the part of the measurement with the least published validity evidence and the clearest disability exposure. Local Law 144 names video platforms that analyze facial expressions, tone, or word choice as a covered category, so the tool is in audit scope by design, not by argument [c136].

On November 6, 2019, the Electronic Privacy Information Center filed a complaint with the Federal Trade Commission alleging deceptive and unfair trade practices under Section 5 of the FTC Act [c435]. The deception claim targeted HireVue's marketing of its tools as a way to eliminate hiring bias without a reasonable basis for saying so [c436]. EPIC also charged that the company falsely denied using facial recognition [c440].

The audits arrived after that pressure. In 2021, HireVue announced it had undergone two audits by O'Neil Risk Consulting & Algorithmic Auditing [c442]. It never released them in full [c443]. The public could see summaries only after handing over personal information and agreeing not to reproduce any part of them [c444]. At least one audit was scoped to a single use case rather than the whole platform, which is not the clean bill of health the company's messaging implied [c445].

In January 2023, HireVue engaged DCI Consulting Group to audit its competency-based and game-based algorithms against Local Law 144, covering race, gender, and intersectional race-gender bias [c447]. That is the right shape for an audit. It was reported as commissioned and underway, with results to be posted on completion, not as a published outcome [c459].

Meanwhile the accessibility record moved. A candidate applying for a Seasonal Manager promotion at Intuit alleged the platform lacked accurate captioning [c454]. Her request for human-generated CART captioning was denied, leaving her with less accurate automated captions [c455]. She contends HireVue's automated feedback then criticized her communication abilities in a way traceable to that gap [c456].

Put the pieces together and the verdict is straightforward. An unresolved FTC complaint, gated audit summaries, an audit still pending publication, and a live accessibility allegation do not establish that this tool has cleared a transparent bias audit under Local Law 144 or the four-fifths rule [c460]. Candidates still cannot see their own scores or the logic behind them [c441]. Most teams will tell you that a vendor's fairness page is not evidence, and here the public record agrees with them.

Pymetrics: The One Tool That Let Auditors Read the Code wrote 374 words, was asked for 356 +5% used 19 facts · 10 of them numbers · 3 sources
What this section was asked to do: Show what a cooperative audit produces when a vendor actually opens the source code, because this is the fullest public account of any tool tested against the methodology in section 4. Establish the terms that made the audit credible, the two changes the vendor made to pass, and the score-reuse practice the auditors found that no disclosure covered.
What the writer actually wrote

Pymetrics gave outside auditors access to its source code, and that decision produced the fullest public account of any AI hiring tool tested against the four-fifths rule [c461][c469]. The product is a suite of 12 games drawn from cognitive science experiments [c463]. They score a candidate's cognitive, social, and emotional attributes, including risk tolerance and learning ability [c465]. The company has marketed the software as "entirely bias free" [c467], which is exactly the kind of claim an audit exists to test.

The terms are what made the finding credible. Researchers at Northeastern University ran the audit as a grant, led by Wilson [c527]. They were paid upfront, so no one could argue the fee shaped the verdict [c528]. They also held the contractual right to publish whatever they found, subject to a 30-day window in which Pymetrics could fix any problem the work surfaced [c529][c530].

That model sits between the two usual options: a permissionless external audit poking at a black box, and an internal team auditing its own employer [c473]. Pymetrics is now cited as evidence that outside experts can get insider access without the vendor losing control of its source code or data [c474].

The auditors read the source code by hand and ran the scoring pipeline themselves [c531]. Pymetrics' central claim held: the algorithm did implement the four-fifths rule as stated [c532]. Because no demographic data entered the training pipeline, the team found no evidence of direct discrimination in the scores [c533].

Passing was not automatic. Pymetrics adopted a more sophisticated imputation algorithm (a method for filling in missing values) in its machine learning pipeline, and tightened its data sampling criteria for language, region, and job type [c471][c472].

The platform recycled applicant scores for up to 330 days, and that reuse crossed employer boundaries [c475][c476]. So a candidate rejected once could carry the same score into an unrelated employer's funnel almost a year later. No disclosure covered it.
