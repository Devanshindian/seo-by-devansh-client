AI Recruitment Tools That Can Survive a Bias Audit

The Four Kinds of AI Hiring Tools, and Which Ones Decide

Four functional categories sit inside what most vendors sell as one product, and only some of them make a decision about a candidate. Parsing pulls data out of a resume and drops it into fields, which is data cleanup rather than a judgment. Screening applies criteria and decides who moves forward. Assessments measure skills or simulate the job, and their scores feed screening; conversational AI runs a chat or SMS flow to capture things like availability 1.

That distinction is the whole reason an audit has to work tool by tool. A vendor bundles all four under one contract, one login, and one responsible-AI page. The four are still separate systems, with separate training data, separate thresholds, and separate outputs. An audit opinion covering the assessment engine tells you nothing about the screening rule that sits on top of it.

The spread is wide enough that few readers will find only one of these in their stack. 88% of organizations worldwide now use AI somewhere in hiring 2. A single job posting draws around 500 applications, and automated screening tools read those resumes with natural language processing and machine learning, ranking candidates on how closely the text matches the job description 2.

That matching step is where the first failure mode shows up, and it has nothing to do with a protected class. AI screening often scans for particular keywords, so a candidate with the skill but not the vocabulary gets filtered out before a human sees the file 3. Matching tools compare candidate profiles against job requirements and produce a ranked shortlist 4. Video interview platforms score recorded answers, including body language and vocal tone 2.

So keep the four labels: parsing, screening, assessment, and conversational AI. Every yardstick, audit record, and verdict that follows attaches to one of them, not to a vendor name.

Which of Your Hiring Tools Count as an AEDT

A tool falls under New York City's Local Law 144 based on what it does, not how advanced it is: if it screens, ranks, or scores candidates, it counts. The final rules cover machine learning, statistical modeling, data analytics, or artificial intelligence that generates a prediction or a classification, where a computer at least in part decides which inputs matter 5. That reaches resume screening, assessment scoring, video-interview analysis, and candidate ranking 6.

The obligations attach earlier than most buyers expect. A bias audit is required even when the tool only screens candidates at the top of the funnel, not just at the final hiring decision 5. Coverage follows the role too, applying to hiring and promotion for any position based in New York City, including a remote position connected to the city 7. Performance evaluation and workforce management tools sit inside the same definition 7.

Work out your own scope before you book an auditor. Inventory the HR technology stack and rule on every system that touches candidate selection 8. For a large organization, that inventory is the foundation of a defensible compliance position, because you cannot prove a tool is out of scope if you never examined it 8. Document, per tool, what data it ingests, whether it produces a score or classification, and whether that output substantially assists or replaces a human decision 9.

The gray area: who decides your ATS is in scope

The line runs through your Applicant Tracking System (ATS), and you are the one who draws it. A platform limited to storage and manual keyword search sits outside Local Law 144; add weighted resume parsing or ranking and it sits inside 10. The awkward part is that nobody draws that line for you.

Researchers documented employers and vendors narrowing the definition until most companies considered their tools exempt from the law altogether 11. The regulator struggles to identify non-compliance partly because of that ambiguity. Posting and candidate disclosure are triggered only once an employer determines a tool is in scope, so an employer who never makes the call leaves little to inspect 8. The most common failure is quieter than a deliberate dodge: scoring functionality buried inside a broader HR platform that the compliance team never flagged during inventory, and therefore never audited 12. In practice, most teams will tell you the buried module is what bites them, not the obvious one.

The Four-Fifths Rule Every Bias Audit Runs

Every tool in this article is scored against one number: 0.80. That is the four-fifths rule, the federal benchmark that treats a group's selection rate below 80% of the top group's rate as evidence of adverse impact 13. Before the number makes sense, three terms have to come apart.

Adverse impact is the statistical evidence: one group gets selected at a much lower rate than another. Disparate impact is the legal theory that evidence triggers. Disparate treatment is the separate theory covering intentional discrimination 14. Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 recognizes the last two as distinct routes to liability, and the Supreme Court established disparate impact in Griggs v. Duke Power Co. in 1971 14.

The distinction matters because a disparate-impact claim needs no intent. Any test, screening rubric, interview format, or algorithm can produce a disparity that trips the rule, whatever the vendor meant to build 14. Nobody has to prove your recruiter wanted a certain outcome.

Where the liability sits is the part worth reading twice. On May 18, 2023, the EEOC issued technical assistance applying the disparate-impact framework directly to algorithmic hiring tools, with the four-fifths rule as its rule of thumb 15. That guidance holds employers liable for discriminatory outcomes from third-party AI hiring products 15. A licensed tool's bias does not transfer to the vendor who sold it. You bought it, so you own the result.

The four-fifths rule, and where the 80% number comes from

The number comes from the Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures (UGESP), the 1978 federal rules governing how employers may screen candidates. The wording is exact: agencies will generally treat a selection rate below four-fifths of the rate for the highest-selected group as a substantially different rate of selection 16. UGESP itself defines adverse impact as a rate that works to the disadvantage of a race, sex, or ethnic group 14.

A ratio under 0.80 is a trigger for investigation, not a verdict 17. Auditors often run statistical significance tests alongside it 18. The threshold also misfires on small pools, where a handful of candidates can swing a ratio 19. It is still the number every audit in this article reports.

How to Run the Four-Fifths Rule on Your Own Funnel

Run the math yourself, in four steps. Divide the number selected by the number who applied to get each group's selection rate. Take the highest rate among the groups as your benchmark. Divide every other group's rate by that benchmark to get its impact ratio, then compare each ratio to 0.80 13.

Two details decide whether the calculation holds up. Where the tool returns a fit score rather than a pass or reject, NYC Local Law 144 tests a scoring rate instead: scores are split at the sample median, and everyone above it counts as a pass 19. And the ratio has to be computed separately for every cohort, because one hiring round can clear on gender and fail on race 14.

A worked example: the same pool fails on race and clears on sex

Here is one pool of 135 candidates who sat the same written screen, cut two ways 13.

Same candidates, same screen, opposite verdicts. Read only the sex rows and the tool looks clean at 0.91 13. The race cut lands at 0.49, well under the threshold, which is why a single headline ratio from a vendor tells you almost nothing 13.

Intersections, unknowns, and the tests beyond adverse impact

Group-level math still misses the people who sit at the crossing point. A system can look balanced on sex and balanced on race while disadvantaging one specific sex-by-race subgroup, such as Black women 20. So the calculation runs on the intersections too, not only on each axis alone 18.

Then there are the candidates you cannot classify. Local Law 144 makes you publish the count of individuals whose race and gender are unknown and therefore excluded from the math 21. Do not drop them silently, because excluding them tends to understate adverse impact for smaller groups 10.

The four-fifths rule is one instrument, not the whole toolkit. A counterfactual test swaps names, standardizes formatting, or alters accents while holding qualifications fixed, and a swing in the result points to bias the ratios never surfaced 17.

The Six Yardsticks Every Tool Is Judged On

Every tool below is scored on the same six yardsticks, and each one is a document you can ask a vendor to hand over. This section states the method before the picks, so you can check the grid against the same evidence we read rather than take the ranking on faith. Nothing here rests on hands-on testing. The scoring comes from published audit summaries, court filings, regulatory complaints, and vendor disclosure pages, because those are the records a legal review will actually accept.

Here is what each tool is measured against:

  • Published bias-audit evidence. Is there an audit summary anyone can read, or only a claim that one exists?
  • Auditor independence. Who ran it, what credential do they hold, and does the fee arrangement leave them free to fail the vendor?
  • Stated methodology. Does the summary say what was tested, on whose data, and how selection rates were calculated?
  • Impact ratios by group. Are selection rates reported by sex, by race and ethnicity, and by the intersections of both, or only in aggregate?
  • Candidate notice and disclosure tooling. Does the product give you what you need to send the required notice and prove you sent it?
  • Contract terms on liability. What do the vendor's indemnity clauses, liability caps, and compliance-with-law language commit them to when a tool produces adverse impact?

One rule governs which tools get a section: a vendor earns one only where the public record speaks to enough of these six to make a fair comparison. That rule cuts both ways. It keeps a vendor with no record from being judged on rumor, and it stops a thin marketing page from standing in for evidence.

The practitioners arguing about this in public are already skeptical of vendor fairness claims, and the yardsticks are built to be checkable rather than persuasive. Take this list into your next vendor call and ask for all six in writing.

HireVue: Two Audits, Neither One You Can Read

No fully public bias audit with disclosed impact ratios exists for HireVue's video-interview scoring. The company has been audited twice by O'Neil Risk Consulting & Algorithmic Auditing and once by DCI Consulting Group, and none of that work is available in a form a compliance review could check 22.

Start with what the system measures. It films candidate responses and analyzes gestures, pose, lean, tone, cadence, and content to produce an employability score. HireVue's chief industrial-organizational psychologist told the Washington Post that facial actions alone can make up 29% of that score 23. That matters because facial action is the part of the measurement with the least published validity evidence and the clearest disability exposure. Local Law 144 names video platforms that analyze facial expressions, tone, or word choice as a covered category, so the tool is in audit scope by design, not by argument 9.

EPIC's FTC complaint, and the audits that followed

On November 6, 2019, the Electronic Privacy Information Center filed a complaint with the Federal Trade Commission alleging deceptive and unfair trade practices under Section 5 of the FTC Act 24. The deception claim targeted HireVue's marketing of its tools as a way to eliminate hiring bias without a reasonable basis for saying so. EPIC also charged that the company falsely denied using facial recognition 24.

The audits arrived after that pressure. In 2021, HireVue announced it had undergone two audits by O'Neil Risk Consulting & Algorithmic Auditing 25. It never released them in full. The public could see summaries only after handing over personal information and agreeing not to reproduce any part of them 25. At least one audit was scoped to a single use case rather than the whole platform, which is not the clean bill of health the company's messaging implied 25.

The captioning complaint, and the verdict on the record

In January 2023, HireVue engaged DCI Consulting Group to audit its competency-based and game-based algorithms against Local Law 144, covering race, gender, and intersectional race-gender bias 22. That is the right shape for an audit. It was reported as commissioned and underway, with results to be posted on completion, not as a published outcome 22.

Meanwhile the accessibility record moved. A candidate applying for a Seasonal Manager promotion at Intuit alleged the platform lacked accurate captioning. Her request for human-generated CART captioning was denied, leaving her with less accurate automated captions 26. She contends HireVue's automated feedback then criticized her communication abilities in a way traceable to that gap 26.

Put the pieces together and the verdict is straightforward. An unresolved FTC complaint, gated audit summaries, an audit still pending publication, and a live accessibility allegation do not establish that this tool has cleared a transparent bias audit under Local Law 144 or the four-fifths rule 27. Candidates still cannot see their own scores or the logic behind them 25. Most teams will tell you that a vendor's fairness page is not evidence, and here the public record agrees with them.

Pymetrics: The One Tool That Let Auditors Read the Code

Pymetrics gave outside auditors access to its source code, and that decision produced the fullest public account of any AI hiring tool tested against the four-fifths rule. The product is a suite of 12 games drawn from cognitive science experiments 28. They score a candidate's cognitive, social, and emotional attributes, including risk tolerance and learning ability 28. The company has marketed the software as "entirely bias free" 28, which is exactly the kind of claim an audit exists to test.

The terms are what made the finding credible. Researchers at Northeastern University ran the audit as a grant, led by Wilson. They were paid upfront, so no one could argue the fee shaped the verdict. They also held the contractual right to publish whatever they found, subject to a 30-day window in which Pymetrics could fix any problem the work surfaced 29.

That model sits between the two usual options: a permissionless external audit poking at a black box, and an internal team auditing its own employer. Pymetrics is now cited as evidence that outside experts can get insider access without the vendor losing control of its source code or data 29.

What the auditors tested, and the two changes made to pass

The auditors read the source code by hand and ran the scoring pipeline themselves. Pymetrics' central claim held: the algorithm did implement the four-fifths rule as stated 29. Because no demographic data entered the training pipeline, the team found no evidence of direct discrimination in the scores 29.

Passing was not automatic. Pymetrics adopted a more sophisticated imputation algorithm (a method for filling in missing values) in its machine learning pipeline, and tightened its data sampling criteria for language, region, and job type 29.

The 330-day score reuse nobody disclosed

The platform recycled applicant scores for up to 330 days, and that reuse crossed employer boundaries 30. So a candidate rejected once could carry the same score into an unrelated employer's funnel almost a year later. No disclosure covered it. Ask any vendor whether scores are reused across clients and for how long, because a transparent one can answer in a sentence.

Eightfold AI: What BABL AI's Audit Covers, and Doesn't

Eightfold AI has published a completed bias audit of its Eightfold Matching Model, run by an independent third party, BABL AI Inc., an AI auditing firm in Iowa City, Iowa 31. Dinah Rabe, Lead Auditor at BABL AI, signed the report on March 26, 2026 31. That is the package a compliance review can work with: a named auditor, a named signatory, and a date you can check against the twelve-month clock.

The credential behind the signature matters as much as the name. BABL AI's lead auditors hold ForHumanity Certified Auditor credentials under the NYC Automated Employment Decision Tool (AEDT) bias audit standard. The firm ties its independence to the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 and the ForHumanity Code of Ethics 31. Its fees are fixed and unrelated to the opinion it reaches, with the opinion grounded only in the audit criteria 31. That fee structure is the detail worth copying into your vendor questionnaire, because an auditor paid on the outcome is not independent in any sense a regulator would accept.

The method is published too. BABL AI calls it the Criterion Audit Framework, modeled after financial auditing practice and published in the Proceedings of the 2024 ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency 31. It is built on PCAOB Auditing Standard 1105, the rule governing what counts as audit evidence 31. Alongside documents, the auditors took verbal testimony from the people who maintain Eightfold's risk register 31.

What the audit covered, and what it deliberately did not

The engagement was designed to meet New York City Local Law 144 of 2021 31, and the criteria were written specifically against that law's definition of a bias audit. The auditors assessed gender, race and ethnicity across the seven groups the law names, and every gender-by-race intersection 31. They also reviewed Eightfold's internal governance and risk assessment process 31, covering risk identification, stakeholder impact, severity, likelihood, risk sources, and controls.

Now the limits, which Eightfold states rather than buries. The opinion covers the Matching Model only; the separate AI Interviewer product carries its own audit 31. Eightfold says plainly that the audit does not certify the model as bias-free, because no audit can. It is not intended to show compliance with any law other than the NYC AEDT rule 31. And while the model was audited as if it were an AEDT, Eightfold makes no determination that it legally is one 31.

That last point is the one to sit with. Publishing the scope and its limits is a transparency choice, separating what the audit covered from what it found 31. Eightfold treats the audit as a recurring obligation, matching Local Law 144's annual re-audit requirement 31. Ask every other vendor for the same package, and watch how few can produce it.

Workday AI Lawsuit, and No Published Bias Audit

Workday has published no bias-audit results, no Local Law 144 disclosure summary, and no four-fifths-rule outcomes for its AI hiring functionality 19. What exists in the public record instead is an active federal case. Derek Mobley, an African American man over 40 with a disability, alleges he applied to more than 100 jobs through employers running Workday's screening platform and was rejected from every one, often before a human read the application 32. The case sits in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California under case number 3:23-cv-00770, before Judge Rita F. Lin 33.

One detail the court singled out shows why the automation question is live. Mobley says a rejection reached him at 1:50 a.m., under an hour after he submitted, which the court accepted as a permissible inference of automated decision-making 34. That inference still has to survive discovery. For a compliance owner, the point is that the only evidence about this tool comes from litigation, not from an audit.

The agent theory that put a vendor on the hook

Mobley never claimed Workday was his employer. He argued Workday is liable as an agent of the organizations using its system 33. In July 2024 the court dismissed the employment-agency claim but let the agent theory stand 35. Workday had described itself as a neutral tool carrying out criteria employers set; the court disagreed, finding the complaint adequately alleged the software participates in the decision by recommending some candidates and rejecting others 34.

The court also dismissed the intentional-discrimination theory while keeping the disparate-impact claim alive 36. That distinction is the whole risk. Disparate impact needs no proof of intent, only a statistically demonstrable adverse effect on a protected group 36. It is the exact finding a bias audit is built to surface early.

The ADEA collective and its scale

On May 16, 2025, Judge Lin granted conditional certification of a collective action under the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA), the federal law protecting applicants aged 40 and over 3236. It covers everyone in that age group who applied through Workday's platform since September 2020 and was denied an employment recommendation 32.

The scale is the part that reprices vendor risk. Workday told the court that roughly 1.1 billion applications were rejected through its system during the relevant period, and warned notice could reach hundreds of millions of people 37. The court rejected that feasibility objection and pushed the parties into discovery on what data could identify collective members 37. Legal commentary has warned that organizations using Workday's AI screening features could be named and disclosed to plaintiffs' counsel through the notice process 32.

Whether Workday gives employers the notice and disclosure tooling the law requires

Workday does publish a responsible-AI page describing an analysis of HiredScore Spotlight by Secretariat, an independent third party 38. Read the disclaimers and it stops short of what a customer needs. Workday states the page is for transparency only, that it is not intended to satisfy any customer's legal obligation, and that it neither suggests nor implies the tool is an automated employment decision tool under any particular law 38.

The data behind it belongs to Workday, not to any customer. The analysis covers Workday's own applicant flow for greater New York City positions submitted between September 1, 2025, and February 28, 2026, limited to the five highest-volume job profiles 38. Workday's own page says outcomes may differ elsewhere depending on job requirements, candidate pools, and configuration 38. An employer still owns the public posting obligation, so what it needs from a vendor is a summary template, a field map for the underlying data, and a record of the tool version and distribution date the audit covers 39. This page is not that.

Scope has been contested in court too. On July 31, 2025, a judge ordered Workday to produce a list of customers who enabled HiredScore AI features, rejecting the company's argument that the collective should reach only candidates scored by Candidate Skills Match 40.

Testlify: A Transparency Page With No Impact Ratios

Testlify publishes a Local Law 144 transparency page and a completed third-party bias audit of its Matching Model, dated June 16, 2023 41. The page states that Testlify provides the information to help the public understand the Matching Model, whether or not the tool is covered by the law 41. That is more than iCIMS, Paradox, or Lumi offer. It is also, measured against the six yardsticks, an incomplete record.

Start with what the audit is. A vendor commissions it, so the scope and methodology sit with the vendor rather than with the employer who carries the legal obligation. That distinction matters commercially, because audit-ready documentation works as a procurement enabler for a vendor whose enterprise clients are the regulated party 42. It does not transfer the duty. Under Local Law 144 the employer, not the vendor, must confirm the audit happened and post the summary on its own site 8.

Testlify also describes continuous monitoring of its algorithms for potential bias, and scoring built on job-relevant criteria rather than subjective factors 2. That is a design commitment, not a measurement. The distance between the two is the whole point of an audit.

The adverse-impact numbers Testlify has not published

What a compliance reviewer needs is the table. Local Law 144 audits report selection rates and impact ratios across six ethnicity categories, two sex categories, and the intersections of the two, such as female Hispanic or male Asian 43. Those figures are not in Testlify's public record.

Compare that with what a full disclosure looks like. Citizens Bank publishes an audit summary showing that 57 of 640 demographic comparisons, or 8.9%, produced an impact ratio below 0.80, with none of them on gender 44. A reader can check that math. A reader cannot check Testlify's.

One more gap is worth naming. The ACLU and NYCLU maintain a public tracker of bias audits posted under the law, listing roughly 30 entries, and Testlify does not appear in it 45. The June 16, 2023 Matching Model audit in that tracker belongs to Eightfold AI, audited by BABL AI 45. Treat Testlify's page for what it is: a disclosure that the work was done, without the numbers that would let you verify the result.

What NYC Local Law 144 Requires, and What Comes Next

Local Law 144 does not ban AI in hiring. It makes using a covered automated employment decision tool (AEDT) in New York City conditional on three things: an independent bias audit conducted within the year before use, a public summary of the results, and notice to the candidate 46. Those conditions bind any employer or employment agency using an AEDT to hire, promote, or screen 47.

The trigger is the candidate's address, not yours. If someone lives in any of the five boroughs, evaluating them with an AEDT pulls you into scope, wherever your headquarters sits 10. The audit is annual, and it has to come from a third party with no hand in building or running the tool 8.

What you publish, and what you tell the candidate

The summary goes on your website before the tool is used, somewhere candidates can actually find it, usually the careers page 21. It has to carry the audit date, the date the tool was first used, selection or scoring rates and impact ratios for every category tested including intersections, the count of people who fell into an unknown demographic category, and where the audit data came from 8. If test data was substituted for your own, you say so and explain the method 8. A vendor's line about a responsible AI review does not satisfy any of this 46.

The candidate notice runs at least 10 business days ahead of the evaluation 46. It names the qualifications the tool assesses, and it tells the candidate how to ask for an alternative selection process or an accommodation 12. On request, you also disclose what data the tool collects, where that data comes from, and how long you keep it 8.

Keep the proof. That means the final audit report, its date, the tool version, and a description of the underlying data 46. Regulators can ask for audit documentation or notice records at any point, and failing to produce them is itself a violation 48.

The state rules, and the 2025 federal shift that changed less than it looked

The April 2025 executive order told the EEOC to deprioritize disparate-impact-only enforcement, and the agency has moved to stop investigating those charges on their own 13. Read that as a change in federal enforcement appetite, not in the law. Title VII still stands, and private plaintiffs can still sue 13.

Local rules never depended on the EEOC anyway. Local Law 144 points at the four-fifths rule directly, and California, Illinois, and Colorado have built AI and anti-discrimination requirements on the same principle 14. Illinois, Ontario, and the EU AI Act each require telling candidates that an AI tool is evaluating them 49. The Colorado AI Act, effective February 2026, goes further and asks for ongoing impact assessments rather than one check before launch 50.

Where Bias Actually Enters an AI Screening Tool

Bias in AI hiring systems is not one defect. It arises through several distinct failure modes, and knowing which one a tool has tells you why its audit came out the way it did 51.

The first is data bias: the training data carries historical inequities forward. Résumé collections underrepresent women in engineering, and interview transcripts over-index on one accent 17. When a tool learns from past hiring decisions that favored one group, it reproduces that preference in screening or scoring 52. It can also pick up proxies for protected traits, such as a zip code that correlates with race, without a protected attribute ever appearing as a model input 52. Practitioners tend to frame it the same way: clean the data, not the algorithm.

The second is measurement bias: the model optimizes for a proxy that does not match the thing the job actually requires 17. A tool can be perfectly consistent and still measure the wrong construct (whether a test measures the trait it claims to measure). Bias also enters through label definitions, feature selection, and masking at other stages of the recruitment pipeline 51.

That matters for scope, because a resume screening pipeline runs through five discrete stages, and each one is a separate point where an audit can fail the tool 53. An audit that only tests the final ranking model misses four of them.

Knockout criteria sit inside that pipeline and deserve their own scrutiny. These are meant to be hard, deterministic rules built on legally defensible facts: right to work, a required license, a security clearance, or minimum shift availability. Years of experience is the criterion vendors most often hard-code, and it draws the most pushback, because it correlates with age and predicts performance poorly 53.

A cutoff written as a rule rather than learned by a model still produces adverse impact. It is simply easier to see, and easier to defend or drop, once someone looks.

What Bias Audits Found When Vendors Were Tested

Across 150+ bias audits of production AI hiring systems, 85% of the tools tested cleared the 0.80 impact ratio bar on every demographic group measured 54. That is the aggregate number a vendor will quote at you. It hides the spread underneath it.

Bias varies by up to 40% between vendors 54. Some systems sit near 1.0, which is close to identical selection rates across groups. Others fall below the four-fifths threshold for at least one protected group, and the 15% that failed clustered in tools trained mainly on historical hiring data 54. Those tools learned from the human decisions the model was supposed to correct.

The human baseline matters before you read any of this as an indictment of AI. Over the last five years, more than 99.9% of U.S. employment discrimination claims concerned human decisions rather than algorithmic ones 54. So the question is not whether the software is worse than a person. It is whether you can produce evidence either way.

The documented failures in real screening tools, by year

The measured failures are specific, dated, and attributable, which is exactly what a legal review will ask you for.

A 2021 Harvard Business School study found automated resume screening systems rejected more than 27 million workers 55. Volume alone is not adverse impact, but it sets the exposure.

Then the direction of the error. A University of Washington study by Kyra Wilson and Aylin Caliskan, presented at the AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society, audited three open-source large language models used for resume screening. Resumes carrying White-associated names were preferred 85% of the time over identical resumes with names associated with other demographics 56.

The pattern is not one-directional. In October 2025, Stanford researchers found AI resume screeners rated older male candidates higher than both female and younger candidates, using resumes generated from the same underlying data 57. Research published through VoxDev in May 2025 found the opposite skew: tools favoring female applicants over Black male applicants with identical qualifications 57. One vendor also withdrew a screening tool after it scored women lower, traced to their underrepresentation in the training data 52.

Practitioners are already ahead of the vendors here. The common view is that the training data carries the old human bias forward, and that auditing the algorithm is the easier half of the problem.

The Data an Auditor Needs, and What You Won't Have

An auditor needs the results of your own prior use of the tool, and most employers cannot produce them cleanly. New York City's rules make historical data the default input, meaning the real outcomes from candidates the tool already scored 21. Test data, defined only as data that is not historical, is a fallback the auditor may use when you can explain why the historical record is insufficient 21. That explanation is not private. The published summary has to say why historical data was not used and describe how the test data was generated and obtained 5.

There is one other route. An audit may pool historical data across employers using the same tool, but only where those employers contributed their own data, or never used the tool at all 5.

The demographic breakdowns an audit must report

The audit reports selection or scoring rates and impact ratios by sex, by race and ethnicity, and by the intersections of the two 19. So the grid is not male against female and Black against white. It runs Black women against white men, standalone groups and combined groups alike 58. Where the tool outputs a continuous score rather than a yes or no, the auditor first splits candidates above and below the median before running the same ratio math 58.

Small samples and intersectional slices

Intersectional testing is where the numbers thin out. Auditors have reported slices for minority categories holding so little data that the results mislead rather than inform 59. Releasing those numbers, one auditor noted, can imply discrimination where the evidence simply is not there 59. The city's rules let an auditor drop any category below 2% of the dataset, so long as the justification and that category's applicant count and rate are still published 5.

The records that go missing, and why they skew the result

Missing demographic data is not spread evenly across your funnel. Disclosure is voluntary and collected late, so the records are fullest for candidates who reached interview or offer. Rejected and low-scoring candidates are the ones missing a race or gender value, which is exactly the population the audit exists to examine 60. You cannot fill the gap by guessing: imputed or inferred demographic data may not replace the required data, and unknowns must be counted and reported rather than quietly dropped 10.

Who Can Run Your Bias Audit, What It Costs, How Often

The auditor has to be genuinely independent, and Local Law 144's final rules spell out what that means: someone not involved in using, developing, or distributing the tool, never employed by the employer or the vendor, and holding no direct or material indirect financial interest in either 21. A vendor cannot audit its own tool, though it may collect and hand over the data a third-party auditor needs 9. Independence is not a nice-to-have here. The law requires it, and the EEOC's 1978 Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures expect the same posture 54.

Verifying it is your job, because New York City publishes no approved list of auditors 9. Ask three things before you engage anyone: the employment relationship, any role in building or selling the tool, and any financial interest in the vendor 46. Then check they have run adverse-impact analyses and four-fifths testing before 9.

The gray area is real. Because most vendors are also the developers, a vendor could arguably run its own audit as long as a different internal team did the work, since the rules never say the auditor must be a separate legal entity 59. A 2023-2024 study of 16 practitioners inside the Local Law 144 regime found wide disagreement over what counts as a legitimate auditor at all 11. Independent third-party audits still come out higher quality than internal or second-party ones 11. Buy the independence you can document.

What an audit costs, and how often you need a new one

Budget in the low five figures. Visible market examples for a Local Law 144 bias audit run roughly $5,000 to $50,000, and that range comes from a vendor's own pricing review rather than any official tariff 46.

The clock matters more than the price. An audit expires after 12 months, and the tool must have been audited within the year preceding its use 946. So the renewal is scheduled, not triggered.

A material model change is the harder case. Re-auditing after one is good practice, but neither the statute nor the adopted rule requires it 18. Treat the audit as a cycle anyway, with monitoring between renewals, because a model that was fair at launch drifts as it takes in new data 6.

Why Video-Interview Scoring Fails a Bias Audit

Automated video scoring is the weakest category in this article, because the science underneath it does not hold. AI Now Institute co-founder Meredith Whittaker called HireVue's approach "profoundly disturbing" and the underlying methodology "pseudoscience". Princeton computer science professor Arvind Narayanan went further, describing it as "AI whose only conceivable purpose is to perpetuate societal biases," and noting that reading emotion off a face is scientifically difficult in the first place 23.

The measurement evidence backs the criticism. A 2020 study in PNAS found automated speech-recognition systems made nearly twice as many transcription errors for Black speakers as for white speakers 17. Any tool that scores tone, cadence, or word choice off that transcript inherits the error rate. A 2024 University of Melbourne study found AI interview tools also misread speech from non-native English speakers and from candidates with disabilities 17, and facial-analysis components score darker skin tones less accurately 61.

Disability is the failure mode nobody has shown how to fix. The EEOC has warned that analyzing speech and facial patterns can disadvantage candidates with disabilities regardless of intent 61. IBM accessibility researcher Shari Trewin names the mechanism: AI judges people by who it thinks they resemble, "even when it may never have seen anybody similar to them" 23. Speech recognition performs badly on atypical speech, including a deaf accent. Because each job type runs its own model, more than 200 at HireVue alone, a fix would need disability representation across every one of them, which the reporting calls a tremendous effort that has not been demonstrated 23.

Auditing a black box: the counterfactual test, and what makes video defensible

You can still audit a tool you cannot see inside. The counterfactual test holds qualifications constant and changes one protected attribute, then checks whether the score moves 52. A peer-reviewed framework formalized this for video, editing attributes like age or gender in interview footage and feeding the original and the edited version into the same model 52. It needs only inputs and outputs, so it works on commercial systems whose vendors disclose nothing about training data or internals 52. Applied to a personality-prediction model, it revealed significant disparities across demographic groups 52.

Run it at the individual level as well as the group level: swap names, gender markers, and ethnic signals across otherwise identical profiles and see whether the judgment changes 54.

What makes video screening defensible is narrower scoring. Guidance across sources converges on limiting biometric, facial, voice, and sentiment inferences, disclosing what is scored, and providing accommodations 62. Score what a candidate said, not how they look or sound, and keep a human on every decision 61. Structured prompts and a fixed competency rubric are the countermeasure 61: three standardized questions, scored by trained reviewers against that rubric, before anyone advances. The complaint you hear most often about a human check on video scoring is that it becomes silent agreement with the machine's ranking, so the rubric has to give the reviewer something concrete to disagree with.

Chat Screeners Cause Adverse Impact Without Asking Anything

A conversational screener that never mentions race, sex, disability, or age can still produce adverse impact, and it is squarely in audit scope when it does. The legal test is what the bot does with the answers, not what it asks for. A bot that poses a fixed set of questions, collects the responses in a set window, and evaluates them to advance or reject a candidate meets the definition of an Automated Employment Decision Tool (AEDT) whenever it substantially assists or replaces a human decision-maker 9. New York City's compliance guidance names chatbot assessments that evaluate candidate responses in the same breath as resume-ranking tools and video-interview scoring 9.

The carve-out is narrower than most buyers assume. Tools that translate, transcribe, or capture data without scoring sit outside AEDT scope 12. So a bot that gathers availability, answers questions about the role, or logs replies for a recruiter to read later carries no audit obligation. The moment it applies pass/fail logic to what it collects, that changes. Academic work on AI-enabled interviewing draws the same line: a traditional video interview uses telecommunication technology with no AI decision-making component, while a scoring bot generates an automated prediction of job suitability 63.

Two failure modes show how a neutral-sounding script does the damage. First, format rejection: a chatbot that rejects candidates whose answers do not arrive in the expected format can screen out people using screen readers or other assistive technology, even when their qualifications are equivalent 62. Nothing in the transcript records a disability. The rejection reads as a formatting error.

Second, employment-gap probing. A bot's rigid logic on a gap penalizes candidates for caregiving or illness-related absences that say nothing about fitness for the job 57. And the underlying models carry their own problem: the FAIRE Benchmark, published in 2025, found significant racial and gender disparities in how large language model screeners scored candidates 17.

Who Is Liable When the Tool You Bought Discriminates

The employer answers for the outcome, not the vendor. Buying screening software from a third party does not move compliance responsibility off the organization that deployed it 62. Under Title VII, an employer stays liable for adverse impact (when a hiring step rejects one group at a much higher rate than another) produced by a vendor's AI, and "the vendor's tool did it" has never worked as a defense 64.

A vendor's own assurance of compliance carries no more weight. If the audit a vendor commissioned does not meet what the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection requires, the employer wears the violation anyway 12. Attorney Caroline Carrier makes the same point about the rest of the stack: employers "cannot hide behind the automated nature of their hiring tools at the risk of engaging in unlawful, yet unintended discrimination" 65. Some suits have named the employer, not the technology provider, as the party responsible for hiring decisions made off AI output 65.

That leaves the buyer with work no purchase order removes. Review the vendor's documentation, validation support, and monitoring processes. Understand what each tool measures and where it stops working. Keep people in the decision loop, and write down why a candidate was rejected instead of accepting the score as the reason 62. A screening system is an employment selection procedure, so it needs ongoing validation, monitoring, and accommodation, not a purchase and a shrug 62.

What the vendor contracts actually say about a law violation

Read the paper and the asymmetry is stark. Workday's and Google Cloud's standard customer agreements both promise the vendor will follow applicable law; Otter's does not. None of the three indemnifies the customer when the vendor itself breaks the law, and all three cap vendor liability for that at one year of fees.

The customer-side obligation runs the other way, uncapped. All three require you to indemnify the vendor for your violations of law arising from using their AI 66. Mobley is the case that pushed compliance-with-laws provisions in enterprise AI contracts into the negotiation 66. Most teams will tell you those clauses are non-negotiable, which is itself the finding: the risk you carry is unlimited, and theirs is capped.

How NYC Local Law 144 Is Enforced, and What It Costs

For its first two years, the law was barely enforced, and that record is exactly the wrong thing to plan around. A New York State Comptroller audit published December 2, 2025 reviewed how the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection (DCWP) enforced Local Law 144 from July 2023 through June 2025, and called the enforcement ineffective 10. The gap was not subtle. DCWP surveyed the websites and bias audits of 32 companies and identified one instance of non-compliance, while Comptroller auditors reviewing the same 32 companies found at least 17 potential violations 10.

The machinery behind that number was thin too. DCWP received only two formal complaints across the entire 24-month window, and 75% of test calls placed to the city's 311 hotline about these tools were misrouted and never reached the agency 1054. DCWP has since agreed to reroute 311 complaints, cross-train investigators, and move from complaint-driven enforcement toward proactive investigation 67. Employment counsel including DLA Piper now read 2026 as the start of a stricter phase 67. A near-1-in-32 detection rate is a description of the past, not a forecast.

Penalty math: how the exposure stacks up

Penalties run $500 for a first violation, $500 for each additional violation on the same day, then $500 to $1,500 for each subsequent violation per day of continued non-compliance 12. The stacking is what hurts. Failing to obtain a bias audit and failing to send the candidate notice count as two separate violations, not one 58. One tool left unaudited for 30 days is estimated at roughly $15,000 to $45,000 in exposure for that month alone, before notice and disclosure violations layer on top 10.

The gaps employers keep missing

Most findings come from visibility and maintenance, not from the audit itself. Three patterns recur: an audit summary that exists but sits outside the careers section where a reviewer would clearly and conspicuously find it, a candidate-notice process built at a tool's launch and never updated when the tool was replaced, and an inventory that misses scoring functionality buried inside a broader HR platform 10. DCWP can ask for the audit or the notification records at any time, and an employer who cannot produce them is non-compliant no matter how fair the tool actually is 48.

What a Passing Bias Audit Still Does Not Prove

A published bias audit is a disclosure document, not a fairness certificate. Local Law 144 sets no pass or fail standard 47, and it does not require any specific remedial action based on the results, even where the numbers show bias in how the tool is used 47. A tool can report an impact ratio well under the four-fifths threshold and still be used lawfully, because the statute requires the finding be disclosed, not that the tool be pulled 46. The Department of Consumer and Worker Protection has said this plainly: the audit reports defined outcome metrics, and it does not prove the tool is fair in every job, disability context, or decision 46.

That silence on remediation is not permission to ignore a bad result. Other anti-discrimination duties still apply 46. Treat a failing ratio as a decision gate: investigate the workflow, validate job relevance, consider pausing the feature, and document what counsel advised 46. That obligation flows from Title VII and the city and state human rights laws sitting alongside Local Law 144, not from the audit itself 10. And because the tables are public, a result that looks subpar by traditional standards invites scrutiny from regulators and third parties without any formal violation 60.

Where the four-fifths rule misfires in both directions

The four-fifths rule is a screening standard, not a definitive legal or statistical test 35. It ignores sample size and probability, so it flags gaps that are not statistically meaningful and misses gaps that are 13. It also misfires by selection rate. A 1.5% versus 2% selection rate breaks the rule on a half-point gap, while 76% versus 95% clears it on a 19-point gap 59.

Clearing the threshold proves even less than failing it. The audit tests whether selection rates differ across groups. It does not test whether the tool's criteria predict job performance at all. Reviewing the Pymetrics audit, Hilke Schellmann's reporting in MIT Technology Review quoted the concern that a tool passing the test still may not identify the best qualified women, so the women hired may not be as successful on the job 28. A passing impact ratio says nothing about predictive validity (whether a score actually forecasts how someone performs in the role).

Shelf life, vendor assurances, and the independence problem

An audit is valid for one year from when it was conducted, and it covers only the specific tool and distribution date it examined 39. It does not carry over to a changed model. If the vendor alters the model, training data, scoring method, decision threshold, input features, or user workflow, the audit and notice materials may no longer describe the product you are running 39. A stale audit can satisfy the letter of the law while describing something the tool no longer is.

Scope is the second gap. The required minimum covers sex, race and ethnicity, and their intersections 68. Age, disability, sexual orientation, gender identity, and religion sit outside what the audit must test 69. Nothing in a clean summary speaks to them.

Then there is who did the work. The rules define an independent auditor as someone not involved in using or developing the tool, and because most vendors build what they sell, one reading lets a vendor's own employee audit it as long as that person did not develop it. Commenters flagged that reading as a loophole during rulemaking 59. Academic research on Local Law 144 compliance found wide disagreement over what even counts as a legitimate auditor, with four distinct auditor roles and no accepted market standard 11. Practitioners give vendor fairness claims almost no credit, and the paperwork is the reason: a responsible-AI statement cannot show which model, threshold, or workflow was audited 46. Neither does a security review, however thorough 10.

The Human Review and Logging No Vendor Audit Covers

A human decision-maker who reviews what the model surfaces, instead of letting it auto-reject, is the single most important compliance control an employer has under NYC Local Law 144 49. It is also the control most likely to exist on paper and vanish in practice. The complaint you hear most often from recruiters is not that the human is missing, it is that the human stops arguing with the score.

That distinction is what a court examines. A reviewer who approves an AI recommendation in under 60 seconds per candidate is not exercising judgment, and judges look for whether the person actually weighed the case or rubber-stamped the output 50. So build the system so the tool never decides. It narrows and sequences the pool, a recruiter reviews a defined band of candidates, and a person signs off on both advances and rejections 53.

Two design rules follow from that. Keep hard must-haves rules-based and use AI scoring for prioritization only, not for final rejection, until the tool has been validated against actual hiring outcomes 1. Then add a mandatory second look for candidates whose scores land near the decision threshold, where a small scoring error flips the result 1.

Logging, monitoring, and validated scoring underneath

Logging is the control teams skip most often, and it decides whether you can reconstruct months later why one candidate ranked where they did 53. Log every ranking with the model version and the job description that produced it 53. Keep an audit trail of who changed the scoring criteria, when the model was updated, and why a given candidate was rejected 1.

Then test on a cadence, because a refreshed training set or an updated algorithm can introduce bias a one-time audit would never catch 16. Pull 25-50 rejected candidates a month and read the stated reason back against the job requirement 1.

Ground the scoring itself: every question should trace to a skill the job analysis flagged, which is what the EEOC asks for when a test gets challenged 70.

How to Choose an AI Recruiting Tool You Can Defend

Bias questions now sit inside procurement, not after it. In Warden AI's survey of more than 100 tech vendors and practitioners, 75% of HR leaders named bias a top concern when evaluating AI hiring tools, second only to data privacy 54. Greenhouse Software's Chief Legal Officer, Jung-Kyu McCann, says buyers are asking more questions about AI bias, and that it is becoming a key factor in purchase decisions 54.

That shift only helps if the questions have documents attached. A vendor demo shows a polished candidate flow and a clean score, not proof of fairness, so diligence has to ask for the evidence behind the demo and verify it on a limited pilot with written pass/fail criteria before signature 71. An auditable vendor can name its inputs and show how a decision gets made. One that talks about "AI" without showing either is selling vibes, not a system you can defend 1.

Put these to the vendor in writing, and name the artifact that answers each:

  • Ask what the score is built from. Request the list of inputs and the scoring logic, plus human-readable reasons attached to each ranking, so you can see why one candidate placed above another 1.
  • Ask what you can change. Request the calibration controls: the weights, thresholds, and role-specific logic you can tune, rather than a fixed black-box score 1.
  • Ask what an accuracy claim was measured against. A 95% figure means nothing until the vendor names the ground truth behind it: recruiter clicks, actual hiring outcomes, or a labeled dataset 1.
  • Ask for the audit rights in the contract. Only 17% of AI vendor contracts commit to complying with all applicable laws, against 36% in ordinary SaaS agreements 72.
  • Ask who pays when the tool discriminates. Request indemnification for bias and discrimination claims, and read the liability cap alongside it 73.

None of that is theater. Practitioners will tell you a vendor's "unbiased" claim earns no credit on its own, and the paperwork above is what separates a claim from a record you could hand a regulator.

Which of the Five You Can Actually Defend, and When

One of the five, Eightfold AI, publishes an audit package a compliance reviewer could read end to end. The other four are defensible only inside narrow limits, and one is not defensible at all right now. What separates them is not fairness. It is whether the evidence exists in a form somebody outside the vendor can check.

That distinction decides which buying situation each tool survives. Most teams will tell you a vendor's "unbiased" claim counts for nothing on its own, and they are right to hold that line. Read the verdicts below as constraints, not as rankings.

  • Eightfold AI holds up when you need to hand a regulator a named auditor, a stated methodology, and a published summary. It stops helping where your own use sits outside the scope the audit opinion deliberately excludes.
  • Pymetrics holds up when the question is whether an independent auditor saw the real model rather than a vendor description. It fails the disclosure test, because the score reuse the auditors found was never disclosed to candidates.
  • HireVue survives only where video scoring is one input into a human-led decision, never the shortlist itself. It fails the moment a reviewer asks to read the audits, since neither one is public in full.
  • Workday is not defensible on bias evidence today. There is no published bias audit of its AI hiring functionality, and the Mobley litigation is still running.
  • Testlify holds up where a reviewer wants a Local Law 144 transparency page and a completed third-party audit of the matching model. It falls short where that reviewer asks for impact ratios by sex and race, which are not in the public record.

The choice comes down to what you personally have to defend in a hearing or a board meeting. Pick Eightfold if your exposure is regulatory and you need a document you can file. Pick Pymetrics if you need proof an auditor read the system itself rather than a summary of it.

If your stack already holds one of the others, the burden moves back to you. Run the four-fifths math on your own funnel, log every rejection, and keep the notice records that prove you sent them.
