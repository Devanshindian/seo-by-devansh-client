# Voices from the field, Running a Hiring Hackathon That Screens for Real Skill, With a Judging Rubric and an AI-Use Policy

> What practitioners are saying in public, boiled down to what this article can use. None of it is a fact, none of it carries a source tag, and none of it quotes or names a person.

**Coverage.** 10 discussion(s) read and 106 comments, from 5 on linkedin, 5 on reddit. 152 results were looked at across 1 search round(s).

## Scores written after the debrief are not independent scores

**What they say.** The failure people describe is not the missing scorecard, it is the timing. Once the room has talked, and especially once the most senior person has spoken, everyone quietly re-anchors and what gets filed as an independent read is really a consensus wearing an independent label. Several practitioners say the single change that moved hiring quality for them was forcing everyone to write their rating down before anyone speaks, and they note that this is the step teams skip because it feels slow.

**Strength.** Strong. Three separate discussions, two on LinkedIn and one in an I/O psychology sub, with different people converging on the same mechanic rather than the same complaint.

**Serves.** Sections 4 and 5.

**What this changes.** Write "scores are locked before discussion" as an explicit, enforceable step in the calibration session and the split-adjudication process, not as an aside. Name the senior-voice anchoring effect as the thing the lock exists to stop.

## Five judges, five private definitions of good

**What they say.** People describe teams that believe they run a structured process, then discover at the debrief that each interviewer was measuring something different and improvising off the CV. The version practitioners find credible is not "we have a rubric" but "we agreed what a strong answer sounds like before the role opened." A related point comes up from the other end: an undefined standard higher in the funnel rejects people invisibly, because nobody debriefs the ones who were screened out.

**Strength.** Strong. Two LinkedIn discussions plus the I/O psychology thread, with the "define it before the role opens" phrasing appearing independently from different people.

**Serves.** The angle, and section 2.

**What this changes.** Frame the before-the-event lock as the load-bearing claim of the rubric section. The reader's likely objection is "we already score submissions" so the article should draw the line between having criteria and having agreed, written meanings for them.

## Practitioners split hard on whether AI use itself should be scored

**What they say.** One camp says the deliverable is the only thing that matters and how the candidate got there is as interesting as which mouse they use. The other camp scores the process directly and lists what they watch for: whether the candidate scoped the task, whether they verified output that looked plausible, whether they changed approach instead of getting tunnel vision. A third position sits between them, saying they do not care about the tooling as long as the candidate owns the outcome and can defend it. Candidates report the practical result of this split as unknowable rules, one comment listing how the same behaviour gets marked as too much AI by one interviewer and too little by the next.

**Strength.** Strong. Four discussions across both platforms, and the disagreement is between people who all actually run these assessments, not between believers and cynics.

**Serves.** Sections 6 and 10.

**What this changes.** Do not present "score how well AI was used" as the obvious consensus. State the split, name the deliverable-only position fairly, then argue the case. The candidate-side complaint about unknowable rules is the strongest available argument for publishing the policy in advance.

## The defence conversation, not the artifact, is where the signal is

**What they say.** The repeated move is to take the tooling away and ask the candidate to walk through the code, the decisions and the trade-offs unassisted. People are blunt that two reviewers will not read a diff properly in an hour, so the deliverable carries less signal than it appears to. The moment several of them name as the informative one is when the model returns something plausible and wrong, because how the candidate catches it is the thing that cannot be reconstructed from the submission afterwards.

**Strength.** Strong. Three discussions, including detailed accounts from people describing their own company's process and from a candidate describing a loop they passed.

**Serves.** Sections 9 and 10.

**What this changes.** Make the live defence the primary verification step in section 9, not a backstop. Section 10's bands should be gradeable from what the candidate says under questioning, since that is where judges can actually observe the behaviour.

## Detection confidence is mostly self-flattery, and it misfires

**What they say.** Claims of being able to spot AI use draw immediate pushback: people who use the tools well are not caught, and the confident spotter is mostly catching the careless. The concern practitioners raise is the false positive, including auto-rejection of candidates whose only AI use was a grammar pass. There is also a widely repeated fairness objection, that employers screening with AI while banning it for candidates is a rule people feel entitled to ignore.

**Strength.** Moderate. Three discussions, but two are job-seeker communities where the complaint runs hot, so treat this as the objection the reader will face rather than as measurement of how well detection works.

**Serves.** Sections 6 and 9.

**What this changes.** Frame detection signals as reasons to open a conversation, never as grounds for a decision. Give the article an explicit line on what the employer itself uses AI for, because the reader's candidates will make the hypocrisy argument.

## A rule the candidate never received is not a rule

**What they say.** The gap people keep naming is that plenty of employers reject for AI use while almost nobody publishes what appropriate use looks like beforehand. The framing that recurs is that AI guidance should be as routine as explaining the interview format. The workable examples shared are narrow and operational, along the lines of use whatever assistant you like as long as it is visible on screen, rather than broad statements of principle. One practitioner notes the obvious contradiction of requiring AI at work while forbidding it in the process that hires for that work.

**Strength.** Moderate. Two discussions, but one is a direct public request for policy templates that surfaced multiple practitioners offering theirs, which is decent evidence that the artifact is scarce and wanted.

**Serves.** Section 7, and the angle.

**What this changes.** Pitch the tier table as the thing readers cannot currently find anywhere. Keep the wording operational and testable, closer to what must be visible on screen than to statements about the spirit of the rules.

## What did not show up

- **No weighted rubrics.** Nobody described criteria with fixed weights, a weighted total, or anchored bands for what a 1 versus a 3 versus a 5 means. The discussion stops at "write the scorecard first" and never reaches arithmetic. Sections 2 and 3 have no field support and must stand on research.

- **No inter-rater reliability in the wild.** Not one practitioner mentioned double-scoring a sample, an agreement figure, or adjudicating a split. The nearest thing is people arguing over the debrief. Section 5 is a genuine gap in practice, which is an argument for writing it, but do not imply anyone is doing it.

- **No hackathons judged as hiring events.** The hackathon material that surfaced is internal-adoption hackathons run for existing staff, with agendas and follow-up funding, not screening events with candidates and scores. The screening discussion is all take-homes and live sessions. Borrow the judging patterns from those formats but do not claim hackathon-specific practice.

- **No disclosure statements.** Nobody described asking candidates to declare what they generated, and nobody described how a disclosure would be scored. The honesty-penalty problem that section 10 exists to fix is not something practitioners have hit yet, so present it as a foreseeable trap rather than a reported one.
