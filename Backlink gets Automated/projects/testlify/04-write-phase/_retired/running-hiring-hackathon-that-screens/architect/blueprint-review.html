The facts are settled. This page is the plan for the article itself: what it argues, which sections carry it, and how long each one gets.

The spine — the one argument this article makes

Every section below exists to move this sentence forward. If a section does not serve it, it should not be here.

A hiring hackathon is a work sample test, so the thing that decides whether it produces a usable hiring signal is not the event but the scoring: a rubric with fixed criteria, weights and anchored bands written before the event, a panel calibrated against sample submissions and checked for agreement, and a published AI-use policy that says what candidates may generate, what they must disclose and how that disclosure is scored. Written for the in-house recruiter or TA lead running this at volume, who should finish able to build the rubric, run the calibration session and publish the AI policy for their next event.

The world — what this article is and is not about

Written in the research phase, before any searching started, and never changed since. It is one of the stated reasons the architect benches material below, so read it before judging whether a bench was right.

IS about
This is about employers running a structured hiring hackathon — a timed, scenario-based build challenge given to job candidates during the hiring process — as a real-skill assessment method, with a defined judging rubric (the criteria and scoring scale evaluators use to score submissions) and an explicit AI-use policy (what generative AI tools candidates may or may not use while building their submission, and how that's disclosed and factored into judging).
is NOT about
This is NOT about public prize hackathons or hackathon competitions run for cash prizes, sponsorship, or PR (e.g. weekend hacker competitions open to the public). It is NOT about community coding weekends, meetups, or open-source sprints. It is NOT about charity or social-good hackathons. It is NOT about student/university hackathons run for learning or networking rather than hiring. It is NOT about internal company hackathons run for product ideation or innovation among existing employees (not a hiring context). It is NOT about security/CTF-style 'hacking' events (penetration testing, capture-the-flag). 'Rubric' here is a hiring evaluation scorecard, NOT an academic classroom grading rubric. 'AI-use policy' here means candidate-facing rules for one hackathon event, NOT a company's broad internal/enterprise AI governance or usage policy for employees.

Rules and warnings it states119
The fix sits in the scoring, not the event.
So a score that changes with the judge is a hire that changes with the judge.
A hiring hackathon gives you a usable signal only if the scoring exists before the event does.
Build the rubric from the job description, not from what the demos happen to show.
Use a handful of weighted criteria, and write down what a failing, an acceptable and a top submission looks like on each.
Then have judges score practice work privately and compare their numbers, so you can fix the wording two people read differently.
Publish an AI rule with the brief: what candidates may generate, what they must disclose, and what disqualifies them.
Score how well AI was used, not whether it was.
It sits inside a hiring decision, so how judges score it carries the same legal and practical weight as any other selection test.
So a properly built one carries far lower adverse impact risk (when a hiring step rejects one group at a much higher rate than another) [c14].
That property is conditional. It holds only when you score on predetermined, job-linked criteria, established through structured analysis rather than guesswork [c15].
That makes a rubric a legal-defensibility argument and not only a fairness one [c16].
