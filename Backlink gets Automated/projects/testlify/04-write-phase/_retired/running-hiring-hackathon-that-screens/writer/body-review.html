Your Hackathon Is a Work Sample Test, Not a Demo Day wrote 349 words, was asked for 238 +47% used 7 facts · 3 of them numbers · 4 sources
What this section was asked to do: Establish that a hiring hackathon is a work sample test in the technical sense, which is why it predicts well and why its judging carries selection-test weight. Settle what the score is for before any criterion gets written, so the rest of the article has a fixed target. Sets up every scoring decision that follows.
What the writer actually wrote

A hiring hackathon that asks candidates to build features in a realistic environment counts as a work sample test in the industrial and organizational psychology sense [c112]. That classification is the whole reason the judging matters. It sits inside a hiring decision, so how judges score it carries the same legal and practical weight as any other selection test.

The format earns that status. Work samples produce a standardized mean difference of roughly 0.38 between Black and white test-takers, against roughly 1.0 for cognitive ability tests, so a properly built one carries far lower adverse impact risk (when a hiring step rejects one group at a much higher rate than another) [c14]. That property is conditional. It holds only when you score on predetermined, job-linked criteria, established through structured analysis rather than guesswork [c15].

There is a second reason to write the criteria down. Because work samples come straight from job tasks, they meet the content-validity requirements of the Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures (1978), which makes a rubric a legal-defensibility argument and not only a fairness one [c16].

Define what the score is meant to determine before you write a single criterion [c97]. A hackathon can fund a pilot, surface a promising concept, or identify someone to hire, and the criteria that matter shift with that answer.

Skip that step and judging drifts into opinion. Without a defined judging plan, judges apply criteria inconsistently, and a project's rating becomes a function of which judge watched it [c3]. Our own topline report with SHRM Labs found 45.8% of HR and talent acquisition practitioners describe their process as inconsistent, with confidence in it varying by hiring manager or role [c8004]. What most teams will tell you is that this gap stays invisible until the debrief, when everyone compares notes and discovers they were each measuring something different.

Fix the target now, and treat the resulting number as one input to a hiring decision rather than the decision itself.

The Rubric: Five or Six Criteria, Fixed Weights, One Total wrote 519 words, was asked for 416 +25% used 9 facts · 4 of them numbers · 8 sources
What this section was asked to do: Render the actual rubric. Fix how many criteria to score, what each one measures, what weight it carries and how the weighted total is calculated, all locked before the event. This is the artifact the title promises, and sections three through five all operate on it.
What the writer actually wrote

Score five or six criteria, not 10, and fix the weights before the event opens. Standard practice for a work sample is to predefine the five to six skills the exercise actually tests, pick one consistent scale, and write out in advance what separates a poor score from a great one [c19]. Too many criteria tire the judges and lower consistency; too few leave the rubric too blunt to tell you what went wrong [c32].

Pull the criteria from the job description rather than from what the demos happen to show you [c39]. That is the line between having a scorecard and having a rubric: most teams believe they run a structured process, then find at the debrief that each judge was quietly measuring something different.

The math is one formula, applied identically to every submission. Score each criterion on a fixed scale, divide by the maximum, multiply by that criterion's weight, and sum the results into a total out of 100 [c9002]. Run it in a spreadsheet rather than by hand, then sanity-check the resulting ranks before anyone uses them to make a call [c53].

Weights are where the rubric encodes what the role needs. Weighting is what stops a candidate who presents beautifully from finishing level with one who actually built the thing [c9003]. Below is a working allocation for a build-style hackathon. Adjust the percentages to the role, keep them summing to 100, and publish them before the event.

| Criterion | What you are scoring | Weight | Score band anchors (1 / 3 / 5) | |---|---|---|---| | Technical execution | Code quality, technical difficulty, how much of the demo is real versus faked [c89] | 30% | 1: no working build · 3: works on the happy path · 5: handles edge cases cleanly | | Problem solving | Understands the problem, sound approach, trade-off analysis, optimization [c9007] | 20% | 1: no evidence · 3: one workable approach · 5: weighs alternatives and picks well | | Feasibility and workflow fit | Whether the build could actually ship and fits how the team works [c98] | 20% | 1: demo only · 3: shippable with rework · 5: ready to hand off | | Testing | Common and corner cases, self-correcting bugs [c9007] | 15% | 1: none · 3: happy-path tests · 5: corner cases covered | | Communication | Clarifies, explains the approach, communicates while building [c9007] | 10% | 1: cannot explain the build · 3: explains what · 5: explains why | | Originality | Novelty of the idea, creative use of the theme or tools [c89] | 5% | 1: template output · 3: familiar idea, own execution · 5: an approach a judge would not have thought of |

What a 1, a 3, and a 5 Actually Mean wrote 361 words, was asked for 297 +22% used 8 facts · 4 of them numbers · 3 sources
What this section was asked to do: Turn each number on the scale into a written description a judge can apply without guessing, then fix what evidence a score may rest on and what a judge must record alongside it. Resolves the gap between having criteria and scoring them consistently, which calibration in the next section then tests.
What the writer actually wrote

"Score from 1 to 5" is not a rubric. Judges need written descriptions of what each point on the scale looks like, because vague criteria produce noisy scores no matter how strong the criteria themselves are [c41]. Write one anchor per number, per criterion, before the event.

Three anchors are enough to hold a five-point scale steady: what a failing submission looks like, what an acceptable one looks like, and what a top one looks like [c42]. On a criterion like problem fit, a 1 might read "solves something the brief did not ask for," a 3 "addresses the stated problem with an obvious workaround," and a 5 "handles the stated problem including the edge case named in the brief." Two and four sit between the written bands. Attach a real example to each anchor so a 5 means the same thing to every judge rather than whatever that judge privately associates with a strong demo [c8].

Pre-graded samples do the rest of the work. Show the panel one submission that scored at the top and one that scored at the bottom, since a clear example of each end of the scale improves consistency more than extra description does [c45].

The rubric has to say what a judge may look at. Only the submission and the stated requirements, or outside knowledge too? Does missing documentation cost points? May a judge infer what the candidate meant to build, or score only what shipped? Tight evidence rules cut disagreement between judges [c48].

Edge cases need the same treatment. Decide in advance whether a working build that ignores a stated constraint fails on completeness, on instruction adherence, or on both [c59].

Then require a written trail. Ask judges to explain their reasoning first and state the score second, which produces more accurate scoring and leaves a record of why the number landed there [c50]. Every low score carries a short rationale quoting the evidence behind it [c49]. Without that, you cannot tell later whether the rubric was wrong or the judge was.

Calibrate the Panel Before Anyone Scores a Real Candidate wrote 617 words, was asked for 327 +89% used 18 facts · 13 of them numbers · 8 sources
What this section was asked to do: Give the reader a runnable calibration session: independent private scoring of sample submissions, side-by-side reveal, outlier discussion that sharpens the rubric rather than forcing agreement. Then state plainly what calibration and structure can and cannot do about bias, so the claim stays honest.
What the writer actually wrote

Calibration is a discrete step that runs before live judging opens, and it is separate from writing the rubric [c340]. The rubric says what a strong submission looks like; calibration is where the panel proves everyone applies that description the same way [c341]. Skip it and the disagreement does not disappear, it just surfaces later, once a real candidate has already been scored [c342].

Run it as one working session, about 90 minutes, in this order:

  • **Walk the rubric together.** Read out each criterion, its weight, and the scale, with concrete examples of what every level looks like. Field questions until the panel uses the same words for the same behaviors [c344].
  • **Score one or two practice submissions privately.** Give each judge an anonymized sample, past candidate work or a seeded practice build, and have them score it against the rubric with no discussion [c346].
  • **Put every score on one sheet.** All judges' numbers for the same submission go side by side on a shared sheet or whiteboard, revealed only once everyone has finished [c348].
  • **Discuss only the outliers.** Where one judge gave a 2 and another gave a 5 on the same criterion, examine that gap [c349]. Leave the rows where everyone landed within a point alone.
  • **Rewrite the ambiguous wording, not the scores.** The goal is not to agree on a "correct" number for the practice item. It is to find the rubric language that let two judges read it differently, and fix it before anyone judges for real [c350].

The private-then-compare order is the part that carries the weight. Without it, the most senior voice in the room sets the answer before anyone else has looked at the evidence themselves [c347]. Most teams find one or two criteria need sharper definitions every time they run this, which is a sign the session is working, not failing [c351].

When two judges still cannot agree, make each one justify the rating with something the submission actually shows. The feeling of a score is not a justification; only a cited behavior or artifact is [c352]. That habit is also what makes the agreement durable rather than a one-off reconciliation that drifts on the next candidate.

Calibration fixes unwritten standards. It does not make the panel neutral. Judges avoid extreme scores early in a series, saving room in case something stronger arrives, so the first submissions get scored more harshly than identical ones seen later [c104]. They also drift toward each other's numbers, a pressure that gets worse when a panel has not built trust before scoring begins [c105].

Structure narrows where bias can operate, and the effect is measurable. Anchored, structured scoring has been shown to cut the Black-White standardized mean difference in ratings from d = 0.56 to roughly d = 0.23, from process design rather than bias training [c359]. That is a real reduction, not an erasure.

The evidence on what structure cannot reach is just as clear. A Yale study published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 2012 found science faculty rated an identical application as more competent when the applicant was labeled male, and offered that person $4,000 more in starting salary [c122]. Trained evaluators were not immune. A rubric narrows the door bias comes through; it does not close it [c125].

So treat the calibrated score as an approximation of judgment, not a substitute for it [c130]. Two independent readers per submission, and a second reader on any borderline result, do more for a close decision than another round of alignment talk [c371].

How to Calculate Inter-Rater Reliability, and Fix Splits wrote 427 words, was asked for 267 +60% used 12 facts · 9 of them numbers · 5 sources
What this section was asked to do: Move from calibration to proof. Show how to double-score a sample, read the agreement figure, adjudicate splits instead of averaging them, log the reasoning, and re-run calibration on a schedule as scores drift. Closes the judging half of the article.
What the writer actually wrote

Calibration tells you the panel talked; agreement tells you the rubric worked. Double-score a slice of submissions, commonly 10 to 20 percent, and adjudicate every disagreement instead of letting it pass [c151]. Measure agreement formally: Cohen's kappa when two judges score the same work, Krippendorff's alpha (an agreement statistic that handles more than two raters and missing scores) when the panel is larger [c152]. In recruiting calibration practice, a reliability figure below 0.40 is read as inconsistent evaluation, tracked monthly, with dips investigated rather than ignored [c361].

Read the split itself, not just the coefficient. When two judges land more than one point apart on the same criterion, that is the trigger to reconvene [c360]. If it keeps happening on the same criterion, the rubric is the problem, not the panel [c355].

Two defects cause most of it. Overlapping dimensions come first: when "quality," "impact," and "completeness" partly mean the same thing, judges score them inconsistently [c155]. Weak anchors come second, where a level offers adjectives instead of a concrete threshold [c155].

Never average a split. Resolve it through discussion or a third reviewer, and log which criterion caused it, since a criterion that repeatedly divides judges needs either a rewritten anchor or a tighter definition [c58]. Write down the final decision and the reasoning [c156]. That log becomes the precedent for the next ambiguous submission and the raw material for your next rubric revision [c156]. It is also what a hiring team pulls when a placement is challenged, rather than asking judges to reconstruct their thinking from memory.

Scores drift. After repeated rounds on the same role, averages tend to inflate toward "strong yes" or compress toward the middle, and both are bias signals rather than a sharper panel. A recurring session, quarterly is a reasonable cadence, re-anchors scoring against the written rubric [c367].

You do not need a standing meeting for this. Share three scored sample submissions by email or chat, have each judge rate them privately, then compare during a meeting you already hold [c364]. Lock the private scores before anyone speaks, since the most senior voice in the room quietly pulls everyone else toward it.

Two housekeeping rules keep it holding. A judge who joins after the first calibration runs an abbreviated version before scoring a live submission [c368]. The rubric lives in a shared system, so each round is checked against the document rather than against someone's memory of the discussion [c369].

Set the AI Rule Before Candidates Set It for You wrote 219 words, was asked for 178 +23% used 5 facts · 2 of them numbers · 1 sources
What this section was asked to do: Open the AI half by pricing the cost of silence: candidates already use AI, submissions are exactly the artifact where it hides, and with no stated rule the scores compare tool access rather than skill. Rules out the blanket ban as a workable default and sets up the tier that follows.
What the writer actually wrote

If your event has no stated AI rule, your scores are comparing tool access, not skill. Candidates are already using these tools. A 2023 survey found almost half of job seekers were using ChatGPT to write resumes and cover letters [c164], and 70% of them reported a higher response rate as a result [c165]. Legal guidance on the practice notes it is often undetected by employers [c166].

A hackathon submission is where that goes invisible. Code, a build plan, and a demo script all look the same whether a person or a model produced them. Your panel cannot separate a candidate's own judgment from generated output unless the submission asks for it directly.

The instinct is to ban it. Some organizations do, requiring candidates to agree to no AI use anywhere in the process, with disqualification if it is detected [c172]. Commentators call that extreme, since using AI to sharpen an application is now treated as a basic job-market skill rather than a violation [c173]. The complaint you hear most from candidates is not the rule, it is that nobody publishes one, so the same behavior reads as too much AI to one judge and too little to the next.

So state the line instead of guarding it.

What Candidates May Generate, and What Disqualifies wrote 593 words, was asked for 446 +33% used 27 facts · 3 of them numbers · 4 sources
What this section was asked to do: Draw the operative line: AI that scaffolds commodity work is permitted, AI that produces the judged deliverable is not. Render the tier as a table of permitted, disclosable and prohibited uses with build-submission examples, published before the event rather than adjudicated after it.
What the writer actually wrote

The line that holds up is preparation versus substitution: AI may scaffold commodity work, but it may not produce the thing being judged [c71]. Candidates cannot calibrate to that line unless it is written down before the challenge starts [c174]. So publish the tier with the brief, not after submissions land, because the rules that score a build have to have been visible while the build was happening [c191]. Any restriction also needs a nondiscriminatory business reason decided in advance, never applied retroactively because a judge disliked how one team worked [c192].

If you run several tracks, set the tier per track rather than one blanket rule, and post it on each track's brief [c207]. A senior engineering track can carry a stricter no-generation rule than a product or analyst track [c208].

Boilerplate has a usable test: if you deleted a block and a template from the internet could replace it in five minutes, it is boilerplate; if deleting it breaks something only your product does, that is the feature code where the hiring signal lives [c228][c229]. Scaffolding of that kind is work most engineering teams already treat as commodity, not the skill the assessment exists to measure [c202].

The harder distinction sits one level up, in who decomposes the problem. AI performs as a fast implementer given clear direction, and fails when handed a whole scenario wholesale [c203][c204]. A candidate who breaks the problem down and directs bounded pieces to a tool is showing the judgment you are testing. A candidate who pastes in the prompt and submits the output is not [c205].

What a Complete AI Disclosure Statement Says wrote 431 words, was asked for 356 +21% used 11 facts · 4 of them numbers · 6 sources
What this section was asked to do: Specify the disclosure itself: the three elements that make it complete, where it sits in the submission, how much detail each kind of AI involvement needs, and what the stated consequence is for hiding it. Gives the reader wording they can paste into the submission form.
What the writer actually wrote

A complete disclosure names three things: the tool, the task it did, and the human review that followed [c211]. Anything missing one of those is incomplete, not absent, and your rubric should score it that way [c314]. Publishing has already settled on a workable sentence for this, and it adapts to a build submission almost verbatim: "the author(s) used [NAME OF TOOL / SERVICE] in order to [REASON]. After using this tool/service, the author(s) reviewed and edited the content as needed and take(s) full responsibility" [c313].

Put it in a dedicated, mandatory field in the submission form, next to the code and the write-up. Buried in terms-of-service language, it goes unread and unwritten. Two to four sentences is enough [c282]. One consistent structure also cuts the work on both sides: the candidate stops guessing at phrasing, and the judge knows exactly what to look for [c212].

How much detail you require should track how much the AI shaped the graded work, not whether it was touched at all. A prose-polishing pass needs one confirming line, along the lines of "no new content was generated, and all edits were reviewed by the author" [c240]. AI that wrote or debugged judged logic needs specifics: which files, which functions, which decisions.

Ask for it category by category rather than as a single checkbox. Borrow the build-relevant buckets: scaffolding and boilerplate, debugging, code review, and documentation. Each maps to a different part of your rubric, so a judge scoring architecture can see whether AI touched architecture. A candidate who writes "used AI to help" without naming the tool or the task scores lower on this line than one who itemizes where AI entered the build [c316].

Attesting to no AI use and disclosing AI use are different policies, not two wordings of one [c209]. An attestation is a prohibition on the honor system. A disclosure tier assumes some AI use and makes the extent of it the thing you score.

Say the consequence in writing, before the event. Employer codes already state that failure to disclose or deliberate misrepresentation may result in disqualification from the process or withdrawal of an offer [c320]. Put the matching point deduction or disqualification rule in the rubric in advance [c326]. Then say the other half just as plainly, because candidates will assume the worst otherwise: disclosed use in a permitted area is not scored down for the disclosure itself [c299].

Spotting Undisclosed AI Use in a Submission wrote 287 words, was asked for 178 +61% used 7 facts · 5 of them numbers · 5 sources
What this section was asked to do: Answer the obvious objection: policies only work if you can spot the candidate who ignored them. Name the checkable signals in the artifact and the process trace, and establish the live defence conversation as the verification step that actually holds.
What the writer actually wrote

A policy only holds if you can check it, and the check that actually holds is the live defence conversation, not a detector score. AI can produce polished work across a wide range of tasks, which is exactly why a conversation with the candidate is what verifies they understand the work behind their submission [c78]. Where a hackathon pairs the build with a defence session, that session is the mechanism that catches undisclosed AI use, not a separate compliance step bolted on afterwards. Everything below feeds that conversation.

Some patterns are worth flagging before the candidate sits down:

  • **Repeated phrasing and generic content** that never addresses the specific task set, alongside formatting inconsistencies inside one submission [c258].
  • **Shifting tone or writing style** within a single document, or across a candidate's materials [c258].
  • **Overlap between candidates**, where two independent submissions share wording or structure [c264].
  • **A solution that arrives fully formed**, with no commit history, no noted dead ends, and no documented trade-offs behind it [c272].
  • **Answers structured to score maximally** against a known rubric, such as complexity analysis volunteered where nobody asked for it [c269].
  • **Over-documented code**, with exhaustive line-by-line comments human developers rarely write [c9028].

None of these is proof. Detection tools misfire badly enough that no serious guide recommends them as the sole indicator of misconduct, and non-native English writers get flagged at higher rates [c9029]. Most teams will tell you the confident spotter is mostly catching the careless. So treat every tell as a question to ask, not a verdict: "walk me through why you chose this loop structure" [c9028]. What the candidate says next is the evidence you score.

Score How Well AI Was Used, Not Whether It Was wrote 352 words, was asked for 267 +32% used 9 facts · 6 of them numbers · 5 sources
What this section was asked to do: Land the article by turning AI use from a compliance flag into a scored dimension: named bands for how well the candidate directed, verified and built on AI output, each score tied to cited evidence. Fixes the failure mode the disclosure section created, where judges quietly punish the candidates who told the truth.
What the writer actually wrote

The last line item on the rubric should not read "AI used: yes/no." It should score how well the candidate directed, checked, and built on what the model gave back [c307]. Practitioners are split on this, and the split is real: one camp holds that the deliverable is the only thing worth judging and the route there is nobody's business. The case against that position is simple. If the score ignores process, it rewards whoever had the better tool.

Score the behavior instead. Give the criterion its own anchored bands and a required evidence component, the same design that keeps every other criterion on your rubric from being gamed [c306]. In practice that means separate line items rather than one holistic AI score: prompting and iteration shown, output verified and debugged by the candidate, disclosure complete and specific [c309]. The artifact you score them from is the disclosure statement plus whatever process trace the candidate submits, a prompt log, git history, or their walkthrough of what they built versus what the model wrote [c311]. Most of these bands are easiest to grade during the live defense, since that is where you can watch the judgment happen.

What separates a 5 from a 3 is not tool avoidance. It is who wrote the better specification, made the better architectural call, and debugged more effectively on top of generated output [c237].

Disclosure carries a measured cost. Across 16 studies with more than 27,000 participants, text labeled as AI-assisted was rated lower than identical text labeled human-written [c328]. Perceived quality did not explain the gap. Authenticity did: the work felt less like it came from a real person [c329].

So a policy promising that disclosure will not count against a candidate does not hold on its own [c303]. Anchor the bands, and require every AI-use score to name the specific evidence behind it, the same rule the rest of your rubric already follows [c334]. Without that, you teach candidates to stop telling you.
