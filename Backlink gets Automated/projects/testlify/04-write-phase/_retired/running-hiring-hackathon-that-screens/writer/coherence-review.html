The Rubric: Five or Six Criteria, Fixed Weights, One Total 66.0% of the block rewritten
  • ### Weight the Criteria, Then Run the Same Math on Every Candidate The math is one formula, applied identically to every submission. Score each criterion on a fixed scale, divide by the maximum, multiply by that criterion's weight, and sum the results into a total out of 100 [c9002]. Run it in a spreadsheet rather than by hand, then sanity-check the resulting ranks before anyone uses them to make a call [c53]. Weighting is what stops a candidate who presents beautifully from finishing level with one who actually built the thing [c9003]. Below is a working allocation for a build-style hackathon. Adjust the percentages to the role, keep them summing to 100, and publish them before the event. | Criterion | What you are scoring | Weight | Score band anchors (1 / 3 / 5) | |---|---|---|---| | Technical execution | Code quality, technical difficulty, how much of the demo is real versus faked [c89] | 30% | 1: no working build · 3: works on the happy path · 5: handles edge cases cleanly | | Problem solving | Understands the problem, sound approach, trade-off analysis, optimization [c9007] | 20% | 1: no evidence · 3: one workable approach · 5: weighs alternatives and picks well | | Feasibility and workflow fit | Whether the build could actually ship and fits how the team works [c98] | 20% | 1: demo only · 3: shippable with rework · 5: ready to hand off | | Testing | Common and corner cases, self-correcting bugs [c9007] | 15% | 1: none · 3: happy-path tests · 5: corner cases covered | | Communication | Clarifies, explains the approach, communicates while building [c9007] | 10% | 1: cannot explain the build · 3: explains what · 5: explains why | | Originality | Novelty of the idea, creative use of the theme or tools [c89] | 5% | 1: template output · 3: familiar idea, own execution · 5: an approach a judge would not have thought of | A 0 sits below the 1 band on every row and means no evidence at all, not poor work [c9002].   • ### Name the Evidence a Score Is Allowed to Rest On The rubric has to say what a judge may look at. Only the submission and the stated requirements, or outside knowledge too? Does missing documentation cost points? May a judge infer what the candidate meant to build, or score only what shipped? Tight evidence rules cut disagreement between judges [c48]. Edge cases need the same treatment: decide in advance whether a working build that ignores a stated constraint fails on completeness, on instruction adherence, or on both [c59]. Then require a written trail. Ask judges to explain their reasoning first and state the score second. That produces more accurate scoring and leaves a record of why the number landed there [c50]. Every low score carries a short rationale quoting the evidence behind it [c49]. Without that, you cannot tell later whether the rubric was wrong or the judge was. → (cut)
Calibrate the Panel Before Anyone Scores a Real Candidate 27.6% of the block rewritten
  • ### What Calibration Fixes, and What It Will Never Fix Calibration fixes unwritten standards. It does not make the panel neutral. Judges avoid extreme scores early in a series, saving room in case something stronger arrives. The first submissions therefore get scored more harshly than identical ones seen later [c104]. They also drift toward each other's numbers, a pressure that gets worse when a panel has not built trust before scoring begins [c105]. Structure narrows where bias can operate, measurably: anchored, structured scoring cuts the Black-White standardized mean difference in ratings from d = 0.56 to roughly d = 0.23 [c359]. That comes from process design rather than bias training, and it is a real reduction, not an erasure. A 2012 Yale study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences found science faculty rated an identical application as more competent when the applicant was labeled male. They also offered that person $4,000 more in starting salary [c122]. Trained evaluators were not immune. A rubric narrows the door bias comes through; it does not close it [c125]. So treat the calibrated score as an approximation of judgment, not a substitute for it [c130]. Two independent readers per submission, and a second reader on any borderline result, do more for a close decision than another round of alignment talk [c371]. → (cut)

  • ### Re-Calibrate on a Schedule, Not Just Before the First Event Scores drift. After repeated rounds on the same role, averages tend to inflate toward "strong yes" or compress toward the middle. Both are bias signals rather than a sharper panel. A recurring session, quarterly is a reasonable cadence, re-anchors scoring against the written rubric [c367]. You do not need a standing meeting for this. Share three scored sample submissions by email or chat, have each judge rate them privately, then compare during a meeting you already hold [c364]. A judge who joins after the first calibration runs an abbreviated version before scoring a live submission [c368]. The rubric lives in a shared system, so each round is checked against the document rather than against someone's memory of the discussion [c369]. → (cut)

What a Complete AI Disclosure Statement Says 44.4% of the block rewritten
  • ### Scale the Disclosure to How Much AI Shaped the Build How much detail you require should track how much the AI shaped the graded work, not whether it was touched at all. A prose-polishing pass needs one confirming line, along the lines of "no new content was generated, and all edits were reviewed by the author" [c240]. AI that wrote or debugged judged logic needs specifics: which files, which functions, which decisions. Ask for it category by category rather than as a single checkbox. Borrow the build-relevant buckets: scaffolding and boilerplate, debugging, code review, and documentation. Each maps to a different part of your rubric, so a judge scoring architecture can see whether AI touched architecture. A candidate who writes "used AI to help" scores lower on this line than one who itemizes where AI entered the build [c316]. ### Attestation, Disclosure, and What Happens When They Hide It Attesting to no AI use and disclosing AI use are different policies, not two wordings of one [c209]. An attestation is a prohibition on the honor system. A disclosure tier assumes some AI use and makes the extent of it the thing you score. Say the consequence in writing, before the event. Employer codes already state that failure to disclose or deliberate misrepresentation may result in disqualification from the process or withdrawal of an offer [c320]. Put the matching point deduction or disqualification rule in the rubric in advance [c326]. → (cut)
