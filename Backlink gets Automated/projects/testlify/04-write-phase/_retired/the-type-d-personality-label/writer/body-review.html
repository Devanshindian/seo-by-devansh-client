Type D Personality Traits: Both, or It Isn't Type D wrote 281 words, was asked for 252 +12% used 12 facts · 6 of them numbers · 6 sources
What this section was asked to do: Define the construct precisely and settle where it came from, so every later section rests on the real thing rather than the loose label. Establish the three facts the reader carries forward: Type D is negative affectivity and social inhibition together, not either alone; Denollet built it in 1995 out of cardiac research; and the label only exists as a DS14 score clearing a fixed cut-off on both halves.
What the writer actually wrote

Type D is one specific thing: negative affectivity and social inhibition present together in the same person, measured by a 14-item questionnaire called the DS14 [c1][c33]. Either trait on its own is not Type D. The D stands for distressed [c2].

Negative affectivity is a steady tendency to feel negative emotions across time and situations: worry, irritability, gloom, anger, and anxiety [c3]. Social inhibition is holding those feelings and opinions back in social situations, out of fear of being rejected or disapproved of [c4]. The first is what a person feels. The second is what they do with it in front of other people.

The construct rests on the joint occurrence of the two, not on either one alone [c5]. Someone who worries constantly but says so freely is not Type D. Neither is someone reserved and calm.

Johan Denollet, a professor of medical psychology at Tilburg University in the Netherlands, first formulated Type D in 1995 [c6]. He built it on clinical observations in cardiac patients, plus existing personality theory [c7]. That origin matters for everything a hiring manager might want to do with the label, and it is where the next section starts.

The DS14 splits into two 7-item subscales, one per trait, each item scored 0 to 4 [c11][c12]. A person is classified as Type D only when they score 10 or more on both subscales [c13]. Denollet set that cut-off as a median split in a sample of cardiac patients [c13]. It is a yes-or-no category, which the literature notes suits clinical settings where a treatment decision has to go one way or the other [c14].

The Mortality Finding That Failed to Replicate wrote 297 words, was asked for 277 +7% used 8 facts · 5 of them numbers · 4 sources
What this section was asked to do: Price the evidence behind the label's authority. Show what the original cardiac cohorts found, then show the large meta-analysis and the failed replications that took it back, and land the verdict plainly: the finding that made Type D famous did not survive scrutiny. This is what makes borrowing the label for a decision it was never tested on worse than it first looks.
What the writer actually wrote

The finding that made Type D famous, that it predicts death in heart patients, did not survive a pooled analysis of more than 11,000 cardiovascular disease patients [c54]. That analysis drew on 19 previously published prospective studies, and it found Type D is not a risk factor for mortality in that population [c54].

Start with what the early work claimed, because the numbers were striking. Denollet followed cardiac rehabilitation and coronary artery surgery patients, never job candidates or employees [c41]. In one group of over 300 cardiac rehabilitation patients, about 27% of Type D patients had died within 10 years, against 6% of the others [c42]. Later cohorts described up to a fourfold rise in mortality, repeat heart attack, or sudden cardiac death, independent of how severe the disease was [c44].

Those numbers are what gave the label its weight. Then the larger studies arrived and could not reproduce them. A big heart-failure sample showed no prognostic value for mortality, and patients treated with intracoronary stents showed no link to major cardiac events [c56]. Researchers reviewing the reversal concluded that the earlier, smaller studies "may have inadvertently reached exaggerated or false conclusions," and that "early type D studies had overestimated the prognostic relevance" [c55].

There is a pattern behind that. A 2023 analysis found smaller studies reported larger Type D effects than bigger ones, which the authors flagged as possibly hinting at publication bias [c57]. So even inside cardiology, the home ground where every Type D study was run [c40], the construct's own headline claim is contested.

That matters for a hiring decision in one specific way. A label whose best-supported finding weakened under scrutiny is a poor thing to stretch into a use nobody has tested at all.

No Type D Study Ever Measured Job Performance wrote 466 words, was asked for 403 +16% used 11 facts · 7 of them numbers · 6 sources
What this section was asked to do: Rule out the assumption the reader arrived with, that a clinical-sounding trait profile must say something about work. Show that every work-adjacent finding in the literature is a health or distress outcome, that no study validates the DS14 against any hiring or performance criterion, and that the single number it produces is self-reported on exactly the traits candidates manage impressions on.
What the writer actually wrote

No study behind the Type D label has ever used job performance, hiring, or workplace conduct as an outcome [c62]. Every study in the construct's evidence base is built around cardiovascular disease [c63]. The label was developed to predict cardiac events, mortality, and transplant rejection in heart patients [c94]. What it predicts is who gets sicker, not who does the work.

Some of the research does touch work, which is where the confusion starts. A 2010 review reports a negative effect of Type D on work-related problems, specifically higher absence-leave and higher levels of vital exhaustion [c72]. Read the outcome and the framing gives itself away. That is self-reported tiredness and days missed, studied by health psychologists as signs of strain, not as a measure of how well someone does their job.

The rest of the work-adjacent evidence sits further into clinical territory. A systematic review of eight studies found Type D robustly associated with suicidal thoughts and behaviors across populations and countries [c74]. That is a mental-health finding, and mental health is precisely the ground a hiring decision is neither equipped nor permitted to screen on.

So the DS14 has never been checked against a job-performance or personnel-decision criterion at all [c67]. A validation study of the Russian-language version confirmed the questionnaire is reliable for research assessment in Russian-speaking respondents [c66]. That is a finding about measurement consistency, not about relevance to job tasks. The label carries the surface trappings of a personality type, a name, a two-letter code, and a defined trait profile, which is how it keeps getting pulled into hiring conversations regardless of what the research measured.

The DS14 is a self-report questionnaire, and it asks about negative affectivity, the stable tendency to feel negative emotions, and social inhibition [c97]. Those are the two traits a candidate is under the most pressure to manage in front of an employer. Candidates tend to answer based on what they think an employer wants to hear rather than their true disposition, so results are not always accurate to begin with [c96].

There is a deeper version of that problem, and practitioners who defend self-report instruments concede it themselves. A score like this describes how a person sees themselves. Nobody has checked that self-image against how the person actually behaves at work.

None of this makes personality assessment worthless. Some instruments were built and validated for selection and hold up. Even for those, the standard guidance is that results add insight rather than make the hiring decision on their own [c80]. Type D was never validated for employment decisions in the first place, so using it as a screening signal skips that caution entirely [c106].

Calling a Candidate Type D Can Cross the ADA Line wrote 522 words, was asked for 428 +22% used 12 facts · 8 of them numbers · 11 sources
What this section was asked to do: Turn the scientific problem into the one the reader will personally answer for. Settle where a screen derived from cardiac-risk research sits against the ADA's medical-examination line, what a defensible test would have to prove about job-relatedness, and why declining a candidate over a perceived distress profile can be actionable even though Type D is not a diagnosis. Builds directly on the no-validation finding above.
What the writer actually wrote

The moment a Type D read influences a hiring decision, the problem stops being scientific and becomes legal. The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) generally bars employers from asking about health conditions before a conditional offer, and it defines a prohibited medical examination partly by whether a test is designed to reveal an impairment or mental health condition [c82]. Congress named invisible disabilities such as epilepsy, heart disease, and mental illness as exactly the kind of thing it worried would get screened out this way [c82]. The DS14 is an instrument whose entire evidence base is cardiovascular disease risk [c83].

That matters because the line the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) draws runs straight through it. Psychological tests designed to identify a mental disorder or impairment are medical examinations; tests measuring personality traits such as honesty, preferences, and habits are not [c143]. Type D was never built as a workplace traits instrument. It is a health-psychology construct, which puts it on the wrong side of that split.

You do not have to administer the DS14 to get there. In *Kroll v. White Lake Ambulance Authority*, the Sixth Circuit held that requiring an employee to attend counseling could count as a medical examination once the record showed the request was tied to mental-health issues, even though nobody used clinical language [c146]. Describing a candidate as guarded or distressed, and then acting on it, can be read the same way.

There is also the question of what would make such a screen defensible, and Type D cannot answer it. Compliance guidance for personality testing asks employers to show a clear link between the traits a test measures and the tasks of the job [c84]. Type D's validated links run to coronary disease and psychological distress, not job tasks. Employers using hiring tools are separately expected to check whether those tools screen out qualified people with disabilities [c104].

Since 2008, a candidate is covered if an employer acts against them because of an actual or perceived impairment [c149]. They do not have to prove the impairment limits a major life activity, only that it is not both transitory and minor [c149]. So the "regarded as" question is never whether the candidate really has a condition. It is whether you acted because you thought they did [c150].

Denollet has been explicit that Type D is not a psychiatric disorder and describes normal personality traits [c17]. That distinction protects the construct. It does not protect you, because the prong turns on your perception, not on the clinical literature.

Scale makes the exposure real rather than theoretical. Roughly 76% of companies with more than 100 employees use some form of personality testing [c155], and the EEOC settled with Best Buy in 2018 over allegations that its application-stage assessments adversely affected candidates by race and national origin [c156]. Personality itself is not protected, and courts accept legitimate fit concerns [c157]. That protection collapses when the trait screens as a proxy for a perceived impairment.

You Can't Spot Type D Personality in a Job Interview wrote 296 words, was asked for 277 +7% used 6 facts · 5 of them numbers · 2 sources
What this section was asked to do: Close the door on the reader's actual situation, which is that they believe they have already seen it in a candidate. Separate normal interview anxiety from a trait profile that by definition has to persist for years, and show that the only mechanism that assigns the label is a scored questionnaire no interviewer is holding. Ends the diagnosis question and hands the reader to the method that replaces it.
What the writer actually wrote

Nothing you observe in an interview can tell you whether a candidate is Type D, because the two traits that define the label look exactly like ordinary nerves. Negative affectivity is the tendency to experience negative emotions across time and situations, producing dysphoria, anxiety, and irritability [c208]. Social inhibition is the tendency to hold back emotions and behavior in social settings to avoid disapproval, leaving a person feeling tense and insecure around others [c209]. Read that description again and picture a stranger sitting across from three people who control whether they get paid next month.

Guardedness in a high-stakes, evaluative encounter is not a psychological profile. It is the expected reaction to the setting. One trait sometimes folded into Type D descriptions, behavioral inhibition, is specifically the tendency to withdraw from unfamiliar situations [c211]. A first-round interview is, by definition, unfamiliar.

What separates a trait from a bad hour is persistence, not intensity. Type D descriptions say plainly that these people experience the traits more consistently over time than the general population, who feel a variety of these things at times [c213]. Both halves of the profile have been shown to stay relatively stable across a four-year period [c16]. One conversation cannot establish anything about four years.

And the label is not assigned by impression at all. It comes from the DS14, a scored questionnaire compared against population norms built from 2,508 respondents [c215]. You are not holding that instrument, you have no normed comparison group, and you have seen the person once.

Experienced interviewers will tell you manner is signal. Other experienced interviewers will tell them they are scoring nerves. The way out is to stop reading the room and start measuring the work.

Run a Structured Interview and Score the Answer wrote 509 words, was asked for 479 +6% used 12 facts · 6 of them numbers · 3 sources
What this section was asked to do: Give the reader the replacement method in enough detail to run it on their next interview. Define what makes an interview structured, show how far past-behaviour questions outperform opinion questions, and render the scoring rubric that makes a guarded candidate's evidence count while their manner does not. This is the section that pays off everything the previous four ruled out.
What the writer actually wrote

Structure is the mechanism that lets you evaluate a candidate on the job instead of on their manner. A structured interview means every candidate for the same role answers the same questions, in the same order, judged against the same scoring criteria written in advance [c219]. That format exists to strip out the impressionistic read, the confident tone, the warmth, the easy rapport, that would otherwise mark a quiet candidate down for reasons unrelated to the work.

The question type matters as much as the order. Behavioral questions ask what someone actually did in a specific past situation, not what they would hypothetically do. Research puts them at 55% predictive of future on-the-job behavior, against 10% for traditional opinion-based interviewing [c172]. That gap is the whole argument: a question demanding a checkable account of past action moves your attention from how this person came across to what this person did.

The bias risk with a guarded candidate almost never lives in the questions. It lives in how you score the answer.

A rubric replaces "I liked her energy" with documented, comparable indicators at each level. Write the anchors before the interview, never adjust them afterward to fit how someone performed [c231]. Five to seven questions across roughly five core competencies is one recommended range [c231].

| Score | Rating | What the candidate's answer actually shows | |---|---|---| | 5 | Exceptional | Detailed, specific example with measurable outcomes; mastery-level skill; articulates lessons learned [c190] | | 4 | Strong | Clear, relevant example with positive outcomes; solid competency, minor gaps in depth [c191] | | 3 | Adequate | Relevant example but thin on detail or outcomes; baseline competency [c192] | | 2 | Below expectations | Vague or off-topic example; does not explain specific actions or impact [c193] | | 1 | Insufficient | No relevant example, or the example reveals a significant skill gap [c194] |

Read down that column. Every anchor points at something observable in the work: a specific example, a measurable outcome, reasoning the candidate can articulate. None of them points at delivery. A hesitant answer that names the actions taken and what changed scores a 4. A fluent, warm answer with no example in it scores a 2.

Probing is not just allowed, it is the point. Push on a thin answer with follow-ups [c245]. The constraint is that a follow-up never changes the rubric the answer is scored against [c246]. A guarded first response that opens up under a good follow-up surfaces the same evidence a chattier candidate volunteered unprompted, and it counts the same [c247]. That improvised exchange belongs in the debrief as color, and it can break a tie between close scores, but it does not drive the primary score [c247].

This is what keeps the conversation from feeling robotic while the evaluation stays sound. You score the substance surfaced across the whole exchange, not the ease with which it came out [c248].

You Hired Them. Now Manage the Behavior, Not the Label wrote 421 words, was asked for 403 +4% used 10 facts · 10 of them numbers · 2 sources
What this section was asked to do: Finish what competing pages abandon mid-list. Settle what a manager does once a quiet, negative-leaning person is on the team, worked through one named hire from first week to first feedback, managing the observed behaviour and never the label. Name what this profile contributes to a team and the one thing to watch, so the reader leaves with moves rather than a warning.
What the writer actually wrote

The label stops being useful the moment the person starts work, because you can now watch what they actually do. Running an assessment is where most managers stop, and that is the failure: the score names something, then nothing changes on Monday. What follows is the opposite, worked through one hire. Call her Priya, an operations analyst who is thorough, quiet in group settings, and quick to name what might go wrong.

Start with what is driving the behavior rather than the manner itself. Reassuring someone of their worth to the people around them takes time and patience, not a single onboarding chat [c128]. The practical moves look like this:

  • **Ask what the flag is behind the flag.** When Priya says a plan will not hold, ask which step she expects to break. Understanding what drives the negative read is the first move, before any attempt to redirect it [c130].
  • **Give her the work she is built for.** Stable, procedural work paired with a team that needs support is where this profile does best, and pairing task fit with team context beats trying to change the person [c138].
  • **Name her as the systems owner.** If the team struggles to stay organized, someone like Priya can bring the tools and habits that pull everyone else into a system [c119].
  • **Set goals in two-week blocks, not annual ones.** Short-term goal-setting builds clarity about what matters most, and teaches a person to look ahead with more hope and confidence [c134]. Give Priya one visible win by the end of the month.
  • **Say out loud that the support role counts.** People in this profile need to feel wanted and useful, and teams that need helping are where they thrive [c123]. Put it in the feedback conversation, not just in your head.

The contribution is real. Priya steadies a room in group settings [c120], and creates the harmony that goes missing on teams focused only on hitting targets [c127]. When the balance between work and life holds, this profile makes for diligent employees who finish specific tasks well [c126].

Watch one thing. Because she holds in a lot of emotion, that willingness to help can be taken advantage of, and it needs monitoring [c125]. The person who never says no is the person you have to protect. Check her load yourself instead of waiting for her to raise it, and none of that requires you to know her DS14 score.
