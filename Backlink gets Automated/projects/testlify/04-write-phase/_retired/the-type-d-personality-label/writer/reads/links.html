Type D Personality: What the Research Supports for Hiring

# Type D Personality: What the Research Supports for Hiring

A candidate came across as guarded and downbeat, and someone on the panel called it Type D. Now you have to decide whether that read counts for anything.

The type D personality label exists, but it came out of cardiology, not the workplace. Its famous claim, that it predicts death in heart patients, fell apart under bigger studies. And once that read shapes a hiring decision, you take on risk under disability law. What holds up is a structured interview, scored against criteria you wrote in advance.

Quick answer

Type D means two traits together: a steady pull toward negative emotions, and holding those feelings back for fear of disapproval. A 14-item questionnaire scores it. Doctors built it to study heart patients, and its best-known claim, about survival, weakened once bigger studies tested it.

Nothing in the evidence touches how well anyone does a job. Ask every candidate the same questions, score the answers against criteria written in advance, then manage what the person does at work.

Type D Personality Traits: Both, or It Isn't Type D

Type D is one specific thing: negative affectivity and social inhibition in the same person, measured by a 14-item questionnaire called the DS14 1. Either trait alone is not Type D. The D stands for distressed 2.

TraitWhat it means
Negative affectivityA steady tendency to feel worry, gloom, anger and anxiety 1
Social inhibitionHolding those feelings back around others, for fear of rejection 1

The first is what a person feels. The second is what they do with it in front of others. Someone who worries constantly but says so freely is not Type D 1.

Johan Denollet, a professor of medical psychology at Tilburg University, defined Type D in 1995, working from what he saw in heart patients 1. Each trait gets a 7-item subscale, scored 0 to 4. You are Type D only if you score 10 or more on both. Denollet set that cut-off in a sample of cardiac patients.

The Type D Mortality Finding That Failed to Replicate

The finding that made Type D famous was that it predicts death in heart patients. Denollet followed over 300 of them for a decade. About 27% of the Type D patients died, against 6% of the others. Later groups showed up to a fourfold rise in death and repeat heart attacks.

Bigger studies could not reproduce any of it. Pooling 19 earlier studies and more than 11,000 heart patients, researchers found no link to death 3. The smaller studies, they wrote, "may have inadvertently reached exaggerated or false conclusions". Small studies kept reporting larger Type D effects than big ones, a possible sign of publication bias.

So even in cardiology, its home ground, the headline claim is contested. That is a shaky thing to stretch into a use nobody has tested at all.

No Type D Personality Study Ever Measured Job Performance

No study behind the label has ever used job performance, hiring, or workplace conduct as an outcome. Researchers built it to predict cardiac events, death, and transplant rejection 2. It points at who gets sicker, not who does the work.

Some research does touch work, which is where the confusion starts. A 2010 review found Type D linked to more absence leave and more exhaustion. That is self-reported tiredness and days missed, not a measure of how well anyone works. A review of eight studies also tied Type D to suicidal thoughts across countries. Mental health is exactly what a hiring decision may not screen on.

Nobody has tested the DS14 against a job-performance criterion. It is a self-report questionnaire about the two traits a candidate most wants to hide from an employer 2. Good selection tests exist, and even those only add insight rather than make the call. Type D was never validated for employment decisions.

Calling a Candidate Type D Can Cross the ADA Line

Once a Type D read shapes a hiring decision, the problem turns legal. The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) bars you from asking about health conditions before a conditional offer 4.

The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) draws a line here. Tests built to identify a mental disorder are medical exams. Tests measuring honesty, preferences, and habits are not. Type D sits on the wrong side: its whole evidence base is heart disease risk.

You never even have to hand out the DS14. In Kroll v. White Lake Ambulance Authority, the Sixth Circuit called an order into counseling a possible medical exam. What made it one was a record tying the order to mental health. Call a candidate guarded, then act on it, and you can land in the same place.

Since 2008, a candidate is covered if you act against them over an impairment you only think they have. Denollet says plainly that Type D is not a psychiatric disorder 1. That protects the construct, not you, because the test turns on your perception.

You Can't Spot Type D Personality in an Interview. Run a Structured Interview Instead

Nothing you see in an interview tells you whether a candidate is Type D, because both traits look exactly like ordinary nerves.

What separates a trait from a bad hour is persistence. People with Type D feel these things consistently over time, where others feel them now and then. Both halves stay fairly stable across four years. One conversation tells you nothing about four years.

So stop reading the room and measure the work. A structured interview means every candidate answers the same questions, scored against criteria you wrote in advance. Ask behavioral questions: what did this person actually do in a specific past situation. Those questions are 55% predictive of on-the-job behavior, against 10% for opinion-based interviewing.

With a guarded candidate, the bias sits in how you score, not in the questions. Write the anchors before the interview, and never adjust them to fit how someone came across. Five to seven questions across about five competencies is one recommended range.

ScoreRatingWhat the answer shows
5ExceptionalDetailed example, measurable outcomes, lessons learned
4StrongClear, relevant example with positive outcomes; minor gaps
3AdequateRelevant example, thin on detail or outcomes
2Below expectationsVague or off-topic; no specific actions or impact
1InsufficientNo relevant example, or one that shows a skill gap

None of those anchors points at delivery. A hesitant answer that names the actions taken and what changed scores a 4. A fluent, warm answer with no example scores a 2.

Push on a thin answer with follow-ups, as long as the follow-up never changes the rubric. A guarded candidate who opens up hands you the same evidence a chattier one volunteered, and it counts the same.

You Hired Them. Now Manage the Behavior, Not the Label

The label stops being useful the moment someone starts work, because now you can watch what they do. Call her Priya, an operations analyst who is thorough, quiet in meetings, and quick to name what might go wrong. Every move below answers something she has done.

  • Ask what is behind the flag. When Priya says a plan will not hold, ask which step she expects to break.
  • Match the work to what she has shown you. Fitting the task to the person beats trying to change them. Go on demonstrated work, never on a profile you read into her manner.
  • Name her as the systems owner. If the team struggles to stay organized, someone like Priya brings the habits that pull everyone into a system.
  • Set goals in two-week blocks. Short-term goals build clarity about what matters most, and teach a person to look ahead with confidence.
  • Watch her load. A willingness to help gets taken advantage of, so it needs monitoring. Check on her yourself instead of waiting for her to ask.

Reassuring someone of their worth to the team takes patience, not one onboarding chat. Priya steadies a room and builds the harmony that goes missing on teams fixed only on targets.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best job for a type D personality?

There is no research-backed match, because no Type D study has measured job performance. Popular guidance points to steady, procedural work on a supportive team, but nobody tested it.

What is the difference between type D personality and introversion?

Introversion is about where someone draws energy, and a quiet person can be perfectly content. Type D needs distress too: negative emotion held back for fear of disapproval 1. A calm, reserved candidate is not Type D 1.

What are type A, B, C, D personalities?

Four labels from separate research traditions, not one system. Type A is driven and impatient, Type B relaxed, Type C compliant, Type D negative and socially inhibited. None was built to predict job performance.

Is type D personality a mental illness?

No. Johan Denollet, who defined it, says plainly that Type D is not a psychiatric disorder and describes normal personality traits 1. Researchers studied it as a risk marker in heart patients.

Can type D personality change over time?

Both halves of the profile stay fairly stable across a four-year period. Stable is not the same as fixed. Nobody meant the label as a permanent verdict on a person.
