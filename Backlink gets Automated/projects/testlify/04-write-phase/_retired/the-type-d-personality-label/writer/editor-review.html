Type D Personality Traits: Both, or It Isn't Type D declared
• fixed reference: The Denollet paragraph said the origin "is where the next section starts", but the DS14 scoring paragraph came after it; cut the false pointer.
Type D is one specific thing: negative affectivity and social inhibition present together in the same person, measured by a 14-item questionnaire called the DS14 [c1][c33]. Either trait on its own is not Type D. The D stands for distressed [c2]. Negative affectivity is a steady tendency to feel negative emotions across time and situations: worry, irritability, gloom, anger, and anxiety [c3]. Social inhibition is holding those feelings and opinions back in social situations, out of fear of being rejected or disapproved of [c4]. The first is what a person feels. The second is what they do with it in front of other people. The construct rests on the joint occurrence of the two, not on either one alone [c5]. Someone who worries constantly but says so freely is not Type D. Neither is someone reserved and calm. Johan Denollet, a professor of medical psychology at Tilburg University in the Netherlands, first formulated Type D in 1995 [c6]. He built it on clinical observations in cardiac patients, plus existing personality theory [c7]. That origin matters for everything a hiring manager might want to do with the label, and it is where the next section starts. label. The DS14 splits into two 7-item subscales, one per trait, each item scored 0 to 4 [c11][c12]. A person is classified as Type D only when they score 10 or more on both subscales [c13]. Denollet set that cut-off as a median split in a sample of cardiac patients [c13]. It is a yes-or-no category, which the literature notes suits clinical settings where a treatment decision has to go one way or the other [c14].

All of that exposure rests on a read no interviewer can actually make. Nothing you observe in an interview can tell you whether a candidate is Type D, because the two traits that define the label look exactly like ordinary nerves. Negative affectivity is the tendency to experience negative emotions across time and situations, producing dysphoria, anxiety, and irritability [c208]. Social inhibition is the tendency to hold back emotions and behavior in social settings to avoid disapproval, leaving a person feeling tense and insecure around others [c209]. Read that description again and picture Picture a stranger sitting across from three people who control whether they get paid next month. Guardedness in a high-stakes, evaluative encounter is not a psychological profile. It is the expected reaction to the setting. One trait sometimes folded into Type D descriptions, behavioral inhibition, is specifically the tendency to withdraw from unfamiliar situations [c211]. A first-round interview is, by definition, unfamiliar. What separates a trait from a bad hour is persistence, not intensity. Type D descriptions say plainly that these people experience the traits more consistently over time than the general population, who feel a variety of these things them at times [c213]. Both halves of the profile have been shown to stay relatively stable across a four-year period [c16]. One conversation cannot establish anything about four years. And the label is not assigned by impression at all. It comes from the DS14, a scored questionnaire compared against population norms built from 2,508 respondents [c215]. You are not holding that instrument, you have no normed comparison group, and you have seen the person once. Experienced interviewers will tell you manner is signal. Other experienced interviewers will tell them they are scoring nerves. The way out is to stop reading the room and start measuring the work.

Structure is the mechanism that lets you evaluate a candidate on the job instead of on their manner. A structured interview means every candidate for the same role answers the same questions, in the same order, judged against the same scoring criteria written in advance [c219]. That format exists to strip strips out the impressionistic read, read: the confident tone, the warmth, the easy rapport, rapport that would otherwise mark a quiet candidate down for reasons unrelated to the work. The question type matters as much as the order. Behavioral questions ask what someone actually did in a specific past situation, not what they would hypothetically do. Research puts them at 55% predictive of future on-the-job behavior, against 10% for traditional opinion-based interviewing [c172]. That gap is the whole argument: a question demanding a checkable account of past action moves your attention from how this person came across to what this person did. The bias risk with a guarded candidate almost never lives in the questions. It lives in how you score the answer. ### Score the Answer, Not the Room A rubric replaces "I liked her energy" with documented, comparable indicators at each level. Write the anchors before the interview, never adjust them afterward to fit how someone performed [c231]. Five to seven questions across roughly five core competencies is one recommended range [c231]. | Score | Rating | What the candidate's answer actually shows | |---|---|---| | 5 | Exceptional | Detailed, specific example with measurable outcomes; mastery-level skill; articulates lessons learned [c190] | | 4 | Strong | Clear, relevant example with positive outcomes; solid competency, minor gaps in depth [c191] | | 3 | Adequate | Relevant example but thin on detail or outcomes; baseline competency [c192] | | 2 | Below expectations | Vague or off-topic example; does not explain specific actions or impact [c193] | | 1 | Insufficient | No relevant example, or the example reveals a significant skill gap [c194] | Read down that column. Every anchor points at something observable in the work: a specific example, a measurable outcome, reasoning the candidate can articulate. None of them points at delivery. A hesitant answer that names the actions taken and what changed scores a 4. A fluent, warm answer with no example in it scores a 2. Probing is not just allowed, it is the point. Push on a thin answer with follow-ups [c245]. The constraint is that a follow-up never changes the rubric the answer is scored against [c246]. A guarded first response that opens up under a good follow-up surfaces the same evidence a chattier candidate volunteered unprompted, and it counts the same [c247]. That improvised exchange belongs in the debrief as color, and it can break a tie between close scores, but it does not drive the primary score [c247]. This is what keeps the conversation from feeling robotic while the evaluation stays sound. You score the substance surfaced across the whole exchange, not the ease with which it came out [c248].
