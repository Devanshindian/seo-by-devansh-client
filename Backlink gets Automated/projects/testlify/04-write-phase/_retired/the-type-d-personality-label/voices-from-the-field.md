# Voices from the field, The Type D 'Personality' Label: What the Research Actually Supports for Hiring Decisions

> This is what practitioners say in public forums about personality labels, quiet candidates, and interview judgment. None of it is a fact, none of it gets a source tag, and no person here is quoted or named.

**Coverage.** 7 discussion(s) read and 70 comments, from 1 on linkedin, 6 on reddit. 139 results were looked at across 1 search round(s).

## Assessment specialists split hard on which personality tests belong in hiring

**What they say.** People who do this for a living draw a sharp line between instruments built on validated trait research and the popular type labels. One camp uses tools derived from established models for selection and says they hold up. The other camp calls most of the field "snake oil" and warns that a test used "just for development" never stays there, because the whole point of running it is to change how people get treated at work. Both camps agree on the same practical move: when a leader wants a test, do not argue psychometrics with them, argue practical impact and offer a better alternative.

**Strength.** Strong. The same split ran across three separate practitioner discussions, with the "there are good ones and a lot of junk" framing appearing independently in each.

**Serves.** Sections 3 and 4, and the angle.

**What this changes.** Do not write as if all personality assessment is suspect. The article's case is narrower and stronger: some instruments were built and validated for hiring, and Type D was not one of them. Say that explicitly so the reader can tell the categories apart.

## A self-report score measures self-image, not behavior

**What they say.** The limits of asking someone to rate their own traits come up repeatedly, and the sharpest version of it is not the obvious one. It is not just that people shade their answers. It is that trying to correct for it runs through the same filter, so introspection recurses instead of escaping. Even people defending self-report instruments concede the score is a different thing from how others actually experience the person, and point to other-rated or behavior-based measures as the separate check.

**Strength.** Moderate. Two practitioner discussions, but with genuine argument on both sides rather than a chorus, which is why it is worth carrying.

**Serves.** Section 3.

**What this changes.** The DS14 self-report problem deserves more than one line. Make the point that the number describes how a person sees themselves on exactly the traits an interview pressures them to manage, and that no one has checked it against how they actually behave at work.

## Quiet in the room is not the same as bad at the job

**What they say.** Two separate strands say this. Managers describe their best leader as someone who barely spoke in meetings and was unfazed by everything, and treat the calm-and-quiet profile as an asset rather than a gap. Separately, when recruiters complain that candidates present badly, the pushback inside their own community is that the behaviors being scored are inexperience, nerves, or cultural difference, and that letting them affect the outcome means the evaluation itself is the problem. Worth noting these are two different rooms with different incentives, and the second one is people arguing with their own colleagues.

**Strength.** Moderate. Appeared in three discussions, and it is a live disagreement rather than a consensus, which is the useful part.

**Serves.** Sections 5 and 7.

**What this changes.** Section 5 should name the disagreement plainly: experienced interviewers do read manner as signal, and other experienced interviewers tell them they are misreading it. That is a better hook than asserting the reader is simply wrong. Section 7 can lean on the quiet-manager picture when naming what this profile contributes.

## Interview nerves show up as physical panic, not a trait

**What they say.** Candidates describe reactions to being observed that read as severe in the moment and disappear outside it. One describes closing the laptop mid-recording and crying; another says being on camera makes them nauseous while the same person is confident in person and fine on calls where they hold authority. The recurring detail is that the same person behaves completely differently depending on whether they feel judged.

**Strength.** Moderate. Appeared across two discussions and multiple people, and the state-versus-situation contrast is what makes it usable.

**Serves.** Section 5.

**What this changes.** Section 5 can separate state from trait with a real texture instead of an abstract definition. The person who reads as withdrawn in a recorded interview is often demonstrably not withdrawn thirty minutes later, and a trait profile that has to persist for years cannot be built from that window.

## Running the test is where managers stop, and that is the failure

**What they say.** The complaint that recurs in both the leadership and the practitioner threads is not that personality frameworks are useless. It is that people treat administering the assessment as the finished work. The stated value in every defense of these tools is downstream of the label: knowing what conditions help a person think clearly, what they need from you, and how a message will land. The label itself starts a conversation and does nothing else.

**Strength.** Strong. Appeared in three discussions and across both a public leadership audience and a skeptical practitioner audience, which rarely agree on anything here.

**Serves.** Section 7 and the angle.

**What this changes.** Section 7 is where the article wins, and this is the argument for it. Frame the managing section as the work the label lets people skip, and keep every move tied to an observed behavior with a concrete response.

## What did not show up

- **Nobody discusses Type D at all.** Across every discussion read, not one practitioner or manager used the term, argued about it, or reported encountering it. The label has essentially no presence in working hiring conversation, which is itself worth knowing: the reader who arrived with it likely got it from a content page, not from peers.
- **No stories of a Type D or "negative profile" hire going wrong.** Nothing surfaced where someone screened for a distress or negativity profile and reported the outcome either way. There is no field evidence to weigh against the research.
- **No legal experience with personality screening.** Searches aimed at lawsuit and ADA risk returned discussions about test validity instead. Nobody described a complaint, a challenge, or advice they received from counsel. The article's ADA section gets nothing from practitioners and must rest entirely on its own research.
- **No one walked through a scoring rubric they actually use.** Section 6 needs the rubric rendered in detail, and nothing here supplies a working example, a scale, or anchors from real practice. That has to come from the article's own material.
