The spine — the one argument this article makes

Every section below exists to move this sentence forward. If a section does not serve it, it should not be here.

Type D is a real research construct, but it was built to predict heart-health outcomes in cardiac patients, its headline mortality finding failed to replicate, and nothing in its evidence base touches job performance. So a hiring manager who thinks they have spotted it in a candidate is working from a label that cannot be assigned in an interview, cannot be defended if challenged, and carries ADA exposure the moment it influences a decision. By the end the reader can drop the label, run a structured, rubric-scored interview that measures job behaviour instead of manner, and manage a guarded or negative-leaning person well once they are on the team.

What it chose to leave out

The architect is shown the topics every ranking page covers. It is allowed to drop any that do not serve this article. Watch for the opposite of a problem here: if it never drops anything, it has stopped judging.

Left out the day-to-day 'Type D trait portrait' (sad, irritable, pessimistic, organised, persistent) that ranking pages lead with: after this article argues the label cannot be assigned by observation, a spotting guide would contradict it, and those claims come from the same popular framing the piece is correcting. Core traits are still covered, as negative affectivity plus social inhibition in section 1. Left out clinical treatment of Type D (counselling, therapy, mindfulness), which is written for the patient or clinician, not for a hiring manager, and which collides with the ADA point about referring employees to counselling. Google's DiSC 'Dominant' disambiguation is explicitly outside this article's subject and is ignored. Health concerns are covered, but as the research story in section 2 rather than as health advice.

The world — what this article is and is not about

Written in the research phase, before any searching started, and never changed since. It is one of the stated reasons the architect benches material below, so read it before judging whether a bench was right.

IS about
This is about the Type D ('distressed') personality construct from health psychology — the negative-affectivity-plus-social-inhibition trait profile defined by researcher Johan Denollet, originally studied for its link to cardiovascular disease outcomes. The article examines what that actual research base does and doesn't support when the label gets borrowed into hiring: whether it's a valid or ethical screening signal, what it was actually shown to predict (heart-health outcomes, not job performance), and how a manager should handle a team member who fits the profile once hired.
is NOT about
This is NOT about the DISC assessment's 'D' (Dominance) type — a popular HR/hiring-assessment framework that shares only the letter 'D' with Type D personality and is a completely different model with no relation to Denollet's research. It is NOT about Type A/Type B personality theory (the coronary-prone-behavior vs. relaxed-behavior framework from the 1950s), which is a separate, older personality-and-heart-health theory easily confused with Type D because both frameworks talk about personality and cardiac risk. It is NOT about Myers-Briggs (MBTI) types, the Enneagram, or any other personality-typing system used in hiring or team-building content. It is NOT about blood-type personality theory (the Type A/B/O/AB blood-group folk-personality claims popular in Japan and Korea). And it is NOT about medical 'Type' classifications unrelated to this construct, such as Type 1/Type 2 diabetes.
