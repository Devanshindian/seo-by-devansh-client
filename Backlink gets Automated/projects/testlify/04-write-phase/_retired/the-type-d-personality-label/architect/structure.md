# STRUCTURE — The Type D 'Personality' Label: What the Research Actually Supports for Hiring Decisions

**Format:** answer-bait-definitional · **Sections:** 7 · **Authored H3s:** 3 · **Boxes used:** 27


**The article's spine:** Type D is a real research construct, but it was built to predict heart-health outcomes in cardiac patients, its headline mortality finding failed to replicate, and nothing in its evidence base touches job performance. So a hiring manager who thinks they have spotted it in a candidate is working from a label that cannot be assigned in an interview, cannot be defended if challenged, and carries ADA exposure the moment it influences a decision. By the end the reader can drop the label, run a structured, rubric-scored interview that measures job behaviour instead of manner, and manage a guarded or negative-leaning person well once they are on the team.

**What it left out, and why:** Left out the day-to-day 'Type D trait portrait' (sad, irritable, pessimistic, organised, persistent) that ranking pages lead with: after this article argues the label cannot be assigned by observation, a spotting guide would contradict it, and those claims come from the same popular framing the piece is correcting. Core traits are still covered, as negative affectivity plus social inhibition in section 1. Left out clinical treatment of Type D (counselling, therapy, mindfulness), which is written for the patient or clinician, not for a hiring manager, and which collides with the ADA point about referring employees to counselling. Google's DiSC 'Dominant' disambiguation is explicitly outside this article's subject and is ignored. Health concerns are covered, but as the research story in section 2 rather than as health advice.

## Material NOT used
- [#6] Is the DS14 a Valid and Reliable Tool?  (3 cards) — DS14 psychometric detail (Cronbach's alpha, German norms, observer-rating correlations) is written for researchers validating an instrument, not for a manager deciding on one candidate; section 1 already establishes that the tool exists and has a cut-off.
- [#11] The negative traits: sadness, irritability, and social avoidance  (5 cards) — A day-to-day 'what a Type D looks like' portrait is a spotting guide, and the article's central argument is that the label cannot be assigned by observation; placing it would contradict sections 4 and 5.
- [#12] The positive traits: organized, caring, and persistent  (3 cards) — Same problem as the negative-trait portrait, and the source is the popular framing this article is correcting rather than the research base it rests on.
- [#20] Reassure Them and Invest in Their Confidence  (2 cards) — Recommending that a manager invest in counselling for a team member is the exact practice the Kroll case in the legal section flags as a possible medical examination; running both would leave a contradiction the reader cannot act on.
- [#22] Encourage Mindfulness, Prayer, or Meditation  (2 cards) — Mindfulness, prayer and meditation are advice for the distressed person or their clinician, not for the manager, and a manager prescribing them for a perceived health-linked trait walks straight back into the ADA exposure the article just warned about.
- [#29] Calibration and independent scoring, before the debrief  (5 cards) — Panel calibration sessions and debrief discipline are interview-programme design for an HR-native owner; this reader hires a few times a year with a recruiting partner, and the section already carries the rubric that does the same protective work.
- [#34] Does health-linked personality labeling add privacy-law exposure on top of ADA risk?  (3 cards) — Privacy law is a fourth legal strand on three cards; the legal section can carry the ADA medical-exam and regarded-as risks properly or all four thinly, and ADA is the load-bearing exposure for this reader.
