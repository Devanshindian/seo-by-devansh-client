# Voices from the field, Strategic Interview Questions Paired With Strong and Weak Sample Answers

> What practitioners say about scoring interviews, cut to what these fifteen sections can use. None of this is a fact to state, none of it carries a source tag, and nothing here quotes or names a person.

**Coverage.** 8 discussion(s) read and 71 comments, from 4 on linkedin, 4 on reddit. 200 results were looked at across 2 search round(s).

## Lock every score before anyone in the room talks

**What they say.** The failure people describe again and again is not a missing rubric. It is that the rubric gets filled in after the group has already started talking, so the most senior voice sets the standard and everyone quietly moves toward it. One person described watching an interviewer arrive ready to reject a candidate, hear a VP say they liked the person, and switch to "well, I did think they showed potential." Another called a scorecard written during the debrief a consensus wearing an independent label. One person who works in assessment said writing ratings down before the debrief was the only change they have ever made that moved hiring quality at all.

**Strength.** Strong. It came up in three separate discussions, including one where six different people in the same thread landed on it independently, and a fourth where a manager's gut feeling overrode a team's ranking and the hire went badly.

**Serves.** Section 14.

**What this changes.** Score-alone-first should be the hardest of the three habits, not one of three equals. Name the exact contamination: the senior person speaks, everyone re-anchors, and the number recorded afterwards is not the number anyone actually held.

## Nerves get scored when nobody wrote down what counts

**What they say.** Interviewers keep describing candidates who presented badly and performed well. A nervous, near-silent candidate became one of the most vocal people on the team. Someone who looked technically weak turned into the person everyone went to first. And in a recruiting forum, several people who describe themselves as strong performers admitted they are bad at being interviewed, one saying that when asked "tell me about a time" they simply cannot retrieve one and flounder. The common reading is that an interview measures how someone interviews, and without a written standard that gap gets filled by delivery.

**Strength.** Strong. Three separate discussions. Worth noting the two most emotional versions are public LinkedIn posts, which is a place people go to look generous, but the recruiting forum version is people confessing about themselves, which is harder to fake.

**Serves.** Section 5 and section 14.

**What this changes.** Section 5's "scope, not polish" line should be widened. The article should say once, plainly, that a shaky delivery of a large-scope story outscores a smooth delivery of a small one, and that the scoring line names the content only. It also gives section 14 a fourth risk worth a sentence: the scale exists to stop fluency doing the scoring.

## Practitioners split on script versus conversation

**What they say.** One camp holds that structured interviewing is the best-evidenced method there is and that drifting off the questions is the whole problem. The other camp is not arguing against evidence. They are saying that in practice neither side enjoys it, that sticking to the questions is hard, that candidates want a conversation, and that a rigid script makes an anxious person perform worse than they are. Several interviewers described deliberately opening with "I would rather have a conversation than an interview" because it takes the nerves off the table and gets better material out of the person. One recruiter in a tight talent market said they avoid formal tools because they do not want to manufacture more reasons to reject.

**Strength.** Strong, and this is the most useful thing in the file because it is a real split. It ran across four discussions, with the pro-structure side concentrated on LinkedIn and the friction side on the forums.

**Serves.** The angle, and section 14.

**What this changes.** The article should resolve this rather than ignore it, because its reader will feel the pull. The resolution the field points at: the questions and the scale are fixed, the delivery of them is not. A warm opening and a fixed question list are not in conflict. Say that once, early, or the non-HR-native reader will read twelve scripted questions as an instruction to sound like a robot and quietly go back to freestyling.

## Nobody probes, so the question alone decides nothing

**What they say.** The complaint that shows up in every professional forum is not that people ask the wrong questions. It is that they ask one and stop. People describe interviewers with no follow-up and no probing, five interviewers each measuring something different, and questions improvised off whatever is on the CV. Several noted that behavioral interviewing is trained rarely and done badly when it is done, and that new people get put in interviewing seats with almost no preparation. One recruiter reported asking a candidate what metrics they track and getting "I don't do that, it's a good idea though."

**Strength.** Strong. Three separate discussions, with several people in each.

**Serves.** The angle, and every numbered question section.

**What this changes.** A question plus a strong answer plus a weak answer still leaves the reader stuck when the real answer lands in the middle, which is where most land. Each question section should carry the one follow-up that resolves a middling answer, not just the two poles. That is the gap the field is describing, and it is the gap the strong/weak pair does not close on its own.

## Generic question lists get dropped for not matching the job

**What they say.** People who build hiring processes say the reason structured interviewing gets abandoned is buy-in, not disagreement. Hiring managers look at an off-the-shelf competency question set, cannot see how it connects to the role they are filling, and stop using it. The complaint is that generic models have no depth on the thing the manager actually cares about, which is whether this person can do the work. A related point from recruiting is that you need real knowledge of the field to interview well in it.

**Strength.** Moderate. It appeared in two discussions, with three people making the point in the stronger of the two.

**Serves.** Section 15.

**What this changes.** Section 15 should not read as an optional extra for advanced readers. It is the reason the other fourteen sections survive contact with a real role. Move its promise earlier, and let the twelve general questions be explicitly framed as the half of the interview that transfers, with the role-specific half derived from the job.

## What did not show up

- **Nothing at all on the 1-5 scale itself.** Not one person argued about how many levels a scale should have, what an anchor should say, or which validity figure is right. No practitioner quoted a correlation number for structured interviews in either direction. Section 13 has no field support and no field objection.
- **Nobody discussed benchmark sample answers.** The article's core device, attaching a real strong and weak answer to each question, was never mentioned by anyone as a practice they use or reject. The closest was one person saying that writing down what a strong answer sounds like before the role opens helped them more than any tool, which is one person, not a pattern.
- **None of the twelve questions came up specifically.** No discussion of what to look for in a departure answer, no competitor-swap test, no talk of the 90-day quit question, no version of the ship-it-anyway scenario. The question-level judgment calls in sections 1 through 12 are unsupported by anything in this material.
- **No discussion of interview length or time budget.** Nobody talked about fitting a question set into 45 minutes, or what gets cut when the interview runs short.
