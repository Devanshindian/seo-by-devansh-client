The facts are settled. This page is the plan for the article itself: what it argues, which sections carry it, and how long each one gets.

The spine — the one argument this article makes

Every section below exists to move this sentence forward. If a section does not serve it, it should not be here.

An interview question is only as useful as the benchmark you hold the answer against, and 'look for strategic thinking' is a label, not a benchmark. This article gives a functional lead who conducts interviews but does not do it for a living twelve questions worth asking, and attaches to each one a real answer that scores well, a real answer that scores badly, and one line naming the thing to score. Underneath them sits a single 1-5 anchored scale and the small set of habits (score in the room, score alone, write the evidence down) that keep the scale honest. At the end the reader can run a 45-minute interview from these twelve questions and defend every number they wrote.

What it chose to leave out

The architect is shown the topics every ranking page covers. It is allowed to drop any that do not serve this article. Watch for the opposite of a problem here: if it never drops anything, it has stopped judging.

Left out the 'skills, capabilities and qualifications' category as a set of its own items — none of the twelve questions is role-technical, and the paired strong/weak benchmark only works on a question every interviewer actually asks, so the IT, ML, sales and customer-success question banks are benched and role-technical questions get one short adaptation section after the items instead. Also left out Google's split of 'core competencies' and 'team collaboration' into separate question categories: those are competencies the twelve questions already test, not questions of their own. Behavioral, situational and culture/values questions are all covered, and covered as items rather than as explained categories.

The world — what this article is and is not about

Written in the research phase, before any searching started, and never changed since. It is one of the stated reasons the architect benches material below, so read it before judging whether a bench was right.

IS about
Interview questions a hiring manager or recruiter asks candidates for a given role, each paired with a real strong-answer example and a real weak-answer example plus a short line on how to score the response — a rubric interviewers use DURING the interview to judge what a candidate actually said, replacing vague 'look for good communication skills' criteria with something to benchmark against.
is NOT about
Not job-seeker interview prep (articles coaching a candidate on how to answer questions to get hired) — this is written for the interviewer's side of the table, not the candidate's. Not 'strategic interview' in the consulting/case-interview sense (McKinsey/BCG-style case questions testing business strategy or market-sizing skills) — 'strategic' here means questions that probe a candidate's ability to think strategically about their job function, not a case-study interview format. Not corporate strategy or strategic planning content. Not 'interview' in the qualitative-research-methodology sense (structured/unstructured interviews as a data-collection technique) or the journalism sense (an interview as a published Q&A piece). Not clinical or psychological intake interviews. Not general behavioral-question listicles that give the question alone with no paired answer or scoring guidance — the answer-pair-plus-score is the defining feature, not just 'good interview questions.' Not interview scheduling, coordination, or ATS workflow tools.
