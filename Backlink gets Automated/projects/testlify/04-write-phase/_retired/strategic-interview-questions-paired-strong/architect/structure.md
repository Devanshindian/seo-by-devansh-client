# STRUCTURE — Strategic Interview Questions Paired With Strong and Weak Sample Answers

**Format:** listicle · **Sections:** 15 · **Authored H3s:** 5 · **Boxes used:** 52

**Items:** "Why are you leaving your current job?" · "Why do you want to work here?" · "Imagine your top priority changes mid-quarter. The new priority conflicts with commitments you've already made. Walk me through how you handle it." · "Tell me about yourself" / "Walk me through your background" · "Tell me about a project or task that was ambiguous or underspecified. How did you decide what to work on?" · "Tell me about a time a change at work required you to adjust your approach. How did you adapt?" · "Describe a situation where you disagreed with a manager or stakeholder. How did you handle it?" · "Tell me about a piece of feedback you received. What did you do with it?" · "Tell me about a situation where you had to manage conflicting expectations from multiple stakeholders. What did you do?" · "Tell me about a decision you made that turned out to be wrong. What did you do next?" · "Your team is asked to ship something you don't think is ready. How do you respond?" · "What would make you quit a job within the first three months?"

**The article's spine:** An interview question is only as useful as the benchmark you hold the answer against, and 'look for strategic thinking' is a label, not a benchmark. This article gives a functional lead who conducts interviews but does not do it for a living twelve questions worth asking, and attaches to each one a real answer that scores well, a real answer that scores badly, and one line naming the thing to score. Underneath them sits a single 1-5 anchored scale and the small set of habits (score in the room, score alone, write the evidence down) that keep the scale honest. At the end the reader can run a 45-minute interview from these twelve questions and defend every number they wrote.

**What it left out, and why:** Left out the 'skills, capabilities and qualifications' category as a set of its own items — none of the twelve questions is role-technical, and the paired strong/weak benchmark only works on a question every interviewer actually asks, so the IT, ML, sales and customer-success question banks are benched and role-technical questions get one short adaptation section after the items instead. Also left out Google's split of 'core competencies' and 'team collaboration' into separate question categories: those are competencies the twelve questions already test, not questions of their own. Behavioral, situational and culture/values questions are all covered, and covered as items rather than as explained categories.

## 2. "Why Are You Leaving?": Specific Gap or Blame  `[item]`
*Job: Settle the question interviewers most often let slide into small talk. Show that the answer predicts engagement in the next role, and separate the two axes that decide the score: whether the candidate names a specific gap and owns their part in it, or externalises the blame. Give the reader the acceptable reasons so they stop treating every departure story as a warning sign.*
*Word target: ~351*

## 3. "Why Do You Want to Work Here?": Swap in a Rival's Name  `[item]`
*Job: Kill the idea that this question measures enthusiasm. Show it measures research depth, and give the reader the single fastest test in the article: swap a competitor's name into the answer and see whether it still works. Following the departure question, this closes the pair that together tell the reader whether the candidate chose this job or just needed one.*

## 5. Ambiguity Answers: Score Scope, Not Polish  `[item]`
*Job: Settle what separates a strong ambiguity answer from a weak one, and correct the assumption most interviewers arrive with: it is scope, not polish. Give the reader the concrete axis to score against — how many people and teams the candidate's story actually touched — so they can tell a junior-shaped answer from a senior one without guessing.*

## 6. "How Did You Adapt?": Score Actions, Not Acceptance  `[item]`
*Job: Rule out the most common misread on adaptability questions: candidates who describe accepting a change rather than acting on it. Show that everything scorable sits in what the candidate did after the change, name the first-second-third action the reader should demand, and flag the pattern where someone adapted only once another person had solved the problem.*
*Word target: ~322*
- (opening, no sub-heading)  (23 cards)
